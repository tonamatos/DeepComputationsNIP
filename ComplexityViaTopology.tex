\begin{abstract}
  We bring the topology of spaces of functions of the first Baire class, culminating in Todorčević's classification of Rosenthal compacta, together with Shelah's model-theoretic notion of independence, to bear on the complexity of deep computations, that is, of limits of computations.
  Such limits arise whenever an iterative procedure is carried to its conclusion, from the classical methods of numerical analysis, such as Newton's method and the power method, to the recurrent, equilibrium and continuous-depth architectures of deep learning; yet a deep computation is, in general, a nonconstructive object, an ultrafilter limit of possibly uncountably many computations that need not be the limit of any sequence of them.
  We show that, when states are observed through countably many real-valued features, a set of computations lacks Shelah's \emph{independence property}, a dividing line of model theory akin to the Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension of statistical learning, exactly when, on every region of the state space on which the features are bounded and which the computations preserve, its deep computations, extended to the limit states of the region, are of the first Baire class;
  on each such region, every deep computation is then the limit of a sequence of computations.
  Todorčević's classification then yields a strict hierarchy of complexity classes of deep computations.
  Its levels are attained, from the top down, by a rotation of the circle, the power method, Bresenham's line algorithm in the plane and in space, and the Euclidean algorithm, while Newton's method for the square roots of unity on the Riemann sphere lies beyond the hierarchy.
  For algorithms that iterate continuous steps on a compact metrizable space of states, the hierarchy coincides with the Glasner--Megrelishvili hierarchy of tame dynamical systems.
  Finally, for any number of features, the absence of the independence property makes every deep computation, extended to the limit states of each such region, universally measurable there;
  consequently, whatever the Radon probability measure that governs the inputs, each feature of its output is, outside a set of arbitrarily small probability, a uniform limit of polynomials in the features of the input.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

This paper brings Shelah's theory of independence and Todorčević's classification of Rosenthal compacta to bear on the complexity of deep computations, the limits of computations.
With these two theories we determine when a deep computation is the limit of a sequence of computations, and we stratify deep computations into a strict hierarchy of complexity classes.
The interest of the undertaking lies in the circumstance that a deep computation need not inherit the properties of the computations that converge to it.
For instance, a pointwise limit of continuous maps need not be continuous.
Likewise, a limit taken along an ultrafilter over uncountably many computations need not be the limit of any sequence of them.
When no sequence of the computations converges to such a limit, the limit lies beyond the reach of sequential approximation.
Deep computations arise whenever an iterative procedure is carried to its conclusion.
The classical methods of numerical analysis furnish instances.
Among them are Newton's method for the roots of a polynomial and the power method for the eigenvectors of a matrix.
We analyze the deep computations of these two methods in Sections~\ref{sec:examples} and~\ref{sec:iterative}.
In deep learning, several architectures are defined as limits of computations.
A recurrent network applies one layer over and over.
An infinitely deep network of this kind, with the same weights in every layer, would reach a fixed point.
The deep equilibrium models of Bai, Kolter and Koltun~\cite{Bai:2019} compute this fixed point directly, by root finding.
A residual network may be regarded as a discretization of a differential equation.
The neural ordinary differential equations of Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt and Duvenaud~\cite{Chen:2018} replace the discrete sequence of layers of a residual network by a differential equation for the hidden state.
This differential equation is obtained in the limit in which the time step between consecutive layers tends to zero.
Neural ordinary differential equations belong to a broader effort to bring differential equations into deep learning.
This effort also includes the physics-informed neural networks of Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis~\cite{Raissi-Perdikaris-Karniadakis:2019}.
In a physics-informed network, the differential equations of a physical system are built into the training of the network.
In all these architectures, as in the classical methods, the object of interest is a limit of computations rather than a single computation.

The framework of this paper is that of compositional computation structures.
Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton introduced these structures in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability} to accommodate all these limits in a single setting.
The framework concerns classical models of computation, in which real-valued quantities are known and manipulated to a finite degree of precision.
In this framework, computations act on states, and each state is observed through the values of real-valued \emph{features}.
In the first paper of the series, the same authors introduced two closely related notions of limit of computations.
A \emph{deep computation} is a pointwise limit of computations evaluated on the states themselves.
The term follows the usage in machine learning~\cite{Bai:2019}.
An \emph{ultracomputation} is a pointwise limit of the extensions of computations to the ideal limit states, the types described below.
The name reflects the fact that an ultracomputation arises as an ultrafilter limit of computations.
Every deep computation is the restriction of an ultracomputation to the states, and for the iterative algorithms of Section~\ref{sec:iterative} the two notions coincide.
The analysis takes place on \emph{shards} and on \emph{type shards}.
A shard is a set of states on which each feature obeys a prescribed bound.
The type shard of a shard is its closure in the space of types.
Passing from a shard to its type shard adjoins the limit states of the shard.
Strictly speaking, the classification below concerns ultracomputations restricted to type shards.
We nevertheless speak of deep computations wherever the distinction plays no role.
A set of computations is \emph{stable} when all its deep computations, extended to the limit states, are continuous on every type shard.
The analysis of stable sets rests on a theorem proved by Grothendieck in 1952~\cite{Grothendieck:1952}, his double limit criterion.
The criterion concerns a uniformly bounded set of continuous functions on a compact space.
Consider a sequence of functions from the set and a sequence of points of the space.
The set has only continuous functions in its pointwise closure if and only if, for all such pairs of sequences, the two iterated limits agree whenever both exist.
Using this criterion, Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton characterized the stable sets by an exchange of iterated limits.
They also showed that the deep computations of stable sets are \emph{polynomially approximable}.
Polynomial approximability means that, on every shard, each output feature of such a deep computation is a uniform limit of polynomials in the input features.
Polynomial approximability corresponds to continuous extendibility in topology, and to \emph{stability} and \emph{type definability} in model theory.

Beyond stability, Shelah's classification theory~\cite{Shelah:1990} singles out the \emph{independence property}, which Shelah isolated in 1971~\cite[Definition~4.1]{Shelah:1971}.
A formula has the independence property when, for every natural number~$n$, there are $n$ parameters with the following property.
Every pattern of truth and falsity of the formula on these parameters is realized by some element of a model.
A theory none of whose formulas has the independence property is called \emph{NIP}, or \emph{dependent}.
The same dividing line appeared independently, within a few years, in two other fields.
There it took the form of a dichotomy between polynomial and exponential growth.
A family of sets shatters a finite set when it cuts out all the subsets of that set.
The traces of the family on a finite set are the intersections of the set with members of the family.
Every family of sets either shatters arbitrarily large finite sets, or leaves on every finite set a number of traces bounded by a polynomial in the size of the set.
In statistical learning, Vapnik and Chervonenkis announced this dichotomy in 1968~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1968} and published its proof in 1971~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971}.
Both works belong to their study of the uniform convergence of relative frequencies to probabilities.
In combinatorics, Sauer~\cite{Sauer:1972} and Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1972} proved the dichotomy in 1972, and it is now known as the Sauer--Shelah lemma.
The largest size of a finite set shattered by a family is called the \emph{Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension} of the family.

In analysis, the same combinatorial pattern emerged from the study of Banach spaces.
Rosenthal's $\ell_1$-theorem of 1974~\cite{Rosenthal:1974} states that every bounded sequence in a Banach space has a weakly Cauchy subsequence or a subsequence equivalent to the unit vector basis of~$\ell_1$.
Here $\ell_1$ is the space of absolutely summable sequences.
At the core of the proof of the $\ell_1$-theorem lies a dichotomy for bounded sequences of real functions on a set.
Such a sequence either has a pointwise convergent subsequence or has an \emph{independent} subsequence.
A sequence is independent when there are two thresholds with the following property.
For any two disjoint finite sets of indices, at some point the functions indexed by the first set take values below the lower threshold.
At the same point, the functions indexed by the second set take values above the upper one.
Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire} passed in 1978 from sequences to sets, and from convergence to the first Baire class.
This class consists of the pointwise limits of sequences of continuous functions.
They considered pointwise bounded sets of continuous functions on a Polish space, that is, on a separable and completely metrizable space.
By a condition of the same kind, they characterized those sets whose pointwise closures consist of functions of the first Baire class (see Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}).
On a compact metrizable space, their condition is exactly the absence of the independence property for the set of functions (see Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2}).
Compact sets of functions of the first Baire class on Polish spaces, with the topology of pointwise convergence, were investigated by Rosenthal in 1977~\cite{Rosenthal:1977}.
In 1980 Godefroy gave them the name \emph{Rosenthal compacta}~\cite{Godefroy:1980}.
Rosenthal compacta have since been studied intensively in functional analysis, especially in the theory of Banach spaces.

The classification of Rosenthal compacta took a decisive form in 1999, when Todorčević~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire} proved his trichotomy.
The trichotomy asserts that every separable Rosenthal compactum that is not metrizable contains a copy of one of a few canonical compacta.
Which of these canonical compacta occur depends on whether the compactum is hereditarily separable, first countable but not hereditarily separable, or not first countable (see Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}).
Todorčević’s proof relies on set-theoretic forcing and infinite Ramsey theory, and his methods continue to be analyzed and exploited~\cite{Todorcevic:2023,horowitz2019compactsetsbaireclass}.
Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame} used the trichotomy to arrange into a hierarchy the tame dynamical systems on compact metrizable spaces~\cite{Kohler1995,Glasner2006}.
The enveloping semigroup of a dynamical system is the pointwise closure of the set of maps of the system.
A dynamical system on a compact metrizable space is tame when its enveloping semigroup consists of maps of the first Baire class.
The levels of the Glasner--Megrelishvili hierarchy are defined by first countability, hereditary separability and metrizability of the enveloping semigroup.

The theories just recalled are the instruments with which we classify the unstable sets of computations.
An unstable set of computations is \emph{wild} when it has Shelah’s \emph{independence property} (IP).
Roughly, having the independence property means that for some feature the computations realize every finite pattern of high and low values on suitable inputs.
An unstable set of computations is \emph{tame}, or \emph{NIP}, when it does not have the independence property.
Between stability and NIP we identify three new levels of tameness, $\NIP_3$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_1$.
Together with NIP, these three levels form a hierarchy of four tame classes beyond the stable ones.
In this classification, the theory of Rosenthal compacta assumes on the topological side the role that Grothendieck’s criterion plays for stable sets.
On the model-theoretic side of the classification, the role of stability passes to NIP.
NIP is a central notion of the part of classification theory beyond stability that is known as \emph{neostability}.

Model theory is the medium through which topology and computation are translated into each other.
With every intermediate state of a computation we associate its \emph{type}, the record of the real values that all the features of the computation take at that state.
The types, together with their limits, form a space of real-valued functions on the set of features.
This space is the \emph{space of types}, and computations act on it.
The embedding of a structure into its space of types is a central device of model theory.
This embedding allows us to study topological limits of computations through the theory of function spaces with the topology of pointwise convergence, known as \emph{$\Cp$-theory}.
The space of types thus becomes a “Rosetta stone” connecting three classification programs.
The first is the classification of Rosenthal compacta in topology, pioneered by Todorčević~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}.
The second is the classification of theories in logic, developed by Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1971,Shelah:1990}.
The third is the theory of Vapnik--Chervonenkis (VC) dimension and of PAC (probably approximately correct) learnability in statistical learning~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971, Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974, Valiant:1984}.
In Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} we show that, under this translation, NIP sets of computations correspond to spaces of deep computations that are Rosenthal compacta on each type shard.
On the side of learning, finiteness of VC dimension implies NIP, and the two notions coincide for first-order formulas (see Section~\ref{sec:NIP-poly-exp}).


Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos~\cite{argyros2008rosenthal} refined Todorčević’s trichotomy into a heptachotomy, which we likewise bring to bear on deep computations.
The heptachotomy concerns families of functions indexed by the binary tree.
Such families are compared along dyadic subtrees, the copies of the binary tree inside itself.
Two such families are \emph{equivalent} when the bijection between them that respects the indexing extends to a homeomorphism of their closures.
Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos exhibited seven pairwise nonequivalent families of Baire class~1 functions indexed by the binary tree.
Each of these seven families is \emph{minimal}, in the sense that it is equivalent to each of its subfamilies indexed by a dyadic subtree.
Their theorem concerns relatively compact families of Baire class~1 functions on a Polish space, indexed by the binary tree.
It states that every such family contains, along a dyadic subtree, a family equivalent to one of the seven.

Approximation by sequences rests on a property shared by two settings.
In the setting of Grothendieck~\cite{Grothendieck:1952}, the functions are continuous on a compact space.
In that of Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}, the functions are of Baire class~1 on a Polish space.
In both settings, \emph{compactness and sequential compactness coincide} for sets of such functions with the topology of pointwise convergence.
Suppose, in either setting, that the closure of a set of such functions is compact and consists of such functions.
Then every point of the closure is the limit of a sequence from the set.
Consequently, when the features are countably many and the set of computations is $\NIP$, every deep computation is, on each shard, the limit of a \emph{sequence} of computations.
%, although in principle it is a nonconstructive ultrafilter limit over a possibly uncountable set of computations.

Our results show how the classification invariants used in computation, topology, descriptive set theory and model theory translate into one another.
The paper presupposes no acquaintance with advanced topology, model theory or the theory of computation.
Undergraduate topology and measure theory suffice, and the further background is assembled in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}.

Our first main result, Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, concerns compositional computation structures with countably many features.
In it we characterize the sets of computations that have the $\NIP$ on every shard that they preserve, one feature at a time.
A set of computations has this property exactly when its deep computations, extended to the limit states, form a Rosenthal compactum on each of the corresponding type shards.
In other words, these extended deep computations are of the first Baire class on those type shards.
Guided by Todorčević’s trichotomy, we then define three levels of tameness by the topology of these compacta (see Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}).
A countable $\NIP$ set of computations is $\NIP_3$ when, on each of these type shards, its deep computations form a metrizable space.
It is $\NIP_2$ when they form a hereditarily separable space on each of these type shards, and $\NIP_1$ when they form a first countable space there.
For countable sets of computations in compositional computation structures with countably many features, the levels form the hierarchy
\[
  \text{stable}\Rightarrow\NIP_3\Rightarrow\NIP_2\Rightarrow\NIP_1\Rightarrow\NIP.
\]
In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we show that every implication in this hierarchy is strict.

In that section we realize each level by a classical algorithm and exhibit algorithms beyond the hierarchy:
\begin{itemize}
\item
  A rotation of the circle is stable.
\item
  The power method is $\NIP_3$ but not stable.
\item
  Bresenham’s line algorithm is $\NIP_2$ but not $\NIP_3$ for lines in the plane, and $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$ for lines in three-dimensional space.
  For lines in space, its deep computations contain a copy of the extended duplicate of the split Cantor set but none of the Alexandroff duplicate of the Cantor set.
\item
  The Euclidean algorithm, acting on the projective line by the group that its steps generate, is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, and its deep computations include exact equality tests.
  Moreover, a countable family of compositions of its steps and their inverses is $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
  The deep computations of this family contain a copy of the Alexandroff duplicate of the Cantor set.
\item
  The logistic map $x\mapsto rx(1-x)$ on $[0,1]$ is stable for $0<r\le1$, $\NIP_3$ but not stable for $1<r<r_\infty$, and not~$\NIP$ for $r_\infty<r\le4$.
  Here $r_\infty\approx3.5699$ is the Feigenbaum parameter.
  At $r_\infty$ itself, as we sketch, the logistic map is again $\NIP_3$ but not stable.
\item
  Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere is not~$\NIP$, for the roots of $z^2-1$ as well as for those of the cubics $z^3-2z+2$ and $z^3-1$.
\end{itemize}
For iterative algorithms, the hierarchy coincides with the Glasner--Megrelishvili hierarchy of tame dynamical systems~\cite{glasner2022tame}.
At the level $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, our two examples are the Euclidean algorithm and free reduction in the free group on two generators.
Both examples are actions of groups with two generators rather than iterations of a single map.
Whether a single iterated map can reach this level is an open question, closely related to a question of Glasner and Megrelishvili (see Question~\ref{q:cascade-NIP1}).
Todorčević’s trichotomy allows two alternatives for separable Rosenthal compacta that are first countable but not hereditarily separable.
At the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$, the three-dimensional Bresenham algorithm realizes one of these alternatives.
The countable family of compositions of Euclidean steps and their inverses realizes the other.
Whether a single iterated map can realize this level with a copy of the Alexandroff duplicate among its deep computations is likewise open (see Question~\ref{q:cascade-duplicate}).

The Argyros--Dodos--Kanellopoulos heptachotomy refines this picture for families of computations indexed by the binary tree.
Fix a type shard and a feature.
Every tame family of computations indexed by the binary tree then contains, along a dyadic subtree, a family equivalent to one of the seven minimal families.
We read these seven families as primitives of binary search (see Section~\ref{sec:heptachotomy}).
Finally, in Section~\ref{sec:measure-theoretic-NIP} we replace topology by measure, which frees the theory from the countability of the set of features.
In Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} we show that a set of computations is $\NIP$ exactly when the restrictions of all its ultracomputations to the type shards are \emph{universally measurable}.
Universal measurability means measurability with respect to every Radon probability measure.
The deep computations of a $\NIP$ set are then \emph{universally Monte Carlo computable}.
Fix any Radon probability measure that governs the inputs.
Then, outside a set of arbitrarily small probability, each output feature of a deep computation is a uniform limit of polynomials in the input features.
We also relate $\NIP$ to \emph{Talagrand stability}, a measure-theoretic condition introduced by Talagrand in his study of Pettis integration.
Talagrand stability likewise yields universal Monte Carlo computability (see Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}).
We show that for countable sets of computations the two conditions coincide (see Corollary~\ref{cor:NIP-Talagrand-computations}).

\section{Topological preliminaries:\\
  From continuity to Baire class~1}
\label{sec:prelim}

In this section we assemble the topology of function spaces on which the rest of the paper rests.
Deep computations are pointwise limits of continuous maps, and the two themes of this paper, the approximation of a deep computation by a sequence of computations and the complexity of the set of all deep computations, therefore belong to the theory of spaces of functions with the topology of pointwise convergence.
We recall, in turn, Polish spaces and Baire category, the first Baire class, Rosenthal's dichotomy and the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy, from which the NIP emerges, and the Rosenthal compacta, five of which serve as yardsticks for the classification;
the section closes with the role of the NIP in model theory, combinatorics and statistical learning.
All spaces are assumed to be Hausdorff unless explicitly stated otherwise.

We write $\mathbb{N}$ for the set $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ of natural numbers, so that the semigroups of iterates considered below contain the identity.
A subset of a topological space is $F_\sigma$ if it is a countable union of closed sets, and $G_\delta$ if it is a countable intersection of open sets.
In a metric space every open set is~$F_\sigma$, and, equivalently, every closed set is~$G_\delta$.

A \emph{Polish space} is a separable topological space whose topology is induced by a complete metric.
Countable products of Polish spaces are Polish.
Classical examples, besides the real line, are the Cantor space~$2^\mathbb{N}$ of all infinite binary sequences, the Baire space $\mathbb{N}^\mathbb{N}$ of all infinite sequences of natural numbers, and the space $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ of all sequences of real numbers, each with the product topology.

\begin{fact}\cite[4.3.24]{Engelking:1989}
  A subset of a Polish space is itself Polish in the subspace topology if and only if it is a $G_{\delta }$ set.
  In particular, closed subsets and open subsets of Polish spaces are also Polish spaces.
\end{fact}

A subset $A$ of a topological space $X$ is \emph{nowhere dense} if its closure has empty interior, and of the \emph{first category}, or \emph{meager}, if it is contained in a countable union of closed nowhere dense sets;
otherwise $A$ is of the \emph{second category}.
The complements of meager sets are called \emph{comeager}.
Since the closure of a nowhere dense set is nowhere dense, the meager subsets of $X$ form the $\sigma$-ideal generated by the nowhere dense subsets of $X$.
A topological space $X$ is \emph{Baire} if every comeager subset of $X$ is dense, or, equivalently, if every countable intersection of dense open sets is dense.
The Baire category theorem states that compact Hausdorff spaces and completely metrizable spaces, Polish spaces in particular, are Baire.%
\footnote{More generally, any Čech-complete space is Baire~\cite[Theorem 3.9.3]{Engelking:1989}.}

For topological spaces $X$ and $Y$, we denote by $\Cp(X,Y)$ the set of all continuous functions $f\colon X\to Y$ with the topology of pointwise convergence, that is, the topology it inherits as a subspace of the product $Y^X$, in which $X$ serves merely as an index set.
We write $\Cp(X)$ for $\Cp(X,\mathbb{R})$.

A function $f\colon X\to Y$ between topological spaces is of \emph{Baire class~1} if it is the pointwise limit of a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of continuous functions, that is, $f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)$ for all $x\in X$.
When $Y$ is metrizable, an elementary argument shows that a function of Baire class~1 is continuous at every point outside a set of the first category.
We denote by $\mathrm{B}_1(X,Y)$ the set of functions $X\to Y$ of Baire class~1 with the topology of pointwise convergence, and we write $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ for $\mathrm{B}_1(X,\mathbb{R})$;
the inclusions $\Cp(X,Y)\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X,Y)\subseteq Y^X$ are topological.
Relatively compact subsets of $\Cp(X)$ and of $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, especially for Polish $X$, are central objects of study in functional analysis and in topological dynamics.

A topological space $X$ is \emph{perfectly normal} if it is normal and every closed subset of $X$ is a $G_\delta$ set (equivalently, every open subset of $X$ is an $F_\sigma$ set).
Every metrizable space (hence, every Polish space) is perfectly normal.

The following characterization of Baire class~1 functions is classical; for a proof, see~\cite[Section 10]{Todorcevic_1997_TopicsTop}.

\begin{fact}[Lebesgue, Hausdorff, Baire\protect\footnotemark]
  \label{baire}
  If $X$ is perfectly normal, then the following conditions are equivalent for a function $f:X\to \mathbb{R}$:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[(1)]
    $f$ is a Baire class~1 function, that is, $f$ is a pointwise limit of continuous functions.
  \item[(2)]
    $f^{-1}[U]$ is an $F_\sigma$ subset of $X$ whenever $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is open.
  \end{itemize}
  If $X$ is Polish, then (1) and (2) are equivalent to:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[(3)]
    For every nonempty closed $F\subseteq X$, the restriction $f|_F$ has a point of continuity.
  \end{itemize}
  Moreover, if $X$ is Polish and $f\notin \mathrm{B}_1(X)$, then there exist countable $D_0,D_1\subseteq X$ and reals $a<b$ such that
  \[
    D_0\subseteq f^{-1}(-\infty,a],
    \quad
    D_1\subseteq f^{-1}[b,\infty),
    \quad
    \overline{D_0}=\overline{D_1}.
  \]
\end{fact}
\footnotetext{The implication (1)$\Rightarrow$(2) holds in every topological space.
Indeed, if $f$ is the pointwise limit of a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of continuous functions and $a$ is a real number, then
\[
  f^{-1}(a,\infty)=\bigcup_{k\ge1}\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{n\ge m}f_n^{-1}[a+1/k,\infty)
\]
is a countable union of closed sets; the same is true of $f^{-1}(-\infty,a)$, and condition~(2) follows because every open subset of~$\mathbb{R}$ is a countable union of intervals $(a,b)=(a,\infty)\cap(-\infty,b)$.
The reverse implication, (2)$\Rightarrow$(1), was established by Lebesgue for real-valued functions of finitely many real variables~\cite{Lebesgue:1904} and extended by Hausdorff to real-valued functions on metric spaces~\cite[Theorem~24.10]{kechris1995cdst}.
The argument requires of~$X$ only that it be normal and that each of its open subsets be an $F_\sigma$ set, which are precisely the defining properties of a perfectly normal space.
The implication (1)$\Rightarrow$(3) holds whenever $X$ is Polish.
If $f$ satisfies~(1) and $F$ is a nonempty closed subset of~$X$, the restriction $f|_F$ is of Baire class~1, and its points of discontinuity therefore form a meager subset of~$F$, as noted before Fact~\ref{baire}.
Since $F$, being closed in a Polish space, is itself Polish and hence a Baire space, this meager set cannot exhaust~$F$, and so $f|_F$ has a point of continuity.
The reverse implication, (3)$\Rightarrow$(1), is due to Baire, who proved it in his thesis for real-valued functions of real variables~\cite{Baire:1899}; for Polish spaces, see~\cite[Theorem~24.15]{kechris1995cdst}.
Finally, the last assertion of Fact~\ref{baire} follows from the implication (3)$\Rightarrow$(1) together with the Baire category theorem.}

\subsection{From Rosenthal’s dichotomy to the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy to Shelah’s NIP}

The question when a deep computation is accessible to approximation, that is, when it is the limit of a \emph{sequence} of computations, is answered by Rosenthal's dichotomy and the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy, which we recall in this subsection and from which the NIP emerges in the theory of function spaces.
In a metrizable space every point of the closure of a set is the limit of a sequence of points of the set;
in spaces of functions with the topology of pointwise convergence the closure need not admit this sequential description, and the two dichotomies determine when it does not.

On the Cantor space $X=2^\mathbb{N}$, the $n$-th coordinate map $\pi_n\colon X\to\{0,1\}$ is continuous for each $n\in \mathbb{N}$;
however, the sequence $(\pi_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ has \emph{no} convergent subsequence in $\mathbb{R}^X$ (see~\cite[Chapter~1.1]{Todorcevic_1997_TopicsTop}).
In a sense, this sequence exhibits the most extreme failure of sequential convergence: the closure of $\{\pi_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ in $\{0, 1\}^X$, or in $\mathbb{R}^X$, is homeomorphic to the Stone--Čech compactification $\beta\mathbb{N}$ of the discrete space of natural numbers.
The space $\beta \mathbb{N}$ has cardinality $2^{2^{\aleph_0}}$, the largest possible for a separable Hausdorff space, and its points correspond to the ultrafilters on~$\mathbb{N}$.%
\footnote{\label{fn:id-ufilters}Addition on~$\mathbb{N}$ extends to an associative operation on~$\beta\mathbb{N}$, and, by the Ellis--Numakura lemma, $\beta\mathbb{N}$ has idempotent elements for this operation, the idempotent ultrafilters.
Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton~\cite{alva2024approximability} use them to prove the existence of \emph{deep equilibria} in compositional computation structures, that is, of limits of the iterates of a single computation, taken along an ultrafilter on~$\mathbb{N}$, that are idempotent under composition.}

The following theorem, proved by Haskell Rosenthal in 1974, shows that, for pointwise bounded sequences of continuous functions on a Polish space, a copy of $\beta\mathbb{N}$, such as the closure of the coordinate maps, is the only obstruction to the existence of a convergent subsequence.

\begin{thm}[Rosenthal’s dichotomy~\cite{Rosenthal:1974}]
  \label{thm:Rosenthal}
  If $X$ is a Polish space and $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a pointwise bounded sequence in $\Cp(X)$, then the sequence $(f_n)$ has either a convergent subsequence or a subsequence whose closure in $\mathbb{R}^X$ is homeomorphic to~$\beta\mathbb{N}$.
\end{thm}

The dichotomy grew out of \emph{Rosenthal’s $\ell_1$-theorem}, which states that every bounded sequence in a Banach space has a subsequence that is either weakly Cauchy or equivalent to the unit vector basis of $\ell_1$, the space of absolutely summable sequences;
in particular, a Banach space contains no isomorphic copy of $\ell_1$ if and only if every bounded sequence in it has a weakly Cauchy subsequence.

The Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand theorem concerns sets rather than sequences: for a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on a Polish space~$X$, it characterizes the relative compactness of the set in the larger space $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, that is, the property that every function in its pointwise closure be of Baire class~1.
The theorem is a counterpart of Rosenthal’s dichotomy, although it is usually stated as an equivalence rather than as a dichotomy (see Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} below).

The statement of the theorem requires the following notation.
For $f\in \mathbb{R}^X$ and a real number $a$, define
\begin{align*}
X^f_{\le a} & \coloneqq f^{-1}(-\infty,a],  &
X^f_{\ge a} & \coloneqq f^{-1}[a, +\infty).
\end{align*}
For a set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ and a real number $a$, define, by a slight abuse of notation,
\begin{align*}
X^A_{\le a} & \coloneqq \bigcap_{f\in A}X^f_{\le a} = \{x\in X: \text{$f(x)\le a$ for all $f\in A$}\}, \\
X^A_{\ge a} & \coloneqq \bigcap_{f\in A}X^f_{\ge a} = \{x\in X: \text{$f(x)\ge a$ for all $f\in A$}\};
\end{align*}
in particular, $X^\emptyset_{\ge a} = X = X^\emptyset_{\le a}$.
For a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^X$ and a set $I\subseteq \mathbb{N}$, define $I^{\complement} \coloneqq \mathbb{N}\setminus I$ and $f_I \coloneqq \{f_i: i\in I\}$.

\begin{thm}[The Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}]
  \label{thm:BFT}
  Let $X$ be a Polish space, and let $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$.
  \item
    For every $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A$ and every $a<b$ there is $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
    X_{\le a}^{f_I} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_{I^{\complement}}} = \emptyset.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
(In this form, the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy is a special case of the equivalence (ii)$\Leftrightarrow$(v) of Corollary~4G of Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}.)

In condition~(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}, the set $X_{\le a}^{f_I}$ is defined by the inequalities $f_i(x)\le a$ with $i\in I$, and the set $X_{\ge b}^{f_{I^{\complement}}}$ by the inequalities $f_j(x)\ge b$ with $j\in I^{\complement}$.
Since $I$ or $I^{\complement}$ is infinite, at least one of the two sets is defined by countably infinitely many inequalities.
A computation, however, can check only finitely many inequalities at a time, and the following definitions accordingly impose only finitely many.

\begin{defn}\label{def:NIP-fns}
  A set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ of functions has the \emph{finitary No-Independence Property (NIP)}
  if, for every sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $A$ and all reals $a<b$,
  there exist finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that $X_{\le a}^{f_I} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_J} = \emptyset$;
  we say that such sets $I, J$ \emph{witness finitary NIP for $(f_n)$ and $a, b$}.

  A set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ has the \emph{finitary Independence Property (IP)} if it does not have finitary NIP,
  that is, if there exist a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $A$ and reals $a<b$ such that
  $X_{\le a}^{f_I} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_J} \ne \emptyset$ for every pair of finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$.
\end{defn}

If the word “finite” is omitted in these definitions, we obtain \emph{countable} NIP, which is weaker than finitary NIP, and \emph{countable} IP, which is stronger than finitary IP.
If, in addition, the witnesses are required to satisfy $J = I^{\complement}$, we obtain the properties that we call \emph{BFT-NIP} and \emph{BFT-IP}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} thus states that, on a Polish space~$X$, a pointwise bounded set $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ if and only if it has BFT-NIP.

From now on, IP and NIP mean \emph{finitary} IP and NIP unless otherwise specified.

\begin{thm}
  \label{thm:BFT2}
  Let $X$ be a compact metrizable (hence Polish) space.
  For every pointwise bounded set $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$, the following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A$ is relatively compact in~$\mathrm{B}_1(X)$;
  \item
    $A$ has BFT-NIP;
  \item
    $A$ has countable NIP;
  \item
    $A$ has finitary NIP.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
(The equivalence \emph{(ii)}$\Leftrightarrow$\emph{(iv)} holds for every compact space~$X$, even without the assumption that $A$ is pointwise bounded, and the equivalence \emph{(ii)}$\Leftrightarrow$\emph{(iii)} holds for every set $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$ on an arbitrary space~$X$.)
\begin{proof}
  The equivalence (i)$\Leftrightarrow$(ii) is Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}.
  The implications (iv)$\Rightarrow$(iii) and (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii) are trivial, and (iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii) follows because, if $I$ and $J$ witness countable NIP, then $J\subseteq I^{\complement}$, and hence $I$ and $I^{\complement}$ witness BFT-NIP.
  To prove (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iv), suppose that $(f_n)$ and $a<b$ witness finitary IP, and let $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ be given.
  The closed sets $X^{f_n}_{\le a}$ ($n\in I$) and $X^{f_m}_{\ge b}$ ($m\in I^{\complement}$) have the finite intersection property, so, by compactness, $X^{f_I}_{\le a}\cap X^{f_{I^{\complement}}}_{\ge b}\neq\emptyset$.
  Since $I$ is arbitrary, $A$ does not have BFT-NIP, and (ii) fails.
\end{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} can be read as a dichotomy: if $X$ is compact metrizable and $A\subseteq\Cp(X)$ is pointwise bounded, then either $A$ is relatively compact in~$\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, or $A$ has IP, in the finitary and, equivalently, in the countable sense.

The independence property was isolated by Saharon Shelah~\cite[Definition 4.1]{Shelah:1971} as one of the dividing lines of his classification of first-order theories~\cite{Shelah:1990}.
We return to it in Section~\ref{sec:NIP-poly-exp}, where it appears together with its counterparts in combinatorics and in statistical learning.

\subsection{Rosenthal compacta}

The Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy leads to the Rosenthal compacta, the compact spaces within which the deep computations of this paper are classified, and we recall them here.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}, if a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on a Polish space has BFT-NIP, then its pointwise closure is a compact set of functions of the first Baire class.
Compact sets of this kind had appeared in Rosenthal's work on the $\ell_1$-theorem, and he made them the subject of a study of their own in 1977~\cite{Rosenthal:1977}; Godefroy~\cite{Godefroy:1980} named them after him in the following definition.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:Rosenthal-compacta}
  A \emph{Rosenthal compactum} is a topological space homeomorphic to a compact subset of $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, with the topology of pointwise convergence, for some Polish space~$X$.
\end{defn}

Rosenthal compacta have strong tameness properties;
for instance, every point in the closure of a subset of a Rosenthal compactum is the limit of a sequence of points of the subset~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}.
In this paper they serve as spaces of deep computations: in Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} below we show that, for countably many features, the deep computations of an NIP set of computations, extended to the limit states, form a Rosenthal compactum on each type shard, a compact set of states and limit states on which every feature obeys a prescribed bound (see Section~\ref{sec:shards}).

\subsection{The special case~\texorpdfstring{$\mathrm{B}_1(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$}{B\_1(X, R\textasciicircum P)} with~\texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{P}$}{P} countable}

This subsection is devoted to reducing the study of maps with values in a product of copies of the real line to that of real-valued functions.
The reduction is needed because a state of a computation is described by the values of its features, and a computation is therefore a map with values in the product of copies of the real line indexed by the features.
Fix a countable set~$\mathcal{P}$ (in this paper, “countable” means finite or countably infinite), whose elements we call \emph{predicate symbols} or \emph{formal predicates}.
Our goal is to characterize the relatively compact subsets of $\mathrm{B}_1(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$, where $X$ is a perfectly normal space, typically a Polish space.

Whenever $\mathcal{P}$ is regarded as a topological space, it carries the discrete topology, and the product $X\times \mathcal{P}$ is regarded either as a set or as a topological product, depending on the context.
The spaces $\Cp(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})$ are subspaces of the ambient space $(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X$, which can be described in two other ways, through the natural homeomorphisms
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X \cong \mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \cong (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}},
\end{equation*}
given by
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \to (\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X: \Phi & \mapsto {\hat{}}\Phi    \\
\mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \to (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}}: \Phi & \mapsto \Phi{\,\hat{}},
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
\hat{}\Phi(x)\coloneqq \Phi(x, \cdot)\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}, &  &
\Phi\,\hat{}(P)\coloneqq \Phi(\cdot, P) \in \mathbb{R}^X.
\end{align*}
These identifications treat $X$ and $\mathcal{P}$ as mere sets;
in particular, the topology of~$X$ plays no role in them.



The following proposition reduces the study of continuous functions, and of functions of Baire class~1, from $X$ to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ to the case of real-valued functions.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Baire-identifications}
  The identification $(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X \cong \mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \cong (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}}$ induces identifications
  \begin{align*}
  \Cp(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}) \cong \Cp(X\times \mathcal{P}) \cong \Cp(X)^{\mathcal{P}}, &  &
  \mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}) \cong \mathrm{B}_1(X\times \mathcal{P}) \cong \mathrm{B}_1(X)^{\mathcal{P}}.
  \end{align*}
\end{prop}
(The cardinality of $\mathcal{P}$ plays no role here.)
\begin{proof}
  The identification of the $\Cp$-spaces follows from the definition of the product topology and the discreteness of $\mathcal{P}$:
  a continuous map $X\to \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ is precisely a $\mathcal{P}$-indexed family of continuous functions $X\to \mathbb{R}$, and such families correspond to continuous functions $X\times \mathcal{P}\to \mathbb{R}$.
  The identification of the spaces of functions of Baire class~1 follows, since membership in the first Baire class is defined through pointwise limits of sequences of continuous functions, and a sequence of $\mathcal{P}$-indexed families of continuous functions is the same as a $\mathcal{P}$-indexed family of sequences of continuous functions.
\end{proof}

For $A\subseteq Y^X$ and $K\subseteq X$, we write $A|_K\coloneqq\{f|_K:f\in A\}$ for the set of restrictions to $K$ of the functions in $A$;
for $A\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$, we write $\pi_P\circ A\coloneqq\{\pi_P\circ f:f\in A\}$, where $\pi_P\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the projection onto the coordinate~$P$.
The following theorem extends Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$-valued functions.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Generalized-BFT}
  Assume that $\mathcal{P}$ is countable, $X$ is a Polish space, and $A\subseteq\allowbreak \Cp(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$ is pointwise bounded in the sense that the set $\pi_P\circ A\subseteq\Cp(X)$ is pointwise bounded for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  The following are equivalent for every compact $K\subseteq X$:

  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A|_K$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(K,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$;
  \item
    $\pi_P\circ A|_K$ has NIP for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
\begin{proof}
  By Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications} and Tychonov’s theorem, condition~(i) holds if and only if $\pi_P\circ A|_K$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(K)$ for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  Since $K$ is compact metrizable, Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} shows that this is equivalent to~(ii).
\end{proof}

In the applications, the NIP is verified on a set of states, whereas compactness is available only on its closure; the following lemma shows that the NIP passes from a set to its closure.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:NIP-and-closure}
  Assume that $X$ is Hausdorff and that $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$.
  The following are equivalent for every $L\subseteq X$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A|_L$ satisfies the NIP;
  \item
    $A|_{\overline{L}}$ satisfies the NIP.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  The implication (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) is trivial.
  Conversely, suppose that a sequence $(f_n)$ in $A$ and reals $a<b$ witness the IP on $\overline{L}$, and choose $a'$ and $b'$ with $a<a'<b'<b$.
  For finite disjoint $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, the open set $\bigcap_{n\in I}f_n^{-1}(-\infty,a')\cap\bigcap_{n\in J}f_n^{-1}(b',\infty)$ contains a point of $\overline{L}$, namely a point at which $f_n\le a$ for $n\in I$ and $f_n\ge b$ for $n\in J$;
  hence it meets~$L$.
  Therefore the sequence $(f_n)$ and the reals $a'<b'$ witness the IP on~$L$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Five canonical Rosenthal compacta}
\label{sec:five-compacta}
Here we fix the notation for five Rosenthal compacta, all built on the Cantor set, that are the canonical members of the classes of Todorčević’s trichotomy (see Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy} below);
by the trichotomy, the space of deep computations of each non-metrizable $\NIP$ example of Section~\ref{sec:iterative} contains a copy of one of them.
The five compacta are distinguished from one another by three topological properties.
Recall that a topological space is \emph{separable} if it has a countable dense subset, \emph{hereditarily separable} if each of its subspaces is separable, and \emph{first countable} if each of its points has a countable base of neighborhoods.

Let $2^{\mathbb{N}}$ carry the product topology and the lexicographic order~$<$, whose order topology is the product topology.
The set of finite binary words is denoted $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, the length of a word $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is denoted~$|t|$, and, for $a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$, we write $a\restr n$ for the word formed by the first $n$ bits of~$a$.
Two words are \emph{comparable} if one extends the other, and an \emph{antichain} is a set of pairwise incomparable words.
For a finite word $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, let $t^\frown0^\infty$ and $t^\frown1^\infty$ be the sequences that begin with $t$ and continue with all $0$’s and with all $1$’s, respectively;
the set of sequences extending $t$ is the clopen interval $[t^\frown0^\infty,t^\frown1^\infty]$.
Copies of the binary tree within itself index the dense subset of the last of the five compacta below, and the families of Section~\ref{sec:heptachotomy} are compared along them:
a \emph{dyadic subtree} of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is a subset isomorphic to $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ under the order of extension;
we write it as $\{w_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, indexed through the canonical isomorphism, which preserves extension and the lexicographic order, and we call it \emph{regular} if each of its levels is contained in a level of~$2^{<\mathbb{N}}$.
A sequence $(u_k)$ of words \emph{converges} to $a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$ if, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, all but finitely many of the words $u_k$ extend $a\restr n$.

Double brackets denote truth values: $\Brack{\dots}$ is~$1$ if the statement inside the brackets is true and~$0$ if it is false.
We introduce the following functions $2^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item
  the \emph{prefix test} $v_t\coloneqq \Brack{\cdot{\restriction}|t| = t}$, the characteristic function of the set of sequences extending a fixed word~$t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$;
\item
  the \emph{equality test} $\delta_a\coloneqq\Brack{\cdot = a}$, the characteristic function of the singleton $\{a\}$ for fixed~$a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$;
\item
  the \emph{threshold tests} $\chi_{[a}\coloneqq\Brack{a\le \cdot}$ and $\chi_{(a}\coloneqq\Brack{a<\cdot}$, the characteristic functions of the final intervals $\{x:a\le x\}$ (closed) and~$\{x:a<x\}$ (open), for fixed~$a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$;
  the special case $\mathbf{0}\coloneqq \chi_{(1^\infty}$ is the identically zero function.
\end{itemize}
Prefix tests are continuous, and so are $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$ and $\chi_{(t^\frown1^\infty}$, since each of these functions depends only on the first $|t|$ bits of its argument;
equality tests, and threshold tests at all other points, are discontinuous functions of Baire class~$1$.
All closures below are taken in the topology of pointwise convergence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  The \emph{Alexandroff compactification}
  $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{\delta_a:a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}$.
  Each $\delta_a$ is isolated, and every neighborhood of $\mathbf{0}$ contains all but finitely many of the functions $\delta_a$.
  Thus the Alexandroff compactification is neither separable nor first countable at~$\mathbf{0}$.
\item
  The \emph{extended Alexandroff compactification}
  $\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\overline{\{v_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}}=\{v_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\cup A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
  As $n\to\infty$, the prefix tests $v_{a\restr n}$ converge to $\delta_a$;
  along an antichain they converge to $\mathbf{0}$.
  Hence the extended Alexandroff compactification is separable but not first countable.
\item
  The \emph{split Cantor set}
  $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{\chi_{[a},\chi_{(a}:a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  The continuous thresholds $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$, together with the isolated point $\mathbf{0}=\chi_{(1^\infty}$, form a countable dense subset:
  thresholds approaching $a$ from below converge to $\chi_{[a}$, and from above to~$\chi_{(a}$.
  It follows that the split Cantor set is separable, first countable and hereditarily separable, but not metrizable.
\item
  The \emph{Alexandroff duplicate}
  $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{(\mathbf{0},a),(\delta_a,a):a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq A(2^{\mathbb{N}})\times2^{\mathbb{N}}$.
  The points $(\delta_a,a)$ are isolated, and every neighborhood of $(\mathbf{0},a)$ contains, for all $b$ in some neighborhood of~$a$, the point $(\mathbf{0},b)$ and, with finitely many exceptions, the point $(\delta_b,b)$.
  Consequently, the Alexandroff duplicate is first countable but not separable;
  it is contained in the separable compactum $\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\overline{\{(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty):t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}}=D(2^{\mathbb{N}})\cup\{(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty):t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
\item
  The \emph{extended duplicate of the split Cantor set}, which we define, as Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos do~\cite[4.3.7]{argyros2008rosenthal}, along a regular dyadic subtree $\{s_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, fixed once and for all, such that $s_t^\frown0^\infty\neq s_{t'}^\frown0^\infty$ and $s_t^\frown1^\infty\neq s_{t'}^\frown1^\infty$ for all distinct $t,t'\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$:
  \[
    \hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))=\overline{\{(v_{s_t},\chi_{[s_t^\frown0^\infty}):t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}}\subseteq\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})\times S(2^{\mathbb{N}}).
  \]
  Call a sequence $a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$ a \emph{branch} of the subtree if each of its initial segments $a\restr n$ is extended by some word~$s_t$;
  the branches form a perfect subset of~$2^{\mathbb{N}}$.
  The compactum $\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ consists of the isolated points $(v_{s_t},\chi_{[s_t^\frown0^\infty})$ and, for each branch~$a$, of the point $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$, of the point $(\mathbf{0},\chi_{[a})$ if some sequence $(u_k)$ of pairwise incomparable words of the subtree converges to~$a$ with $u_k^\frown0^\infty$ increasing in~$k$, and of the point $(\mathbf{0},\chi_{(a})$ if some such sequence converges to~$a$ with $u_k^\frown0^\infty$ decreasing in~$k$~\cite[4.3.7]{argyros2008rosenthal}.
  The points $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$ are reached along the branches:
  if $a$ is a branch and $u_1,u_2,\dots$ are the words of the subtree that are initial segments of~$a$, listed by increasing length, then the sequences $u_k^\frown0^\infty$ increase strictly to~$a$, and the points $(v_{u_k},\chi_{[u_k^\frown0^\infty})$ converge to $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$ as $k\to\infty$.
  As the closure of a countable set, the extended duplicate of the split Cantor set is separable;
  it is also first countable, since it embeds into the Helly space of nondecreasing functions from $[0,1]$ into itself, which is first countable~\cite[4.3.7]{argyros2008rosenthal}.
  Since $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$ is the only point $(\delta_b,\chi_{[b})$, with $b$ a branch, whose first coordinate takes the value~$1$ at~$a$, these points form a discrete subspace, which has the cardinality of the continuum because the branches form a perfect set;
  hence the extended duplicate of the split Cantor set is not hereditarily separable.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{NIP in model theory, combinatorics and learning: polynomial versus exponential growth}
\label{sec:NIP-poly-exp}

We now trace the NIP of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns} to its counterparts in model theory, combinatorics and statistical learning, and make precise how it is related to them;
the relation is used in Section~\ref{sec:iterative}, where a bound on a Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension yields the NIP of Bresenham’s algorithm.
For families of $\{0,1\}$-valued functions, that is, of sets, a combinatorial dividing line closely related to the NIP appeared independently in three fields between 1968 and 1972, some years before Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}:
in model theory, in the work of Saharon Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1971,Shelah:1990};
in combinatorics, in the work of Norbert Sauer~\cite{Sauer:1972} and, again, of Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1972,Shelah:1990};
and in statistical learning, in the work of Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1968,Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971,Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974}.
In each of them, the line separates the “tame” families of sets, whose number of traces on an $n$-element set (the intersections of the members of the family with the set) is bounded by a polynomial in~$n$, from the “wild” ones, for which, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, some $n$-element set has all $2^n$ possible traces.

\begin{description}

\item[In model theory]
  Among the dividing lines of Shelah’s classification theory~\cite{Shelah:1990}, the independence property is detected by counting types over finite sets of parameters.
  Let $T$ be a complete first-order theory, let $\varphi(x;y)$ be a formula of its language, in which $x$ and $y$ are finite tuples of variables of lengths $|x|$ and $|y|$, and let $B$ be a finite set of $|y|$-tuples of elements of a model $M$ of~$T$.
  A \emph{$\varphi$-type} over $B$ is a maximal set of formulas $\varphi(x;b)$ and $\neg\varphi(x;b)$, with $b\in B$, that is consistent with the theory of $M$ with constants for the elements of~$M$;
  since $B$ is finite, every $\varphi$-type over $B$ is realized by an $|x|$-tuple $a$ of elements of $M$, and it is determined by the set $\{b\in B: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$.
  Hence the set $S_\varphi(B)$ of $\varphi$-types over $B$ is in bijection with the set of traces on $B$ of the family of sets $\{b: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$, with $a$ ranging over $|x|$-tuples of elements of $M$, and $|S_\varphi(B)|\le2^{|B|}$.
  The formula $\varphi$ has the \emph{independence property} (IP) if, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, some set $B$ of $n$ tuples satisfies $|S_\varphi(B)|=2^n$;
  otherwise the formula is \emph{NIP}, or \emph{dependent}, and the theory $T$ is NIP if all its formulas are NIP.
  Since $T$ is complete, these notions do not depend on the model~$M$.

  If $\varphi$ is NIP, the Sauer--Shelah lemma below bounds $|S_\varphi(B)|$ by a polynomial in $|B|$.
  If $\varphi$ has IP, then for every $n$ some set $A$ of at most $n|y|$ parameters satisfies $|S_\varphi(A)|\ge2^n$, where $S_\varphi(A)$ denotes the set of $\varphi$-types over the set of $|y|$-tuples of elements of~$A$~\cite[Section~2.3.4]{simon2015nip}.
  Consequently, $\varphi$ is NIP if and only if $|S_\varphi(A)|$ is bounded by a polynomial in $|A|$ as $A$ ranges over the finite sets of parameters.
  Over infinite sets of parameters, counting $\varphi$-types does not separate NIP from IP without further set-theoretic hypotheses: if $\kappa$ is infinite and $2^\kappa=\kappa^+$, every unstable formula, NIP or not, already has $2^\kappa$ $\varphi$-types over some set of $\kappa$ parameters~\cite[Section~2.3.4]{simon2015nip}.

\item[In combinatorics]
  Sauer~\cite{Sauer:1972} and Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1972} proved independently the following dichotomy, now known as the \emph{Sauer--Shelah lemma}.
  Let $\mathscr{F}=\{C_i:i\in I\}$ be a family of subsets of a set $\Omega$, indexed by a set~$I$.
  Then either
  for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$ there is a set $B\subseteq \Omega$ with $|B|=n$ such that $|\{C_i\cap B: i\in I\}|=2^n$, in which case $\mathscr{F}$ is said to have “exponential complexity”,
  or
  there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that, for every $B\subseteq \Omega$ with $|B|\ge N$,
  \[
    |\{C_i\cap B: i\in I\}| \le \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \binom{|B|}{j},
  \]
  in which case $\mathscr{F}$ is said to have “polynomial complexity” (every family of subsets of a \emph{finite} set $\Omega$ has polynomial complexity).
  The name is justified by the following estimate, valid for fixed $N>0$ and for $|B| = m > 0$, which bounds the number of traces by a polynomial in~$m$:
  \begin{equation*}
  \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \binom{m}{j}
  \le \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{m^{j}}{j!}
  \le \left( \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{j!} \right)\cdot m^{N-1}
  < e\cdot m^{N-1}.
  \end{equation*}

\item[In machine learning]
  The Sauer--Shelah lemma is also the dichotomy that Vapnik and Chervonenkis, in their study of uniform convergence in statistics, had announced in 1968~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1968} and whose proof they published in 1971~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971}, a year before the papers of Sauer and Shelah (see also~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974}).
  The largest size of a set $B\subseteq \Omega$ with $2^{|B|}$ traces $C_i\cap B$, that is, the integer $N-1$ for the least integer $N$ as in the Sauer--Shelah lemma, is called the \emph{VC dimension} of $\mathscr{F}$;
  if no such integer $N$ exists, the VC dimension of $\mathscr{F}$ is infinite.
  Through the Vapnik--Chervonenkis theorem, the lemma bounds the number of data points (the sample size) needed to “learn” a concept class~$\mathscr{F}$ of finite VC dimension in a statistical sense, that is, up to a preset error and with a preset confidence.
  The Fundamental Theorem of Statistical Learning states that a class of $\{0,1\}$-valued functions is PAC-learnable (PAC stands for “Probably Approximately Correct”~\cite{Valiant:1984}) if and only if its VC dimension is finite, provided that the class satisfies a mild measurability condition~\cite{Blumer-Ehrenfeucht-Haussler-Warmuth:1989};
  see also~\cite[Theorem~6.7]{Shalev-Shwartz-Ben-David:2014}.

\end{description}

For first-order formulas the three dichotomies coincide:
by the description of $\varphi$-types given above, a formula $\varphi(x;y)$ has IP exactly when the family of sets $\{b: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$, with $a$ ranging over the $|x|$-tuples of elements of a model $M$ of~$T$, has infinite VC dimension, and for an NIP formula the Sauer--Shelah lemma gives the polynomial bound on the number of $\varphi$-types.
For an arbitrary family of sets, finiteness of the VC dimension implies that the characteristic functions of its members have the NIP of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns}.
The NIP does not, however, imply finite VC dimension: the finite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ form a family of infinite VC dimension whose characteristic functions have the NIP.

\subsection*{Historical remarks}
The Baire hierarchy of functions was introduced by René-Louis Baire in his 1899 doctoral thesis, \emph{Sur les fonctions de variables réelles}.
The theory of spaces of continuous functions with the topology of pointwise convergence, $\Cp$-theory, was pioneered by A.~V.~Arhangel’skiĭ and his students in the 1970s and 1980s~\cite{Arkhangelskii:1992},
and it has since found numerous applications in model theory and in functional analysis;
Tkachuk’s problem book~\cite{tkachuk2011cp} develops the subject systematically, through problems with complete solutions.

\section{Compositional Computation Structures: \\
  A structural approach to arbitrary-precision computations}
\label{sec:CCS}

The theory of function spaces assembled in Section~\ref{sec:prelim} is brought to bear on computation through the framework of compositional computation structures, in which deep computations become points of a function space;
the present section is devoted to that framework.
A computation on real-valued data is observed through its features, each known to finite precision, and a deep computation is a limit of computations in the topology of this observation, the topology of pointwise convergence.
We first recall the framework of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}: states are described by their types, the tuples of values of all their features;
computations act on states and are composed;
and deep computations are the pointwise limits of computations, taken in the space of types.
We then recall the stable sets of computations, studied in that paper, and close the section with two examples that motivate the classification of Section~\ref{sec:Classification}.

\subsection{Computation States Structures}
\label{sec:CSS}

The first ingredient of the framework, which we introduce here, is the set of states on which computations act, together with the real-valued features through which the states are observed.
A \emph{computation states structure} is a pair $(L,\mathcal{P})$, where $L$ is a set whose elements we call \emph{states} and $\mathcal{P}$ is a collection of real-valued functions on $L$ that we call \emph{predicates} or \emph{features}.
Intuitively, $L$ is the set of states of a computation, and the predicates are the primitives that are given and accepted as computable;
for $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $v\in L$, the number $P(v)$ is the value of the (observable) feature~$P$ at the state~$v$.
Maps $f\colon L\to L$ are called \emph{transitions} of $(L,\mathcal{P})$;
the computations of Definition~\ref{def:CCS} below are transitions.

The \emph{type} of a state $v\in L$ is the indexed family%
\footnote{As in Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications}, where $\mathcal{P}$ is merely a set of symbols, we regard each predicate as named by a formal label~$P$, a \emph{predicate symbol}, whose meaning in the computation states structure is the real function $P(\cdot)\colon v\mapsto P(v)$ on~$L$.
  The symbol $P$ thus names an observable feature, whose value at a state $v\in L$ is $P(v)$.
  Whenever $\mathcal{P}$ is used as an index set, as in the product $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ below, it is regarded as a set of symbols.
  The distinction between a symbol and its meaning is usually suppressed in mathematics, but the model-theoretic framework requires awareness of it.
  When necessary, we write $P(\cdot)$ for the function on~$L$ and $P$ for the symbol;
  when no confusion can arise, we also write $P$ for the function.}
\[
  \operatorname{tp}(v)= (P(v))_{P\in \mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}
\]
of its features.



We assume that each state $v\in L$ is determined by its type $\operatorname{tp}(v)$;
we may then identify $L$ with its image $\tp[L]\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ under the type map and give it the subspace topology, that is, the topology of pointwise convergence induced by the product topology of~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$.
In particular, for each $P\in \mathcal{P}$, the map $v\mapsto P(v)$ is continuous on~$L$.

The principal examples are the state space $L=\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$, with the predicates $P_i(v) = v_i$ for $i\in\mathbb{N}$, and, for a positive integer~$n$, the state space $L=\mathbb{R}^n$, with the predicates $P_i(v)=v_i$ for $0\le i<n$.
Their subspaces are the state spaces of primary interest in this paper;
through the identification of each state with its type, every computation states structure with countably many predicates is, once its predicates are enumerated, such a subspace.

\begin{defn}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P})$ be a computation states structure.
  The type $\tp(v)\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of a state $v\in L$ is called a \emph{realized} (state) type.
  The closure of the set of realized types in~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$, with the topology of pointwise convergence, is called the \emph{space of (state) types} of~$(L,\mathcal{P})$ and is denoted~$\mathcal{L}$;
  its elements $\mathbf{v}\in \mathcal{L}$ are called \emph{state types}, and those in $\mathcal{L}\setminus L$ are called \emph{unrealized} state types.
\end{defn}

State types formalize the intuitive notion of a limit state, and the predicates extend to them continuously.
Indeed, for each $P\in \mathcal{P}$, the $P$-th coordinate map $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{v}_Q)_{Q\in \mathcal{P}}\mapsto \mathbf{v}_P$, restricts to a continuous extension to~$\mathcal{L}$ of the predicate $P(\cdot)$ on~$L$;
abusing notation, we still denote this extension by~$P(\cdot)$, or just~$P$.

\begin{rem}\label{rem:poor-mans-ultraprod}
  One may regard unrealized state types as “hyper-states”;
  when such a type is obtained as the limit of a sequence (or as an ultralimit) of realized state types, it may be called a “deep state”.
  For readers versed in model theory we note that, at least for the purpose of realizing state types with parameters in the state space~$L$,
  the type space $(\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{P})$ serves as a “poor man’s” ultraproduct of~$(L, \mathcal{P})$ in which, by the very definition of~$\mathcal{L}$, every type $\mathbf{v}$ is realized.
\end{rem}

\subsubsection{Bounds on precision and magnitude. Shards}
\label{sec:shards}

The analysis of this paper takes place on shards, the sets of states on which each feature obeys a prescribed bound, and before defining them we explain why actual computation imposes such bounds.
Computation states structures are designed to model actual computation, in which operations on real-valued quantities can be carried out only approximately, to a preset degree of precision.
A bound on the magnitude of the quantities involved is no less important in practice, for no machine can store, let alone compute with, arbitrarily large numbers.
An actual computation therefore fixes at the outset bounds for its errors and for the absolute magnitudes of the quantities it handles, even when the collection~$\mathcal{P}$ of predicates is infinite, possibly uncountable;
different parts of a computation may require different error bounds and may deal with quantities of very different sizes.
Focusing on magnitudes, we consider sets of computations for which a bound for each predicate is fixed in advance, and we are thereby led to the notion of \emph{shard}, introduced in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.

\begin{defn}
  Let $(L, \mathcal{P})$ be a computation states structure, with $L$ identified with the set $\tp[L]\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of its realized types.
  A \emph{sizer} is a family $r_{\bullet}=(r_P)_{P\in\mathcal{P}}$ of positive real numbers indexed by~$\mathcal{P}$.
  For a sizer $r_\bullet$, let $\mathbb{R}[\rb] \coloneqq \prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}[-r_P,r_P]$, a compact space, and define the $r_\bullet$-\emph{shard} of the state space~$L$ by
  \[
    L[r_\bullet] = L\cap \mathbb{R}[\rb].
  \]

  The \emph{$r_{\bullet}$-type shard} is the closure $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]=\overline{L[r_\bullet]}$, a closed and hence compact subset of $\mathbb{R}[\rb]$.

  The union of all type shards, as the sizer $r_{\bullet}$ varies, is denoted $\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ and called the space of \emph{shard-supported types}.
\end{defn}

We have $\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\subseteq \mathcal{L}$, and the inclusion may be proper;
equality holds in the important special case when $\mathcal{P}$ is countable (see~\cite{alva2024approximability}).

\subsection{Compositional Computation Structures}

The compositional computation structures of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}, within which deep computations are defined, are recalled in this subsection, beginning with the ingredients of their definition.
Computations transform states, and two computations performed one after the other form a computation;
the computations of a system therefore form a semigroup under composition, which we take to contain the identity, the computation that does nothing.
Deep computations are the limits of computations in the topology of pointwise convergence, taken in the space of types.
Since an actual computation keeps the magnitudes that it handles within bounds, the shards of Section~\ref{sec:shards} enter the definition through the notion of confinement.
The following definition records these ingredients.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:CCS}
  A \emph{compositional computation structure} (CCS) is a triple $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$, where
  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    $(L,\mathcal{P})$ is a computation states structure, and
  \item
    $\Gamma\subseteq L^L$ is a semigroup under composition that contains the identity map $\text{id}$ of~$L$, that is, a monoid of transformations of~$L$.
  \end{itemize}
  The elements of $\Gamma$ are called the \emph{computations} of $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$.
  The state space $L$ is identified with a subset of the type space~$\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of~$(L, \mathcal{P})$.

  We topologize $\Gamma$ as a subset of the product $L^L$, in which, as usual, the exponent~$L$ serves merely as an index set, while the base~$L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ carries the topology of pointwise convergence.
  The semigroup $\Gamma$ is thus a subset of the product~$\mathcal{L}^L \subseteq (\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^L$, and its closure $\overline{\Gamma}$ is contained in~$\mathcal{L}^L$.
  The elements $\xi\in\overline{\Gamma}$ are called (real-valued) \emph{deep computations}, and the elements of the closure $\overline{\Delta}$ of a set $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ are called the deep computations of~$\Delta$.%
  \footnote{In the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}, the deep computations of~$\Delta$ are the accumulation points of~$\Delta$ that satisfy a confinement condition.
    Counting the members of~$\Delta$ among them affects none of the properties studied in this paper: under the Extendibility Axiom of Section~\ref{sec:computability} the members of~$\Delta$ are continuous on the type shards, and those that are not accumulation points of~$\Delta$ are isolated points of~$\overline{\Delta}$.}

  A collection $\mathscr{R}$ of sizers is \emph{exhaustive} if $L = \bigcup_{\rb\in \mathscr{R}}L[\rb]$.
  A computation $\gamma\in\Gamma$ is \emph{$\mathscr{R}$-confined} if it maps $L[r_\bullet]$ into itself for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$, and a subset $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is \emph{$\mathscr{R}$-confined} if each $\gamma\in \Delta$ is.
\end{defn}

Unlike its subspace $L^L$, which is a semigroup under composition, the space $\mathcal{L}^L$ has no natural semigroup structure;
deep computations are therefore not composable in general.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:confined-compact}
  If $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is confined by an exhaustive collection of sizers, then its closure $\overline{\Delta}$ is a compact subset of~$\Lshard^L$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
  Let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers that confines~$\Delta$, and choose, for each $v\in L$, a sizer $\rb^{(v)}\in \mathscr{R}$ with $v\in L[\rb^{(v)}]$.
  Since every $\gamma\in\Delta$ maps $v$ into $L[\rb^{(v)}]$, the set $\Delta$ lies in the compact space $\prod_{v\in L}\mathcal{L}[\rb^{(v)}]\subseteq\Lshard^L$, which therefore contains~$\overline{\Delta}$.
\end{proof}

For a CCS $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$, we regard the elements of $\Gamma$ as “standard” finitary computations, and the elements of $\overline{\Gamma}$, i.e., deep computations, as possibly infinitary limits of standard computations.

\begin{rems}(For readers versed in model theory.)%
  \footnote{The real-valued structures used here are those of Keisler’s model theory for real-valued structures~\cite{Keisler:2023}; see also~\cite[Section~6]{Duenez-Iovino:2017} for an introduction.}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    Compositional computation structures, as defined above, are not literally real-valued structures, because nothing in the structure itself forces $\Gamma$ to be realized as a subset of the product space $L^L$.
    However, as in their original definition~\cite{alva2024approximability}, they may be regarded as real-valued structures $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma,\circ,\operatorname{ev})$, where
    \begin{itemize}
    \item
      $(L,\mathcal{P})$ is a computation states structure,
    \item
      $(\Gamma,\circ)$ is a semigroup,
    \item
      $\operatorname{ev}: \Gamma\times L\to L: (\gamma,v)\mapsto \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,v)$ is a semigroup action of $\Gamma$ on~$L$
      (i.e., $\operatorname{ev}(\gamma\circ\delta,v) = \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,\operatorname{ev}(\delta,v))$ for $\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma$ and $v\in L$).
    \end{itemize}
    The two definitions are reconciled by identifying each $\gamma\in \Gamma$ with the map $v\mapsto \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,v)$.

  \item
    Regarded as real-valued structures in this sense, the compositional computation structures with a fixed collection~$\mathcal{P}$ of predicate symbols form an elementary class.
    The notion of state type introduced above (essentially, that of a quantifier-free type with parameters of sort $L$ only)
    may be enriched by further formulas, for instance with parameters in~$\Gamma$ or with quantifiers,
    and extended to types of sort~$\Gamma$ (“transform-types”).
    As usual, all types over a structure $\underline{C}=(L,\mathcal{P}, \Gamma, \circ, \operatorname{ev})$
    are realized in some ultrapower~$\underline{C}^{(\mathcal{U})}$ of~$\underline{C}$, constructed as in the references above.%
    \footnote{By contrast, no ultrapower is needed to realize state types in a computation states structure (see Remark~\ref{rem:poor-mans-ultraprod}).}
    In Banach space theory, this approach to spaces of types goes back to Krivine's real-valued languages~\cite{Krivine:1972,Krivine:1974} and to the work of Krivine and Maurey on stable Banach spaces~\cite{Krivine-Maurey:1981}.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{rems}

\subsection{Computability of deep computations and the Extendibility Axiom}
\label{sec:computability}
The computability of transforms and the Extendibility Axiom, which requires every computation to have an extension that is continuous on each type shard, are the two notions on which the rest of the paper depends, and we recall them in this subsection.
A machine evaluates only sums and products of finitely many observed features, and only to within a preset error on a preset range of magnitudes;
for this reason, computability is defined by uniform approximation on shards by polynomials in the features.
Let $(L,\mathcal{P})$ be a computation states structure.
A map $f\colon L\to\mathcal{L}$ from its state space to its space of types is called a \emph{transform}.
In the two definitions that follow, and in the characterization of computability after them, the collection~$\mathcal{P}$ of predicates is assumed to be \emph{countable}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  A \emph{computable predicate} is a function from~$L$ to~$\mathbb{R}$ whose restriction to each shard is a uniform limit of polynomials, with real coefficients, in the features~$P(\cdot)$.
\item
  A transform $f\colon L\to \mathcal{L}$ is \emph{computable} if, for each $Q\in\mathcal{P}$, the output feature $Q{\circ}f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$ is a computable predicate.
\end{enumerate}

Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton~\cite{alva2024approximability} showed that a transform $f\colon L\to\mathcal{L}$ is computable if and only if it extends to a continuous map $\tilde{f}\colon \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{L}$.
This characterization motivates the following definition, in which continuity is required on each type shard.

A computation $\gamma\in \Gamma$ is \emph{extendable} if it has an extension $\tilde\gamma\colon\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\to\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ such that,
for every sizer $r_{\bullet}$, there is a sizer $s_\bullet$ for which the restriction $\tilde\gamma|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}\colon\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\to\mathcal{L}[s_\bullet]$ is continuous.
Such an extension is unique, since it is continuous on each type shard $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$, in which $L[\rb]$ is dense.
For a set $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ of extendable computations, we write $\tilde{\Delta}$ for the set $\{\tilde\gamma\colon\gamma\in\Delta\}$.

\begin{AxExt}
  \label{ax:extendibility-axiom}
  Every computation $\gamma\in\Gamma$ is extendable.
\end{AxExt}

The Extendibility Axiom is discussed at length in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.
\emph{From now on, all compositional computation structures are assumed to satisfy the Extendibility Axiom.}

The following lemma extends deep computations to the type shards;
it is used in Theorems~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} and~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:extension}
  Let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $\mathscr{R}$-confined.
  Every deep computation $f\in\overline{\Delta}$ has an extension $\tilde f:\Lshard\to\Lshard$ that, for every $\rb\in \mathscr{R}$, maps $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ into itself and satisfies $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}\in\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}}$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  Write $f=\lim_{i,\mathcal{U}}\gamma_i$, where $(\gamma_i)_{i\in I}$ is a family in~$\Delta$ and $\mathcal{U}$ is an ultrafilter on the index set~$I$, and let $\rb\in \mathscr{R}$ be given.
  Each $\tilde\gamma_i$ maps $L[\rb]$ into itself and is continuous on $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$, so it maps the closure $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ of $L[\rb]$ into itself;
  since $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ is compact, the limit $\tilde f(v)\coloneqq\lim_{i,\mathcal{U}}\tilde\gamma_i(v)$ exists for every $v\in\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ and lies in $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$, and $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}=\lim_{i,\mathcal{U}}\tilde\gamma_i|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}$.
  Since $\mathscr{R}$ is exhaustive, this defines $\tilde f$ on a set that contains~$L$, and there $\tilde f$ agrees with~$f$;
  at the remaining points of $\Lshard$ we let $\tilde f$ be the identity.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:extension} connects deep computations with the second notion of limit introduced in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.
There, an \emph{ultracomputation} of a set $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ of extendable computations is an accumulation point of~$\tilde\Delta$, subject to a confinement condition, in the space of maps from the space of types to itself with the topology of pointwise convergence;
thus a deep computation sends states to types, whereas an ultracomputation sends types to types.
Every deep computation is the restriction to~$L$ of an ultracomputation~\cite{alva2024approximability}, and on the type shards $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ with $\rb\in\mathscr{R}$ the ultralimit $\lim_{i,\mathcal{U}}\tilde\gamma_i$ in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension} provides such an extension.
The members of the sets $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}}$, on which the classification of Section~\ref{sec:Classification} is based, are the restrictions to $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ of such ultralimits of extended computations;
strictly speaking, the classification therefore concerns the restrictions of ultracomputations to type shards.
For the dynamical compositional computation structures of Section~\ref{sec:dynamical-CCS}, in which every type is realized and every computation is its own extension, deep computations and ultracomputations coincide.

\subsection{Stability}
\label{sec:stable}
The highest level of the hierarchy studied in this paper is occupied by the stable sets of computations, all of whose deep computations are computable, and we recall them before descending, in Section~\ref{sec:Classification}, to the levels below.
By the theorem of Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton recalled above, the computable transforms are exactly those that extend to continuous maps on the space of types, and accordingly the stable sets are defined by the continuity of their deep computations on every type shard.

\begin{defn}\label{def:stable}
  A set $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ of computations is \emph{stable} if, for every sizer $r_\bullet$, every deep computation in $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is continuous, that is, computable.
\end{defn}

Stable sets of computations are the subject of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}, where they are characterized by Grothendieck’s double limit criterion and identified with stability in the sense of model theory.

\subsection{Two motivating examples}
\label{sec:preview}
Before developing the classification, we describe two algorithms whose deep computations differ sharply in character, for the contrast between them indicates what the classification must distinguish;
both are treated in detail in Section~\ref{sec:examples}.

\emph{Square roots.}
Newton’s method for $p(x)=x^2-a$, with $a>0$, acts on the real projective line $\RP^1=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ by the map $N_p(x)=(x^2+a)/(2x)$, with $N_p(0)=N_p(\infty)=\infty$.
Its iterates are continuous, and they converge pointwise to the map $\mathfrak f$ that sends every positive number to $\sqrt a$, every negative number to $-\sqrt a$, and both $0$ and $\infty$ to~$\infty$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:sqrt}).
The deep computations are the iterates together with~$\mathfrak f$.
Since $\mathfrak f$ is discontinuous, it is not computable, and the set of iterates is not stable;
nevertheless, the map $\mathfrak f$ is the limit of a \emph{sequence} of computations, and the space of deep computations is countable.

\emph{Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere.}
For $q(z)=z^3-2z+2$, Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere $\hatC=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ displays three distinct modes of behavior, each confined to its own part of the sphere (see Figure~\ref{fig:newton} in Section~\ref{sec:Newton-cubic}).
On the basins of the three roots the iterates converge;
on the basin of the cycle $0\mapsto1\mapsto0$ they do not, although the even iterates and the odd iterates converge there separately, to different limits;
and on the common boundary of these basins, a fractal set, the iterates are chaotic.
For the simpler polynomial $z^2-1$, chaos of this kind produces deep computations that are not even limits of sequences of computations (see Proposition~\ref{prop:Newton-wild} and Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).

The classification of Section~\ref{sec:Classification} is designed to separate such behaviors.
In Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} below we characterize the sets of computations whose deep computations are of the first Baire class on every type shard;
for them, as in the case of square roots, every deep computation is, on each shard, the limit of a sequence of computations.
Todorčević’s trichotomy, which we recall in Section~\ref{sec:trichotomy}, then measures the complexity of the space of such deep computations.

    The continuous maps $N_p^n$ converge pointwise to the deep computation $\mathfrak f$, which sends the positive half line to $\sqrt a$, the negative half line to $-\sqrt a$, and $0$ and $\infty$ to~$\infty$.}

\section{Classifying deep computations}
\label{sec:Classification}

The main theorems of the paper, which classify sets of computations by the complexity of their deep computations and thereby yield a new hierarchy of complexity classes, are proved in this section by means of the topology of function spaces.
The examples of Section~\ref{sec:preview} call for such a classification, since some natural algorithms have discontinuous deep computations that are nevertheless limits of sequences of computations, while others have deep computations that are not even limits of sequences.
The main topological tool is Todorčević’s trichotomy for Rosenthal compacta~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}, and our model is the work of Glasner and Megrelishvili on tame semigroups in topological dynamics~\cite{glasner2022tame}.

\subsection{NIP, Rosenthal compacta, and deep computations}

The classification rests on Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, which we prove in this subsection and which characterizes, by a finitary condition, the sets of computations whose deep computations are of the first Baire class on every type shard, just as the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability} characterized, by Grothendieck’s double limit criterion, those whose deep computations are continuous.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:nip-baire1def}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a compositional computation structure (see Definition~\ref{def:CCS}) satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, with $\mathcal{P}$ countable.
  Let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers,
  and suppose that $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is $\mathscr{R}$-confined.
  Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$;
  \item
    $\pi_P\circ \Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ satisfies the NIP for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$, where $\pi_P\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the projection onto the coordinate~$P$;
    that is, for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$, $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$, reals $a<b$ and sequences $(\gamma_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in~$\Delta$, there are finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that
    \[
      L[r_\bullet]\cap\bigcap_{n\in I}(\pi_P\circ\gamma_n)^{-1}(-\infty,a]\cap\bigcap_{n\in J}(\pi_P\circ\gamma_n)^{-1}[b,\infty)=\emptyset.
    \]
  \end{enumerate}

  Furthermore, if these conditions hold, then every deep computation $f\in\overline{\Delta}$ has an extension $\tilde f\colon\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\to \mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ such that $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}\in \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_{\bullet}\in \mathscr{R}$.
  In particular, on each shard, every deep computation is the pointwise limit of a sequence of computations.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\mathcal{P}$ is countable, each type shard $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ is a compact metrizable, hence Polish, subspace of~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$.
  Let $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$ be given.
  By the Extendibility Axiom and confinement, every $\tilde\gamma$ with $\gamma\in\Delta$ maps $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ continuously into itself (see the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension}), and hence $\pi_P\circ \tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  By Theorem~\ref{thm:Generalized-BFT} and Lemma~\ref{lem:NIP-and-closure}, condition~(ii) holds at $r_\bullet$ if and only if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq\mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})$, which (i) implies.
  Conversely, suppose that this inclusion holds.
  For a Polish space $X$, every compact subset of $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ is Fréchet--Urysohn, that is, every point in the closure of a subset is the limit of a sequence of points of the subset
  (see~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire} or~\cite[Theorem~4.1]{debs2013rosenthal}).
  Applied to $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$, regarded by Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications} as a compact subset of $\mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\times\mathcal{P})$, where $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\times\mathcal{P}$ is Polish because $\mathcal{P}$ is countable and discrete, this shows that every member of $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is the pointwise limit of a sequence of maps $\tilde\gamma_n|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ with $\gamma_n\in\Delta$, which are continuous maps from $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ to itself;
  hence (i) holds at~$r_\bullet$.
  Finally, if (i) holds, the extension $\tilde f$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension} satisfies
  $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}\in\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$, and, by the argument just given, every deep computation is, on each shard, the pointwise limit of a sequence of computations.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Todorčević’s trichotomy and levels of NIP}
\label{sec:trichotomy}
Todorčević’s trichotomy now enables us to stratify the NIP sets of computations into three levels.
The trichotomy concerns separable Rosenthal compacta, and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} and under its hypotheses, the deep computations of a countable $\NIP$ set of computations form such a compactum on each type shard.
We measure the complexity of such a compactum by three topological properties, metrizability, hereditary separability and first countability, which form a chain:
every compact metrizable space is hereditarily separable, and R.~Pol proved that every hereditarily separable Rosenthal compactum is first countable (see~\cite[Section~10]{debs2013rosenthal}).
These properties define three levels of NIP.

\begin{defn}\label{def:NIP-levels}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, with $\mathcal{P}$ countable, and let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers.
  Let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be an $\mathscr{R}$-confined countable set of computations satisfying the NIP on shards and features (condition (ii) in Theorem \ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
  We say that $\Delta$ is:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\NIP_1$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is first countable for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$;
  \item
    $\NIP_2$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is hereditarily separable for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$;
  \item
    $\NIP_3$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is metrizable for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

By the chain of properties above, the levels satisfy $\NIP_3 \Rightarrow \NIP_2\Rightarrow \NIP_1\Rightarrow \NIP$.
Moreover, a countable stable set of computations (see Definition~\ref{def:stable}), which has the NIP by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, is $\NIP_3$:
on each type shard its deep computations form a separable compact subset of a countable product of spaces $\Cp(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$, that is, a separable Eberlein compactum, and separable Eberlein compacta are metrizable~\cite{Arkhangelskii:1992}.
The five compacta of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} show that none of the implications between the three topological properties can be reversed for separable Rosenthal compacta:
the extended Alexandroff compactification $\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is separable but not first countable;
the extended duplicate $\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ of the split Cantor set and the compactum $\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ that contains the Alexandroff duplicate are separable and first countable but not hereditarily separable;
and the split Cantor set $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is hereditarily separable but not metrizable.
Todorčević proved that these examples are canonical~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}, in the following sense.

\begin{thm}[Todorčević’s trichotomy~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}; see also {\cite[Theorem~3]{argyros2008rosenthal}}]\label{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}
  Let $K$ be a separable Rosenthal compactum.
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    If $K$ is hereditarily separable but non-metrizable, then the split Cantor set $S(2^\mathbb{N})$ embeds into $K$.
  \item
    If $K$ is first countable but not hereditarily separable, then either the Alexandroff duplicate $D(2^\mathbb{N})$ or the extended duplicate $\hat{D}(S(2^\mathbb{N}))$ of the split Cantor set embeds into $K$.
  \item
    If $K$ is not first countable, then the Alexandroff compactification $A(2^\mathbb{N})$ embeds into $K$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

Together with the chain of properties above, the trichotomy yields the following classification of separable Rosenthal compacta:

The three levels admit a second description, in terms of the number of tests required to distinguish deep computations from one another.
Let $\Delta$ and $\mathscr{R}$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}, fix $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$, and write $K_{r_\bullet}=\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$.
We identify each $f\in K_{r_\bullet}$ with the real-valued function $(x,P)\mapsto P(f(x))$ on $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\times\mathcal{P}$, whose points we call \emph{tests}:
the test $(x,P)$ applies the feature $P$ to the output on the input~$x$.
The topology of pointwise convergence on $K_{r_\bullet}$ is then the topology of pointwise convergence on tests.
A set $\mathcal{T}$ of tests \emph{separates} a set of deep computations if any two distinct members of the set differ on some test in~$\mathcal{T}$.
A member $f$ of $K_{r_\bullet}$ is \emph{countably determined} if some countable set $\mathcal{T}_f$ of tests separates $f$ from every other member of $K_{r_\bullet}$, and a set $E\subseteq K_{r_\bullet}$ is \emph{finitely separated} if, for each $f\in E$, there are finitely many tests and a real number $\varepsilon_f>0$ such that every $g\in E\setminus\{f\}$ differs from $f$ by at least $\varepsilon_f$ on one of these tests.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:levels-tests}
  Let $\Delta$ and $\mathscr{R}$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}, let $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$ be given, and let $K_{r_\bullet}=\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$.
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    The compactum $K_{r_\bullet}$ is metrizable if and only if one countable set of tests separates $K_{r_\bullet}$.
  \item
    It is first countable if and only if every member of $K_{r_\bullet}$ is countably determined.
  \item
    It is hereditarily separable if and only if no finitely separated subset of $K_{r_\bullet}$, that is, no subset that is discrete in the topology of pointwise convergence on tests, has cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  (i) If a countable set $\mathcal{T}$ of tests separates $K_{r_\bullet}$, restriction to $\mathcal{T}$ is a continuous injection of the compact space $K_{r_\bullet}$ into the metrizable space $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}$, hence a homeomorphism onto its image.
  Conversely, if $K_{r_\bullet}$ is metrizable, then the square $K_{r_\bullet}\times K_{r_\bullet}$ is second countable, so the open set $\{(f,g):f\ne g\}$ is Lindelöf;
  it is covered by the open sets $\{(f,g):f(t)\ne g(t)\}$, for tests $t$, and a countable subcover gives the required set $\mathcal{T}$.
  (ii) If $f$ is countably determined, the singleton $\{f\}$ is a countable intersection of open sets, and in a compact Hausdorff space such a point has a countable base of neighborhoods.
  Conversely, let $(U_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a countable base of neighborhoods of $f$, and choose in each $U_k$ a basic neighborhood of $f$ defined by a finite set $F_k$ of tests;
  then $\mathcal{T}_f=\bigcup_kF_k$ separates $f$ from every other member of $K_{r_\bullet}$.
  (iii) A set is finitely separated exactly when it is a discrete subspace, and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, the compactum $K_{r_\bullet}$ is a separable Rosenthal compactum.
  A hereditarily separable space has no uncountable discrete subspace, since a separable discrete space is countable.
  Conversely, if $K_{r_\bullet}$ is not hereditarily separable, then, by Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}, it contains a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, of $\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ or of $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, and each of these contains a discrete subspace of cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta});
  for separable Rosenthal compacta, the existence of such a subspace in the absence of hereditary separability is due to R.~Pol (see~\cite{argyros2008rosenthal} and~\cite[Theorem~0.4]{glasner2022tame}).
\end{proof}

Thus the set $\Delta$ is $\NIP_3$ when, on each type shard, a single countable set of tests separates all its deep computations; it is $\NIP_2$ when no type shard carries a finitely separated set of deep computations of the cardinality of the continuum; and it is $\NIP_1$ when, on each type shard, each deep computation is separated from all the others by countably many tests of its own.

In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we exhibit computations at each level of the hierarchy and show that every implication between the classes stable, $\NIP_3$, $\NIP_2$, $\NIP_1$ and $\NIP$ is strict.
The definitions of $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_3$ are topological, and the descriptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests} still involve infinitely many tests;
whether the three levels admit a finitary, combinatorial characterization, like that of $\NIP$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, is Question~\ref{q:NIP-levels}.

\subsection{The Argyros--Dodos--Kanellopoulos heptachotomy and binary search}
\label{sec:heptachotomy}

{\tolerance=2000 % local: avoids an overfull line after the run-in heading
For families of computations indexed by the binary tree, the Argyros--Dodos--Kanellopoulos heptachotomy describes their closures more finely than Todorčević’s trichotomy, which concerns the closures of arbitrary countable sets of computations, and we refine the classification accordingly, reading the minimal families of the heptachotomy as primitives of binary search.
The countable dense subsets exhibited in the separable compacta of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} are indexed by the binary tree $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, and this indexing suits our computational emphasis:
real numbers are represented by infinite binary sequences, which are the branches of the binary tree, and approximated by finite bitstrings, which are its nodes.
Recall from Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} that a dyadic subtree of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is written $\{w_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, indexed through the canonical isomorphism, which preserves extension and the lexicographic order.\par}

\begin{defn}
  Let $X$ be a Polish space.

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    If $I$ is countable and $\{f_i:i\in I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$, $\{g_i:i\in I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$ are two families indexed by $I$, we say that they are \emph{equivalent} if the map $f_i\mapsto g_i$ extends to a homeomorphism from $\overline{\{f_i:i\in I\}}$ to $\overline{\{g_i:i\in I\}}$.
  \item
    If $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is a pointwise bounded family, we say that it is \emph{minimal} if, for every dyadic subtree $\{w_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, the family $\{f_{w_t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos proved that, up to equivalence, there are exactly seven minimal families $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ that are relatively compact in~$\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, and that every relatively compact family $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X)$ contains, along a dyadic subtree, a family equivalent to one of them (see Theorem~\ref{thm:heptachotomy} below).
We describe the seven minimal families next, in the notation of Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos, along the regular dyadic subtree $\{s_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ fixed in Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} for the definition of the extended duplicate of the split Cantor set, for which $s_t^\frown 0^{\infty}\neq s_{t'}^\frown 0^\infty$ and $s_t^\frown 1^{\infty}\neq s_{t'}^\frown 1^\infty$ whenever $t\neq t'$.
The families are built from the prefix tests $v_t$ and the threshold tests $\chi_{[a}$, $\chi_{(a}$ of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}.
As in~\cite{argyros2008rosenthal}, a point $\sigma\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$, identified with a point of the Cantor set in~$[0,1]$, is regarded as the constant function on~$2^{\mathbb{N}}$ with value~$\sigma$, and a pair $(f,g)$ of functions on $2^{\mathbb{N}}$ is regarded as a single function on the disjoint union $2^{\mathbb{N}}\sqcup2^{\mathbb{N}}$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  $D_1=\{\frac{1}{|t|+1}v_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  Its closure is $D_1\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}$, the one-point compactification of a countable discrete set.
\item
  $D_2=\{s_t^\frown 0^{\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_2}$, which Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos denote by $2^{\leq\mathbb{N}}$;
  this closure is compact and metrizable, and its accumulation points form a copy of~$2^{\mathbb{N}}$.
\item
  $D_3=\{\chi_{[s_t^\frown 0^\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_3}$, a closed subspace of~$S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_4=\{\chi_{(s_t^\frown 1^\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_4}$, a closed subspace of~$S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_5=\{v_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_5}=\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_6=\{(v_t,t^\frown 0^\infty):t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_6}=\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_7=\{(v_{s_t},\chi_{[s_t^\frown 0^\infty}):t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, a discrete subset of its closure $\overline{D_7}=\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{thm}[Heptachotomy of minimal families {\cite[Theorem~2]{argyros2008rosenthal}}]
  \label{thm:heptachotomy}
  Let $X$ be a Polish space.
  For every relatively compact family $\{f_{t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X)$, there exist $i\in\{1,2,\dots,7\}$ and a regular dyadic subtree $\{w_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$
  such that $\{f_{w_t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to $D_i$.
  Moreover, the families $D_i$ are minimal and pairwise non-equivalent.
\end{thm}

For deep computations, the building blocks of the families $D_1,\dots,D_7$ have a direct reading as primitives of binary search on $2^{\mathbb{N}}$, the space of binary expansions.
A node $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ determines the dyadic interval $[t^\frown0^\infty,t^\frown1^\infty]$, the set of inputs that round to $t$ at precision $|t|$.
The prefix test $v_t$ asks whether the input rounds to $t$;
the point $t^\frown0^\infty$ is the rounded value itself;
the comparisons $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$ and $\chi_{(t^\frown1^\infty}$ ask whether the input lies at or to the right of the left endpoint of the interval, or strictly to the right of its right endpoint.
In Table~\ref{tab:hepta-search} we read the seven families accordingly.

\section{Examples of  Compositional Computation Structures}
\label{sec:examples}
From the general theory we turn to the examples that motivate it, among them the two announced in Section~\ref{sec:preview}, and describe the deep computations of three compositional computation structures, namely Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere for two cubic polynomials (Sections~\ref{sec:Newton-cubic} and~\ref{sec:Newton-cyclotomic}) and Newton’s method for square roots on the real projective line (Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}).
In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we place the last of these, together with other classical algorithms, at the levels of the hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:Classification}.

We begin by formalizing the Newton--Raphson root-finding method as a CCS~\cite{Galantai200025}, \cite[Section~2.3]{Burden2016numerical}.
Let $\hatC = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ be the extended complex plane, that is, the Alexandroff (one-point) compactification of~$\mathbb{C}$.
We identify $\hatC$ with the unit sphere $S^2\subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$, the Riemann sphere, through the inverse stereographic projection, the homeomorphism $\hatC\to S^2$ given by $z \mapsto (P_1(z), P_2(z), P_3(z))$, where $(P_1(\infty), P_2(\infty), P_3(\infty)) = (0,0,1)$ and, for $z\ne\infty$,
\begin{align*}
P_1(z) & = \frac{2\,\Re{z}}{|z|^2+1}, &
P_2(z) & = \frac{2\,\Im{z}}{|z|^2+1}, &
P_3(z) & = \frac{|z|^2-1}{|z|^2+1}.
\end{align*}
The extended complex plane $\hatC$ thus carries the real predicates $P_1, P_2, P_3$, with values in~$[-1,1]$, and its topology is metrized by the pullback of the Euclidean metric of~$S^2$.

\subsubsection*{Newton  CCSs:  steps and fractals}
\label{sec:Newton-map}
Here we describe the compositional computation structure generated by the Newton map, the single step that Newton's method iterates to approximate a root of a function, and recall the Fatou and Julia sets, on which the iterates of this map behave regularly and chaotically, respectively.
The structure so obtained is the common setting of the three examples that follow.
Let $p = p(z)$ be a nonconstant rational function with complex coefficients, that is, a nonconstant meromorphic map $\hatC\to\hatC$.
The \emph{Newton step}, or \emph{Newton map}, of $p$ is the rational map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Np}
N_p: z \mapsto z-\frac{p(z)}{p'(z)}.
\end{equation}
The iterates of the Newton map, that is, its compositional powers~$N_p^n$, may be regarded as the computations of a CCS with state space $L = \hatC$.
The natural predicates are the coordinates $P_1, P_2, P_3$ of the inverse stereographic projection, since the real and imaginary parts $z\mapsto\Re{z}$ and $z\mapsto\Im{z}$ are not defined at~$z = \infty$.

Let $\Gamma_p\coloneqq\{N_p^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ be the semigroup of iterates of the Newton map~\eqref{eq:Np}.
The CCS $(\hatC, (P_1,P_2,P_3), \Gamma_p)$ is called \emph{Newton’s method for~$p(z)$}, or the \emph{Newton CCS for~$p(z)$}.
Since the state space $\hatC$ is compact and the Newton map $N_p$ is continuous on it, every Newton CCS satisfies the Extendibility Axiom.

Newton’s method succeeds at an initial estimate $z_0\in\hatC$ when the sequence $(z_n)_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$ of Newton steps, where $z_n \coloneqq N_p^n(z_0)$, converges to a root of~$p$.

\paragraph{Fatou and Julia sets}
Recall that the \emph{Fatou set $\mathcal{F}_g$} of a rational map~$g\colon\hatC\to\hatC$ consists of the points that have a neighborhood on which every sequence of iterates of~$g$ has a subsequence that converges uniformly with respect to the spherical metric;
its complement $\mathcal{J}_g\coloneqq\hatC\setminus \mathcal{F}_g$ is the \emph{Julia set} of $g$, a closed set that is typically a fractal.
The Julia set of the Newton map~$N_p$ is called the \emph{Newton fractal} of~$p$.

\subsection{Example: The Newton CCS for the cubic $q(z) = z^3-2z+2$}
\label{sec:Newton-cubic}
Our first example is the second motivating example of Section~\ref{sec:preview}, a cubic polynomial whose Newton map has, besides the three roots, an attracting cycle.
Newton's method therefore fails on an open set of initial estimates, its iterates do not converge, and among its deep computations are two distinct limits, one of the even iterates and one of the odd iterates.
The polynomial $q(z) = z^3-2z+2$ has Newton map
\begin{equation*}
N_q: z \mapsto z - \frac{z^3-2z+2}{3z^2-2} = \frac{2z^3-2}{3z^2-2}.
\end{equation*}
Denote the real root of $q$ by $r_0$, and the roots in the upper and the lower half plane by $r_{+}$ and $r_{-}$.
Starting from $z_0=0$, the Newton steps alternate $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$, and neither $0$ nor $1$ is a root of~$q$.

Let $B_0, B_+, B_-$ be the basins of the roots $r_0, r_+, r_-$, and let $B_{01}$ be the basin of the superattracting cycle $\{0,1\}$.
The Fatou set of $N_q$ is $B_{01} \cup B_0 \cup B_{+} \cup B_{-}$, and each of these four sets is nonempty and open.
Since $B_{01}$ is nonempty, the iterates $N_q^n$ do not converge pointwise.
Fix nonprincipal ultrafilters $\mathcal{U}_0$ and $\mathcal{U}_1$ on~$\mathbb{N}$ that contain the even and the odd numbers, respectively, and let $f_0$ and $f_1$ be the deep computations obtained as the limits of the iterates $N_q^n$ along~$\mathcal{U}_0$ and along~$\mathcal{U}_1$.
On the Fatou set the even and the odd iterates converge pointwise, and there the two limits do not depend on the choice of the ultrafilters:
both send each basin of a root to that root, while on~$B_{01}$ they take only the values $0$ and~$1$ and exchange them, because $f_1=N_q\circ f_0$ on the Fatou set by the continuity of~$N_q$.
In particular, the two deep computations are distinct.%
\footnote{The point $\infty$ is a repelling fixed point of~$N_q$: if $|z|$ is large, then $N_q(z) = \frac{2}{3}z + O(1)$.
  Hence $\infty$ lies on the Newton fractal of~$q$, and $f_0(\infty) = \infty = f_1(\infty)$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:newton} depicts $f_0$ and $f_1$, together with the Newton fractal of~$q$, on which the iterates behave chaotically.

\subsection{Example: The Newton CCS for the cubic $u(z) = z^{3}-1$}
\label{sec:Newton-cyclotomic}
For contrast, we turn to $u(z) = z^3-1$, whose roots are the cube roots of unity $r_0=1$ and $r_{\pm} = \exp(\pm2\pi i/3)$ and whose Newton map is
\begin{equation*}
N_u: z\mapsto z-\frac{z^3-1}{3z^2}=\frac{2z^3+1}{3z^2}.
\end{equation*}
Unlike $N_q$, the map $N_u$ has no attracting cycle other than its three fixed points, the roots:
the Fatou set is the union of the three basins, and on each basin the iterates converge to the corresponding root.
Figure~\ref{fig:newton2} depicts Newton’s method for~$u$;
to render the iterates themselves, and not merely their limits, we color each point $z_0$ by blending the colors of the three roots with relative weights $|z_n-r|^{-3}$, for $r\in\{r_0,r_+,r_-\}$, where $z_n\coloneqq N_u^n(z_0)$.
This coloring is a continuous function of~$z_0$, blurred for small~$n$;
as $n$ increases, it sharpens into large regions of nearly constant color, separated by narrow regions where the color varies rapidly, that is, where the coloring is nearly discontinuous.

\subsection{Example: Square roots}\label{exa:square-roots-compute}
We return to the first motivating example of Section~\ref{sec:preview}, Newton’s method on the real projective line for the polynomial $p(x) = x^2-a$ with real $a>0$, whose iterates approximate the square roots $\pm\sqrt a$, and work out its deep computations, which place it at the level $\NIP_3$ (see Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP3}).
As for every rational function with real coefficients, the Newton map $N_p$ maps the real projective line $\RP^1 = \mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ into itself, and the Newton CCS for $p$ may be regarded as having state space~$\RP^1$.

Let $\mathbb{R}\to S^1$, $x\mapsto (P_1(x),P_3(x))$, be the inverse stereographic projection of the real line onto $S^1\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, given by
\begin{align*}
P_1(x) & = \frac{2x}{x^2+1},    &
P_3(x) & = \frac{x^2-1}{x^2+1},
\end{align*}
and extended to a homeomorphism of the real projective line $\RP^1 = \mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ onto~$S^1$ by $\infty \mapsto (P_1(\infty), P_3(\infty)) = (0, 1)$;
these predicates are the restrictions to $\RP^1$ of the predicates $P_1$ and $P_3$ of the Riemann sphere, whose names they keep, and they make $(\RP^1, (P_1, P_3))$ a computation states structure with a compact state space.
The Newton map $N_p$ is
\begin{equation*}
x \mapsto x - \frac{x^2-a}{2x}
=
\begin{cases}
\frac{x^2+a}{2x}, & x\ne0,\infty; \\
\infty,           & x=0, \infty.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
From every initial point $x_0\ne0,\infty$, the Newton steps converge to one of the square roots $\pm\sqrt{a}$;
more precisely, the iterates of $N_p$ converge pointwise to the map $\mathfrak{f}\colon \RP^1\to\RP^1$ given by
\[
  \mathfrak{f}(x)
  =
  \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}N_p^n(x)
  =
  \begin{cases}
  \sqrt{a},  & x\in(0,\infty); \\
  -\sqrt{a}, & x\in(-\infty,0); \\
  \infty,    & x = 0, \infty.
  \end{cases}
\]
The map $\mathfrak{f}$ is therefore discontinuous at $0$ and at~$\infty$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:sqrt}).

\section{Computations across the hierarchy}
\label{sec:iterative}

The hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:Classification} descends from the stable sets of computations through the classes $\NIP_3$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_1$ to the class $\NIP$;
its implications hold for countable sets of computations in compositional computation structures with countably many features, and its levels are defined through the topology of the space of deep computations.
Each of these levels is nevertheless attained by classical algorithms, and some familiar algorithms lie beyond the hierarchy.
In this section we establish both facts, and with them the strictness of every implication.
In each example the computations are compositions of the steps of an algorithm.
At all levels but one they are the powers of a single step map; at the level $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$ they form a group generated by two steps, both in the Euclidean algorithm and in free reduction in the free group on two generators, and a countable family of elements of the first of these groups provides a second example at the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
The level of each algorithm is then a theorem about the closure of its computations, and for such computations the hierarchy coincides with the Glasner--Megrelishvili hierarchy of tame dynamical systems~\cite{glasner2022tame}.
We begin with this coincidence (see Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame});
Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy} at the end of the section summarizes the examples.

\subsection{Iterative algorithms and tame dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamical-CCS}
The algorithms of this section act by continuous steps on a compact space of states, and within this setting, which we now isolate, our hierarchy coincides with the Glasner--Megrelishvili hierarchy of tame dynamical systems;
the coincidence allows their results to be brought to bear on the examples that follow.
A \emph{dynamical CCS} is a compositional computation structure $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ in which $L$ is a compact metrizable space, $\mathcal{P}$ is a countable family of continuous predicates that separates the points of~$L$, and $\Gamma$ is a countable semigroup of continuous maps $L\to L$ that contains the identity.
It is a \emph{cascade CCS} if $\Gamma=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ is the semigroup of iterates of a single continuous map $\gamma\colon L\to L$.
Since $\mathcal{P}$ separates points and $L$ is compact, the map $v\mapsto\tp(v)$ is a homeomorphism of $L$ onto the space of types;
hence $L$ is covered by a single shard, every type is realized, and the Extendibility Axiom holds with $\tilde\gamma=\gamma$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$.
The deep computations of a dynamical CCS form the pointwise closure
\[
E(L,\Gamma)=\overline{\Gamma}\subseteq L^L ,
\]
which is the \emph{enveloping semigroup}, introduced by Ellis, of the dynamical system $(L,\Gamma)$;
for a cascade CCS we write $E(L,\gamma)$.
The system $(L,\Gamma)$ is called \emph{tame} if, for every continuous function $f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$, the family $\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ has the $\NIP$ of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns}~\cite{Kohler1995,Glasner2006}.
We say that a dynamical CCS is \emph{stable}, $\NIP$, $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ or $\NIP_3$ when its set~$\Gamma$ of computations has the corresponding property of Definition~\ref{def:stable}, of condition~(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} or of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}, the last two with respect to a single sizer~$r_\bullet$ with $L[r_\bullet]=L$.

A map $\xi\colon L\to L$ is \emph{of Baire class~$1$} if $f\circ \xi$ is of
Baire class~$1$ for every continuous $f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$.
It suffices that $P\circ \xi$ be of Baire class~$1$ for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
Indeed, the type map embeds $L$ into $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$, and
$\mathcal{P}$ is countable, so every open subset of $L$ is a countable union
of finite intersections of sets $P^{-1}(U)$, with $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and
$U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ open; by Fact~\ref{baire}, the preimage of every open
set under $\xi$ is then an $F_\sigma$ set, and so is the preimage of every open
set under $f\circ \xi$, which is therefore of Baire class~$1$, again by Fact~\ref{baire}.
In general, this definition is less restrictive than the one adopted in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}, according to which a map from~$L$ to~$L$ is of Baire class~$1$ when it is the pointwise limit of a sequence of continuous maps from~$L$ to~$L$ (for example, if $L=[0,1]\cup\{2\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, the map that sends $[0,1/2]$ to~$0$ and the rest of~$L$ to~$2$ is of Baire class~$1$ in the sense just defined, but it is not the pointwise limit of a sequence of continuous maps from~$L$ to~$L$, because each such map sends the connected set $[0,1]$ either into $[0,1]$ or to the point~$2$).
For the deep computations of a dynamical CCS, however, the two definitions agree: if every deep computation is of Baire class~$1$ in the sense just defined, then the CCS is $\NIP$, as we show in Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame} below, and the last assertion of Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, applied to the single sizer~$r_\bullet$ with $L=L[r_\bullet]$, shows that every deep computation is the pointwise limit of a sequence of computations, which are continuous maps from~$L$ to~$L$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:cascade-tame}
A dynamical CCS is $\NIP$ if and only if the dynamical system $(L,\Gamma)$ is
tame, and if and only if every deep computation is of Baire class~$1$.
In that case it is $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ or $\NIP_3$ if and only if $E(L,\Gamma)$ is,
respectively, first countable, hereditarily separable or metrizable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The single sizer $r_\bullet$ with $L=L[r_\bullet]$ forms an exhaustive
collection that confines~$\Gamma$.
If $(L,\Gamma)$ is tame, the CCS is $\NIP$: take $f=P$ for $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
If the CCS is $\NIP$, then, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications}, the function $P\circ \xi$ is of Baire
class~$1$ for every $\xi\in E(L,\Gamma)$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$, so every deep
computation is of Baire class~$1$.
Finally, suppose that every deep computation is of Baire class~$1$, and let
$f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous.
The map $\xi\mapsto f\circ \xi$ is continuous on $L^L$, so the closure of
$\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ is the compact set
$\{f\circ \xi:\xi\in E(L,\Gamma)\}$, which lies in $\mathrm{B}_1(L)$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2}, the family $\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ has the
$\NIP$, so $(L,\Gamma)$ is tame.
The last statement is Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}, since
$\overline{\tilde\Gamma|_{L}}=E(L,\Gamma)$.
\end{proof}

For metrizable systems, Glasner and Megrelishvili call the tame systems whose enveloping semigroup is first countable, hereditarily separable or metrizable \emph{tame$_1$}, \emph{tame$_2$} and \emph{hereditarily non-sensitive}, respectively.
They prove the inclusions between these classes~\cite[Proposition~0.6]{glasner2022tame} and show by examples that the inclusions are proper.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, a dynamical CCS is $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ or $\NIP_3$ exactly when its dynamical system is tame$_1$, tame$_2$ or hereditarily non-sensitive, respectively.

The simplest iterative methods are those whose iterates converge pointwise.
In Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent} we show that all of them are $\NIP_3$, and that the continuity of the limit decides whether they are stable.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:convergent}
Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a cascade CCS, with $\Gamma=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ for a continuous map $\gamma\colon L\to L$, and suppose that the iterates $\gamma^n$
converge pointwise to a map $\gamma_\infty\colon L\to L$.
Then $E(L,\gamma)=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\gamma_\infty\}$, and the CCS is
$\NIP_3$.
It is stable if and only if $\gamma_\infty$ is continuous.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
A convergent sequence, together with its limit, is a compact subset of the
Hausdorff space $L^L$, hence closed; so $E(L,\gamma)$ consists of the iterates
and~$\gamma_\infty$.
For each $P\in\mathcal{P}$, the map $P\circ\gamma_\infty$ is the pointwise limit of the
continuous functions $P\circ\gamma^n$, so it is of Baire class~$1$, and, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS is $\NIP$.
The space $E(L,\gamma)$ is a countable compact Hausdorff space, hence
metrizable, so the CCS is $\NIP_3$.
Its members other than $\gamma_\infty$ are continuous, which gives the statement about
stability.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Stable and metrizable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP3}
Our descent through the hierarchy starts at its top two levels, stable and $\NIP_3$, at which the deep computations form a metrizable space;
there we place a rotation of the circle, Newton’s method for square roots, the power method and the logistic map for $0<r\le3$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent}, every convergent method lies at one of these two levels;
convergence, however, is not necessary for stability, as the first example shows.

\emph{A stable example: rotation.}
Let $L=\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, with the predicates $\cos2\pi x$ and $\sin2\pi x$, and let $\gamma(x)=x+\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is irrational.
A pointwise limit of rotations is a rotation, and the points $n\alpha$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, are dense in~$\mathbb{T}$;
hence the deep computations are all the rotations $x\mapsto x+c$, $c\in\mathbb{T}$.
They are continuous, so the rotation CCS is stable, and its space of deep computations is the circle group, a metrizable compactum.

\emph{$\NIP_3$ but not stable: square roots.}
The iterates of the Newton map $N_p$ of $p(x)=x^2-a$, with $a>0$, on the real projective line (see Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute})
converge pointwise to the map $\mathfrak f$ defined there, which is discontinuous at $0$ and $\infty$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent}, the square-root CCS is $\NIP_3$ but not
stable.

\emph{$\NIP_3$ but not stable: the power method.}
The power method computes a dominant eigenvector of a matrix by repeated multiplication and normalization.
Let $d\ge2$ be an integer, let $A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d)$, where $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\cdots>\lambda_d>0$, let $L=\RP^{d-1}$ be the real projective space of lines through the origin in~$\mathbb{R}^d$, in which $[v]$ denotes the line spanned by a nonzero vector~$v$, and let $\mathcal{P}$ consist of the predicates $P_{ij}([v])=v_iv_j/\|v\|^2$, for $1\le i,j\le d$, the entries of the orthogonal projection onto the line $[v]$.
The step of the algorithm is $\gamma([v])=[Av]$.
For $[v]\in\RP^{d-1}$, let $i(v)$ be the least index $i$ with $v_i\ne0$, and put $\ell([v])=[e_{i(v)}]$, where $e_1,\dots,e_d$ is the standard basis of~$\mathbb{R}^d$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:power}
The deep computations of the power method are
$E(L,\gamma)=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\ell\}$.
Hence the power method is $\NIP_3$.
Its deep computation $\ell$, the only one that is not an iterate, is of Baire class~$1$ and discontinuous, so the power method is not stable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For $i=i(v)$, we have $\lambda_i^{-n}A^nv=v_ie_i+\sum_{j>i}(\lambda_j/\lambda_i)^nv_je_j\to v_ie_i$,
so $\gamma^n\to\ell$ pointwise, and Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent} applies.
The map $\ell$ is discontinuous at every $[v]$ with $i(v)>1$, since $[v+te_1]\to[v]$ as $t\to0$
while $\ell([v+te_1])=[e_1]\ne\ell([v])$.
\end{proof}

The deep computation $\ell$, which sends each input to the eigenline of the
largest eigenvalue whose eigenvector has a nonzero component in the input, is
not computable in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:CCS}, since it is discontinuous
on the space of types~$L$; yet it is the limit of a sequence of
computations, and the closure in which it lives is countable and metrizable.

\emph{Both levels: the logistic map.}
The logistic map $f_r(x)=rx(1-x)$, with $0<r\le4$, maps $[0,1]$ into itself.
Its CCS has states $L=[0,1]$, the single predicate $x\mapsto x$, and the
semigroup of iterates of~$f_r$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:logistic}).
For $0<r\le1$, let $\ell_r$ be the constant map~$0$; for $1<r\le3$, let
$\ell_r(x)=1-1/r$ for $0<x<1$, and $\ell_r(0)=\ell_r(1)=0$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:logistic}
For $0<r\le3$, the iterates of $f_r$ converge pointwise to~$\ell_r$.
Consequently the logistic CCS is stable for $0<r\le1$, and $\NIP_3$ but not
stable for $1<r\le3$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Write $f=f_r$ and $x^{*}=1-1/r$, and note that $f(x)/x=r(1-x)$ for $x>0$.
If $r\le1$, then $f(x)<x$ for $0<x\le1$, so every orbit decreases to a fixed
point of~$f$, and $0$ is the only fixed point in $[0,1]$.

Suppose that $1<r\le2$.
Then $0<x^{*}\le\frac12$, and $f(x)>x$ for $0<x<x^{*}$, while $f(x)<x$ for $x^{*}<x\le1$.
For $0<x<1$ we have $f(x)\in(0,\frac r4]\subseteq(0,\frac12]$, and $f$ is
increasing on $[0,\frac12]$ and fixes~$x^{*}$.
Hence the orbit of $f(x)$ is increasing and bounded by $x^{*}$ if $f(x)<x^{*}$, and
decreasing and bounded below by $x^{*}$ if $f(x)>x^{*}$.
In both cases, and trivially if $f(x)=x^{*}$, it converges to a fixed point of $f$
in $(0,\frac12]$, that is, to~$x^{*}$.

Suppose that $2<r\le3$, and put $c=\frac r4$, the maximum of $f$, and $b=f(c)$.
We have $x^{*}<c$, since $c-x^{*}=(r-2)^2/(4r)$, and therefore $b<f(x^{*})=x^{*}$, since $f$ is strictly decreasing on $[\frac12,1]$.
We also have $b\ge1/r$, because $b-1/r=(r^3(4-r)-16)/(16r)$ and $r^3(4-r)$ increases from $16$ on $[2,3]$.
The concave function $f$ takes the value $x^{*}$ at $1/r$ and at $x^{*}$, so $f\ge x^{*}$ on $[1/r,x^{*}]$.
Hence $f$ maps $J_-=[b,x^{*}]$ into $J_+=[x^{*},c]$, and, being decreasing on $J_+$, it maps $J_+$ onto~$J_-$.
Let $0<x<1$.
The orbit of $f(x)$ lies in $(0,c]$, and it meets $J_-\cup J_+=[b,c]$:
otherwise all its points would lie in $(0,b)$, where $f(y)>y$ because $b<x^{*}$, and the orbit would increase to a fixed point of $f$ in $(0,b]$, which is impossible, since the only positive fixed point of $f$ is $x^{*}$ and $x^{*}>b$.
Since $f$ maps $J_-\cup J_+$ into itself, it remains to be shown that the orbit of every $y\in J_-\cup J_+$ converges to~$x^{*}$.
The points of period~$2$ of $f$ that are not fixed are the roots of $r^2y^2-r(r+1)y+(r+1)$, whose discriminant $r^2(r+1)(r-3)$ is negative for $r<3$; for $r=3$ the only root is~$x^{*}$.
Hence the function $f(f(y))-y$ vanishes on $[0,1]$ only at $0$ and $x^{*}$, and it is negative on $(x^{*},1]$, since it is negative at~$1$.
For $y\in J_+$ with $y>x^{*}$, the point $f(f(y))$ lies in $J_+$, so $x^{*}\le f(f(y))<y$.
The sequence $(f^{2n}(y))$ therefore decreases to a fixed point of $f\circ f$ in $[x^{*},y]$, that is, to $x^{*}$, and $f^{2n+1}(y)\to f(x^{*})=x^{*}$.
Every $y\in J_-$ satisfies $f(y)\in J_+$, so its orbit converges to $x^{*}$ as well.

Finally, $f(0)=f(1)=0$.
In all cases the iterates converge pointwise to~$\ell_r$.
The map $\ell_r$ is continuous for $r\le1$, and discontinuous at $0$ for
$r>1$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent} gives the remaining statements.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Hereditarily separable but not metrizable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP2}
Bresenham’s line algorithm in the plane, which we analyze in this subsection, lies at the level $\NIP_2$ but not $\NIP_3$, immediately below the metrizable level.
Below that level, no countable set of tests, that is, of pairs of an input and a feature (see Section~\ref{sec:trichotomy}), separates the deep
computations (see Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}).
Rounding gives rise to spaces of deep computations of this kind, since an
algorithm that rounds at each step takes a new discrete decision at each step.

\emph{Drawing a line.}
Fix an irrational number $\alpha$ with $0<\alpha<1$.
To draw the line $y=\alpha x+\rho$ on a screen, Bresenham's algorithm~\cite{Bresenham1965} moves one pixel to the right at each step and decides whether to move one pixel up.
The $n$-th decision is $b_n(\rho)=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha+\rho\rfloor-\lfloor n\alpha+\rho\rfloor=\Brack{\{n\alpha+\rho\}\in[1-\alpha,1)}$, where $\{\cdot\}$ denotes the fractional part, and the state of the algorithm is the error term $\rho$ modulo~$1$.
The step $\rho\mapsto\rho+\alpha$ is a rotation of the circle $\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, but the decision bit is not a continuous function of~$\rho$.
The standard remedy~\cite{MorseHedlund1940,Lothaire2002} is to take the decision sequences themselves as states.

Let $\mathcal{O}=\{-k\alpha:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}$, a countable dense set
containing both endpoints $0$ and $1-\alpha$ of the decision interval.
The \emph{split circle} $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$ is obtained from $\mathbb{T}$ by replacing
each $t\in \mathcal{O}$ by two points $t^-<t^+$, with the circular order topology; it is
a compact metrizable space, homeomorphic to the Cantor set.
The rotation by $\alpha$ preserves $\mathcal{O}$ and lifts to a homeomorphism $R$ of
$\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$ that preserves the labels $\pm$.
The decision interval becomes clopen in $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$, and the coding map
$\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}\to2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, $y\mapsto(\Brack{R^ny\in[1-\alpha,1)})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$,
is a homeomorphism onto the \emph{Sturmian shift} $X_\alpha$ that conjugates $R$
to the shift~$\sigma$.
The \emph{Bresenham CCS} has states $L=X_\alpha$, the coordinate predicates $P_j(y)=y_j$ for $j\in\mathbb{Z}$, and
the semigroup $\Gamma=\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$.

A point of $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$ lies \emph{over} $t\in\mathbb{T}$ if the natural projection $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}\to\mathbb{T}$ maps it to~$t$.
For $c\in\mathbb{T}$, let $R_c^+$ (respectively $R_c^-$) be the map of $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$ that sends a point over $t$ to $(t+c)^+$ (respectively $(t+c)^-$) when $t+c\in \mathcal{O}$, and to $t+c$ otherwise;
through the coding map, we regard $R_c^+$ and $R_c^-$ as maps of~$X_\alpha$.
The \emph{double arrow circle} $\mathbb{D}$ is $\mathbb{T}\times\{-,+\}$ with the lexicographic circular order and its order topology, and we also write $c^-$ and $c^+$ for its points $(c,-)$ and $(c,+)$; it is compact, first countable, hereditarily separable and not metrizable~\cite[3.10.C]{Engelking:1989}.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Bresenham}
The Bresenham CCS is $\NIP$.
Its deep computations are
\[
E(X_\alpha,\sigma)=\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{R_c^+,R_c^-:c\in\mathbb{T}\},
\]
where each $\sigma^n$ is isolated and $c^\pm\mapsto R_c^\pm$ is a homeomorphism
of $\mathbb{D}$ onto the remaining part.
In particular, the Bresenham CCS is $\NIP_2$ but not $\NIP_3$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
For $\NIP$, note that the predicates $P_j\circ\sigma^n$ are $\{0,1\}$-valued, and that the $j$-th coordinate of $\sigma^n y$ is $1$ exactly when $R^{n+j}y$ lies in the clopen arc $[1-\alpha,1)$ of $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$, that is, when $y$ lies in an arc of $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$.
A family of arcs of a circularly ordered set has VC dimension at most~$3$, so, by the remark at the end of Section~\ref{sec:NIP-poly-exp}, the characteristic functions of its members have the $\NIP$.

For the deep computations, let $\gamma$ be the limit of $\sigma^{n_i}$ along an
ultrafilter, and let $c=\lim n_i\alpha$ in $\mathbb{T}$.
If $n_i$ is eventually constant, then $\gamma$ is a power of $\sigma$.
Otherwise, since the points $n\alpha$ are distinct, the ultrafilter decides
whether $n_i\alpha$ approaches $c$ from the right or from the left.
In the first case, for every point $y$ over $t$ the points $R^{n_i}y$ over
$t+n_i\alpha$ approach $t+c$ strictly from the right, and they converge
in $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$ to $(t+c)^+$ if $t+c\in \mathcal{O}$ and to $t+c$ otherwise; so
$\gamma=R_c^+$.
The second case gives $R_c^-$.
Conversely, every $c$ is approached from both sides by the points $n\alpha$
with $n\ge N$, for every $N$, so every $R_c^\pm$ is a deep computation, and
$R_c^+\ne R_c^-$, since they differ at the points over $d-c$ with $d\in \mathcal{O}$.
The same argument shows that $c_i^{\varepsilon_i}\to c^+$ in $\mathbb{D}$ implies
$R_{c_i}^{\varepsilon_i}\to R_c^+$ pointwise, and symmetrically for $c^-$; so
$c^\pm\mapsto R_c^\pm$ is a continuous bijection from the compact space
$\mathbb{D}$ onto a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism.
Finally, fix $d\in \mathcal{O}$ and consider the two points
$(d-n\alpha)^\pm$ of $\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{O}}$, which $\sigma^n$ sends to $d^-$ and $d^+$.
Every map $R_c^\pm$ sends them to the same point, and so does every pointwise
limit of $\sigma^{m_i}$ with $m_i\to\infty$, by the argument above.
Hence no net of such maps, and no net of maps $\sigma^m$ with $m\ne n$ and $m$ bounded, converges to $\sigma^n$, so $\sigma^n$ is isolated.
Thus $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)$ is the union of a countable set and a copy of
$\mathbb{D}$; it is hereditarily separable and not metrizable.
\end{proof}

The two deep computations $R_c^+$ and $R_c^-$ are the two ways of drawing the
line infinitely far away, where the accumulated error has reached $c$: they
agree except at the pixels where the line passes exactly through a corner,
and there they apply the two opposite rounding conventions.
Figure~\ref{fig:bresenham} shows the two conventions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{bresenham.pdf}
  \caption{Left: the pixels drawn by Bresenham’s algorithm for $\alpha=(\sqrt5-1)/2$, with the decision bits $b_n$.
    Right: where the line passes exactly through a pixel corner, the deep computations $R_c^+$ and $R_c^-$ break the tie in opposite directions; they agree on every other pixel.}
  \label{fig:bresenham}
\end{figure}

The space $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)$ is the closure of a countable set, so it is a
separable Rosenthal compactum; it is hereditarily separable and not
metrizable, so, by Todorčević's trichotomy
(see Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}), it contains a copy of the split
Cantor set $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
For the action of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by the shift, Glasner and
Megrelishvili proved that Sturmian systems are tame$_2$ but not hereditarily
non-sensitive~\cite[Corollary~8.3]{glasner2022tame}.


\subsection{First countable but not hereditarily separable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP1}
When two rounding algorithms run in parallel, the computation descends a further level, as we show here in the case of Bresenham’s algorithm for lines in three-dimensional space, which lies at the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
At this level each deep computation is still separated from all the others by countably many tests of its own, but some finitely separated set of deep computations has the cardinality of the continuum (see Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}).

\emph{Drawing a line in space.}
Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be irrational numbers in $(0,1)$ such that $1,\alpha,\beta$
are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.
To draw a line in $\mathbb{Z}^3$ with direction $(1,\alpha,\beta)$, the
three-dimensional Bresenham algorithm runs the planar algorithm in two
coordinates at once.
Its CCS has states $L=X_\alpha\times X_\beta$, the coordinate predicates of
both factors, and the semigroup generated by $\sigma\times\sigma$.

The analysis of this algorithm uses a property of the double arrow circle~$\mathbb{D}$ of Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP2} that keeps every compact metrizable subset of $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$ countable.
Recall that a \emph{network} of a space is a family of subsets such that every neighborhood of a point contains a member of the family that contains the point;
every base is a network.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:double-arrow-metrizable}
Every subspace of the double arrow circle~$\mathbb{D}$ that has a countable network, in particular every metrizable subspace, is countable.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
A metrizable subspace of~$\mathbb{D}$ is separable, since $\mathbb{D}$ is hereditarily separable, and it therefore has a countable base, which is a countable network.
Let $M\subseteq\mathbb{D}$ have a countable network $\{N_k:k\in\mathbb{N}\}$.
For each $c\in\mathbb{T}$ with $c^+\in M$, the open arc $[c^+,(c+\frac12)^-)$ is a neighborhood of $c^+$ in~$\mathbb{D}$, and we may choose $k(c)\in\mathbb{N}$ with $c^+\in N_{k(c)}\subseteq[c^+,(c+\frac12)^-)$.
If $k(c)=k(c')$, then $c'^+\in[c^+,(c+\frac12)^-)$ and $c^+\in[c'^+,(c'+\frac12)^-)$, so that both $c'-c$ and $c-c'$ lie in $[0,\frac12)$ modulo~$1$, which forces $c=c'$.
Thus $c\mapsto k(c)$ is injective, and $M$ contains only countably many points~$c^+$;
the same argument, applied to the arcs $((c-\frac12)^+,c^-]$, shows that $M$ contains only countably many points~$c^-$.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}\label{cor:DxD-compact-metrizable}
Every compact metrizable subspace of $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$ is countable.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
Let $C\subseteq\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$ be compact and metrizable, and let $\mathcal{N}$ be a countable base of~$C$.
Since the two projections are continuous, the images of the members of~$\mathcal{N}$ under each projection form a countable network of the image of~$C$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:double-arrow-metrizable}, both images are countable, and so is~$C$, which is contained in their product.
\end{proof}

For $c'\in\mathbb{T}$, we also write $R_{c'}^+$ and $R_{c'}^-$ for the maps of $X_\beta$ defined as in Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP2}, with $\beta$ in place of~$\alpha$.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Bresenham3}
The three-dimensional Bresenham CCS is $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
Its space of deep computations $E=E(X_\alpha\times X_\beta,\sigma\times\sigma)$ is the union of the set of iterates $(\sigma\times\sigma)^n$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, each of which is isolated in~$E$, and of the closed subspace
\[
\{(R_c^\varepsilon,R_{c'}^{\varepsilon'}):c,c'\in\mathbb{T},\ \varepsilon,\varepsilon'\in\{+,-\}\},
\]
which is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$.
The space $E$ contains a copy of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$, and it contains no copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, of $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ or of~$A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Each predicate is a coordinate of one factor, so the CCS is $\NIP$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}.
Every deep computation is the limit $\xi$ of the iterates $(\sigma\times\sigma)^n$ along an ultrafilter $\mathcal{U}$ on~$\mathbb{N}$, and the coordinates of~$\xi$ are the limits of the iterates $\sigma^n$ along~$\mathcal{U}$ on $X_\alpha$ and on~$X_\beta$.
If $\mathcal{U}$ is principal, then $\xi$ is an iterate;
otherwise, by the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}, it is a pair $(R_c^\varepsilon,R_{c'}^{\varepsilon'})$.
By Kronecker's theorem, the points $(n\alpha,n\beta)$ with $n\ge N$ are dense in $\mathbb{T}^2$ for every $N$, so every point $(c,c')$ is approached by them from inside each of the four open quadrants at $(c,c')$;
as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}, it follows that every pair $(R_c^\varepsilon,R_{c'}^{\varepsilon'})$, with $\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\in\{+,-\}$, is a deep computation.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}, applied to each factor, the set of these pairs is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$, and it is therefore compact and closed in~$E$;
each iterate $(\sigma\times\sigma)^n$ is isolated in~$E$, since $\sigma^n$ is isolated in $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)$ and is the first coordinate of no other deep computation.
The space $E$ is a closed subspace of $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)\times E(X_\beta,\sigma)$, which is first countable as a product of two first countable spaces;
hence the CCS is $\NIP_1$, and $E$ contains no copy of the space $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, which is not first countable.
Consider the pairs $(R_c^+,R_{-c}^+)$, for $c\in\mathbb{T}$.
For $0<\delta<\frac12$, the set $[c^+,(c+\delta)^-)\times[(-c)^+,(-c+\delta)^-)$ is a neighborhood of $(c^+,(-c)^+)$ in $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$, and it contains $(s^+,(-s)^+)$ only if $c\le s<c+\delta$ and $-c\le -s<-c+\delta$, that is, only if $s=c$.
These pairs therefore form an uncountable discrete subspace, and the space of deep computations is not hereditarily separable;
thus the CCS is not $\NIP_2$.
A compact metrizable subspace of $E$ meets the copy of $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$ in a compact metrizable set, which is countable by Corollary~\ref{cor:DxD-compact-metrizable}, and it is therefore countable.
Since the non-isolated points of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ form a copy of the Cantor set, neither $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ nor the compactum $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ that contains it embeds into~$E$.
Finally, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, the space $E$, the closure of a countable set, is a separable Rosenthal compactum, first countable but not hereditarily separable;
by Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}, it contains a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ or of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$, necessarily of the latter.
\end{proof}

The uncountable discrete set consists of the deep computations that break ties upward in both coordinates at points where the two accumulated errors are opposite.
Each of the two planar algorithms is $\NIP_2$, and drawing in two directions at once leaves the class, just as the Sorgenfrey plane shows that a product of two hereditarily separable spaces need not be hereditarily separable;
Glasner and Megrelishvili show similarly that a product of two tame$_2$ systems need not be tame$_2$~\cite[Proposition~4.1]{glasner2022tame}.%
\footnote{Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite[Remark~4.2]{glasner2022tame} assert that the square of the double arrow space (the set of the points $p^-<p^+$, for $p\in[0,1]$, with its order topology) contains a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, namely the set of the points $(p^+,(1-p)^+)$ and $(p^-,(1-p)^+)$ with $p$ in the Cantor set.
  This set is not closed, and hence not compact: if $p$ is the limit of a decreasing sequence $(x_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ of points of the Cantor set, the points $(x_k^-,(1-x_k)^+)$ of the set converge to $(p^+,(1-p)^-)$, which does not belong to it.
  Moreover, the square of the double arrow space contains no copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ at all: the arguments of Lemma~\ref{lem:double-arrow-metrizable} and Corollary~\ref{cor:DxD-compact-metrizable} apply, with intervals in place of arcs, to the double arrow space, and the non-isolated points of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ form an uncountable compact metrizable set.
  Their Proposition~4.1 does not depend on this remark.}
A second example at this level, with a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ among its deep computations, is a countable family of elements of the group generated by the steps of the Euclidean algorithm (see Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid-duplicate});
whether a single iterated map can realize the level with such a copy is Question~\ref{q:cascade-duplicate}.

\begin{rem}\label{rem:Bresenham3-explicit}
A copy of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ in~$E$ whose dense set of isolated points consists of iterates can be exhibited explicitly.
For $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, let $x_t$ be the right endpoint of the closed interval of length $3^{-|t|}$ indexed by~$t$ in the construction of the middle-thirds Cantor set, and put $y_t=\frac32\,3^{-|t|}-x_t$.
By Kronecker's theorem, there are pairwise distinct $n_t\in\mathbb{N}$ such that, modulo~$1$, the point $n_t\alpha$ lies in $(x_t,x_t+3^{-|t|-1})$ and the point $n_t\beta$ in $(y_t,y_t+3^{-|t|-1})$.
For $b\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$, let $x_b$ be the point of the Cantor set coded by~$b$, the limit of the points $x_{b\restr n}$.
The iterates $(\sigma\times\sigma)^{n_t}$ converge to $(R_{x_b}^+,R_{-x_b}^+)$ as $t$ runs through the initial segments of~$b$, to $(R_{x_b}^-,R_{-x_b}^+)$ along words $t$ that converge to~$b$, are not initial segments of~$b$ and satisfy $t^\frown0^\infty<b$, and to $(R_{x_b}^+,R_{-x_b}^-)$ along words that converge to~$b$ and satisfy $t^\frown0^\infty>b$.
These are the convergence patterns of the family $D_7$ of Section~\ref{sec:heptachotomy}, and, since the iterates are isolated in~$E$, a lemma of Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos~\cite[Lemma~19]{argyros2008rosenthal} shows that the family $\{(\sigma\times\sigma)^{n_t}:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to~$D_7$;
its closure is therefore homeomorphic to $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.
These three patterns are drawn in the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:trichotomy-ii};
its right panel shows the copy of $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ that maps built from the Euclidean algorithm produce in Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP}.
\end{rem}


\subsection{NIP but not first countable: the Euclidean algorithm}
\label{sec:ex-NIP}
The last tame level, $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, is reached by the Euclidean algorithm, acting on the real projective line through the group generated by its steps.
At this level some deep computation is not determined by countably
many tests of its own, that is, by countably many pairs of an input and a feature (see Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}).
A constant deep computation has this property whenever uncountably many exact equality tests, maps each of which takes one value at a single input and the constant value at every other input, are also deep computations: countably many tests involve only countably many inputs, and they therefore cannot distinguish the constant map from an equality test whose single input they miss.
The Euclidean algorithm gives rise to equality tests of precisely this kind among its deep computations.

Let $L=\RP^1=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$, with the predicates $P_1,P_3$ of
Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}, and let $\Gamma=\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ be the
group of Möbius maps $x\mapsto(ax+b)/(cx+d)$ with integer coefficients and
$ad-bc=\pm1$.
It is generated by the steps $x\mapsto x+1$ and $x\mapsto1/x$ of the
Euclidean algorithm, and the maps that the continued fraction algorithm
composes are elements of~$\Gamma$.
We call $(\RP^1,(P_1,P_3),\Gamma)$ the \emph{Euclidean CCS}.
It is a dynamical CCS whose semigroup is a group, and its deep computations
form $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.

For an irrational number $\eta=[a_0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$, let $p_k/q_k$ be its
convergents, put $p_{-1}=1$ and $q_{-1}=0$, and let
$\eta_{k+1}=[a_{k+1};a_{k+2},\dots]$ be its complete quotients.
Let $M_k$ be the Möbius map $t\mapsto(p_kt+p_{k-1})/(q_kt+q_{k-1})$, which belongs
to $\Gamma$ because $p_kq_{k-1}-p_{k-1}q_k=(-1)^{k+1}$.
Writing $A_j(t)=a_j+1/t$, we have $M_k=A_0\circ A_1\circ\dots\circ A_k$, so
$M_k^{-1}=A_k^{-1}\circ\dots\circ A_0^{-1}$ with $A_j^{-1}(t)=1/(t-a_j)$.
Thus $M_k^{-1}$ is the map that the first $k+1$ steps of the continued fraction
algorithm for $\eta$ apply, and the classical identity $\eta=M_k(\eta_{k+1})$ says
that it sends $\eta$ to its complete quotient~$\eta_{k+1}$.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:euclid-limit}
Let $\eta$ be an irrational number, and let $x\in\RP^1$ be distinct from~$\eta$.
Then $M_k^{-1}(x)+q_{k-1}/q_k\to0$ as $k\to\infty$, while $M_k^{-1}(\eta)=\eta_{k+1}>1$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Solving $M_k(t)=x$ gives $M_k^{-1}(\infty)=-q_{k-1}/q_k$ and, for real $x$,
\[
M_k^{-1}(x)=\frac{q_{k-1}x-p_{k-1}}{p_k-q_kx}=-\frac{q_{k-1}}{q_k}\cdot\frac{x-p_{k-1}/q_{k-1}}{x-p_k/q_k}
\]
for $k\ge1$ (for such $k$ we have $q_{k-1}\ge1$), where the value is $\infty$ if $x=p_k/q_k$.
The convergents of an irrational number are pairwise distinct, so $x$ equals
$p_k/q_k$ for at most one $k$; for all larger $k$ the formula gives a real number.
Both convergents tend to $\eta\ne x$, so the last fraction tends to $1$, and
$0<q_{k-1}/q_k\le1$ for $k\ge1$; hence $M_k^{-1}(x)+q_{k-1}/q_k\to0$.
The identity $M_k^{-1}(\eta)=\eta_{k+1}$ is the classical one, and
$\eta_{k+1}>a_{k+1}\ge1$ because $\eta_{k+1}$ is irrational.
\end{proof}

Let $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ be the golden ratio.
Let $G$ be the set of irrational numbers $\eta=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ whose sequence
of partial quotients is a concatenation $1^{1}d_11^{2}d_21^{3}d_3\cdots$, where
$1^n$ denotes $n$ consecutive ones and each $d_n\in\{1,2\}$.
The block $1^n$ occupies the indices $s_n,\dots,s_n+n-1$ with $s_n=n(n+1)/2$.
Distinct choices of $(d_n)$ give distinct numbers, by the uniqueness of
continued fraction expansions, so $G$ has the cardinality of the continuum.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:euclid}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item For every $\eta\in G$, the map $\delta_\eta$ that sends $\eta$ to $\varphi$
and every other point of $\RP^1$ to $-1/\varphi$ is a deep computation of the
Euclidean CCS.
\item The constant map $c$ with value $-1/\varphi$ is a deep computation, and it
has no countable base of neighborhoods in $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ is not first countable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $\eta\in G$, and for each $n$ let $k_n=s_n+\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ be the
middle index of the block $1^n$, and $m_n=\lceil n/2\rceil-1$.
Then $a_{k_n}=a_{k_n-1}=\dots=a_{k_n-m_n}=1$ and $a_{k_n+1}=\dots=a_{k_n+m_n}=1$,
and $m_n\to\infty$.
We have $q_{k-1}/q_k=[0;a_k,a_{k-1},\dots,a_1]$ and
$\eta_{k+1}=[a_{k+1};a_{k+2},\dots]$.
Suppose that two continued fractions, finite or infinite, $[c_0;c_1,\dots]$ and
$[c_0';c_1',\dots]$ satisfy $c_j=c_j'$ for $j<m$, with $c_1=\dots=c_{m-1}=1$.
Then they share the convergents of index at most $m-1$, and both lie between
the last two of them (or equal one of them).
It follows that they differ by at most $1/(q_{m-2}q_{m-1})\le1/(F_{m-1}F_m)$, where $F_j$ are
the Fibonacci numbers, indexed so that $F_1=F_2=1$, whatever the remaining partial quotients are.
The hypothesis holds with $m=m_n$ for the pair $[0;a_{k_n},\dots,a_1]$, $[0;1,1,\dots]$ and for the pair $[a_{k_n+1};a_{k_n+2},\dots]$, $[1;1,1,\dots]$.
Hence $q_{k_n-1}/q_{k_n}\to[0;1,1,\dots]=1/\varphi$ and
$\eta_{k_n+1}\to[1;1,1,\dots]=\varphi$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit}, the maps $M_{k_n}^{-1}\in\Gamma$ converge
pointwise to $\delta_\eta$, at $x=\infty$ as well.

(ii) Let $(\eta_j)$ be a sequence of distinct elements of $G$.
For each $x\in\RP^1$, at most one $j$ has $\eta_j=x$, so
$\delta_{\eta_j}(x)=-1/\varphi$ for all but at most one $j$.
Hence $\delta_{\eta_j}\to c$ pointwise, and $c\in E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ because this set
is closed.
Now let $(U_i)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ be open neighborhoods of $c$ in
$E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.
Each $U_i$ contains a basic neighborhood
$\{f:d(f(x),c(x))<\varepsilon_i\text{ for }x\in F_i\}$ with $F_i\subseteq\RP^1$
finite, $\varepsilon_i>0$ and $d$ a metric on~$\RP^1$.
The set $F=\bigcup_iF_i$ is countable, so some $\eta\in G$ lies outside $F$, and
then $\delta_\eta$ agrees with $c$ on~$F$.
Hence $\delta_\eta\in\bigcap_iU_i$, while $\delta_\eta\ne c$.
Thus no countable family of neighborhoods of $c$ has intersection $\{c\}$.
In a Hausdorff space, the intersection of a base of neighborhoods of $c$ is
$\{c\}$, so $c$ has no countable base of neighborhoods.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}\label{cor:euclid}
The Euclidean CCS is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$.
Its space of deep computations contains the copy
$\{\delta_\eta:\eta\in G\}\cup\{c\}$ of the Alexandroff compactification
$A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
Ellis proved that the action of $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ on the projective line is
tame~\cite{Ellis1993}; see~\cite[Example~5.7]{glasner2022tame}.
For every continuous $f\colon\RP^1\to\mathbb{R}$, the family
$\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ is contained in the corresponding family for
$\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, so it has the $\NIP$.
Hence the action of $\Gamma$ is tame, and, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the Euclidean CCS is $\NIP$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid}, it is not $\NIP_1$.
Each $\delta_\eta$ is isolated in $\{\delta_\eta:\eta\in G\}\cup\{c\}$, since it
is the only member that sends $\eta$ near~$\varphi$, and every neighborhood of
$c$ contains all but finitely many of the $\delta_\eta$, as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid}.
The set $\{\delta_\eta:\eta\in G\}\cup\{c\}$ is therefore the one-point compactification of a discrete set of cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$, and hence it is homeomorphic to $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ (see Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}).
\end{proof}

The deep computation $\delta_\eta$ is an exact equality test (see Figure~\ref{fig:euclid}): it takes one value if the input is exactly $\eta$ and another value otherwise.
It is the pointwise limit of maps that the continued fraction algorithm applies to $\eta$, when these maps are applied to all inputs.
The mechanism is that of convergence groups:
groups of homeomorphisms of a compact space in which every sequence of distinct elements has a subsequence converging to a constant map uniformly on the compact subsets of the complement of some point.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit}, the maps $M_k^{-1}$ draw the images of all
points other than $\eta$ together, near $-q_{k-1}/q_k\in[-1,0)$, while they
send $\eta$ into $(1,\infty)$;
in the language of convergence groups, every irrational $\eta$ is thus a
conical limit point of~$\Gamma$, that is, some sequence in~$\Gamma$ sends~$\eta$ to one limit and every other point to another.
The choice of $G$ makes the two limits the same for uncountably many~$\eta$.
Glasner and Megrelishvili use the same device to prove that an infinite
countable uniform convergence group acting on a compact metric space with
infinite limit set is tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Proposition~6.1 and Theorem~6.5]{glasner2022tame}.
Their theorem does not cover $\Gamma$, which contains parabolic elements and is
not a uniform convergence group on~$\RP^1$.
Ellis's example, the projective action of the whole group
$\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, is also tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Example~5.7]{glasner2022tame}; the Euclidean CCS shows that
the countable group generated by the two steps of the algorithm already
reaches this level.

Iterating a single element of $\Gamma$ cannot produce $\delta_\eta$.
For $g\in\Gamma$, the closure of $\{g^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ in $(\RP^1)^{\RP^1}$ is
countable, hence metrizable, and it does not contain $\delta_\eta$.
Indeed, if $g$ has finite order, the set of its powers is finite.
If $g$ is parabolic, with fixed point $x_0$, then $g^n$ converges pointwise to the constant map $x_0$ as $n\to\pm\infty$.
If $g$ is hyperbolic, with attracting and repelling fixed points $x_a$ and $x_r$, then $g^n$ converges to the map that fixes $x_r$ and sends every other point to $x_a$ as $n\to+\infty$, and to the map that fixes $x_a$ and sends every other point to $x_r$ as $n\to-\infty$.
If $g$ reverses orientation and has infinite order, then its trace $t$ is
nonzero, so $g^2$ has trace $t^2+2>2$ and is hyperbolic, with the same fixed
points as $g$; the odd powers $g^{2m+1}=g\circ g^{2m}$ have the same two limits
as the even ones.
In every case the closure consists of the powers of $g$, which are
homeomorphisms, and at most two limit maps, each of which is constant or
fixes the unique point at which it differs from a constant.
The map $\delta_\eta$ is none of these, since it is not injective and
$\delta_\eta(\eta)=\varphi\ne\eta$.

\emph{A copy of the Alexandroff duplicate.}
The device of Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid} also produces, among the deep computations of the Euclidean CCS, a copy of the Alexandroff duplicate $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, and with it a countable set of computations at the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
Instead of sending uncountably many irrational numbers to the same pair of values, we now let the value that a deep computation takes away from its exceptional point vary continuously with that point.
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of irrational numbers $[0;\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots]$ with $\omega_n\in\{1,2\}$ for every $n\ge1$, a Cantor set homeomorphic to $\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ through continued fraction expansions;
for $\omega=(\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots)\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ we write $[0;\omega]$ for the point $[0;\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots]$ of~$\mathcal{C}$.
For a finite or infinite sequence $\omega$ over the alphabet $\{1,2\}$ and for each positive integer~$n$ not exceeding its length, let $\Lambda_n(\omega)$ be the block $(\omega_n,\omega_{n-1},\dots,\omega_1,1^n)$, formed by the first $n$ terms of~$\omega$ in reverse order followed by $n$ ones.
For $\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, let $\eta(\omega)$ be the irrational number in $(0,1)$ whose partial quotients after the initial~$0$ form the concatenation $\Lambda_1(\omega)\Lambda_2(\omega)\Lambda_3(\omega)\cdots$;
since $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n$ can be read off from $\Lambda_n(\omega)$, the map $\omega\mapsto\eta(\omega)$ is injective.
For a finite word $\mathsf{w}=(\mathsf{w}_1,\dots,\mathsf{w}_k)$ of positive integers, let $M_{\mathsf{w}}$ be the Möbius map $x\mapsto[0;\mathsf{w}_1,\dots,\mathsf{w}_{k-1},\mathsf{w}_k+1/x]$, so that $M_k=M_{(a_1,\dots,a_k)}$ for an irrational number $[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ in $(0,1)$, and let $M_{\emptyset}(x)=1/x$ for the empty word.
For a word $t=(t_1,\dots,t_m)$ in $\{1,2\}^{<\mathbb{N}}$, the set of finite words over $\{1,2\}$, let $t^\sharp$ be the word $\Lambda_1(t)\cdots\Lambda_{m-1}(t)\,(t_m,t_{m-1},\dots,t_1)$, of length~$m^2$, and put $\gamma_t=M_{t^\sharp}^{-1}\in\Gamma$.
Finally, for $\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, let $\tau_\omega$ be the map that sends $\eta(\omega)$ to $\varphi$ and every other point of $\RP^1$ to $-[0;\omega]$, and let $c_\omega$ be the constant map with value~$-[0;\omega]$, so that the map $c$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid} is $c_\omega$ for the constant sequence $\omega=(1,1,1,\dots)$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:euclid-duplicate}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item
  The closure of the countable set $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}=\{\gamma_t:t\in\{1,2\}^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\cup\{\tau_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{c_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}$, and it is homeomorphic to $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ through a homeomorphism that maps $\{\tau_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{c_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}$ onto $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
  In particular, $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ contains a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, and the set $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ of computations of the Euclidean CCS is $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
\item
  Every hereditarily separable compact subset of $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ is metrizable.
  In particular, $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ contains no copy of $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ and none of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) We use two facts from the proofs of Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit} and Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid}.
First, two continued fractions, finite or infinite, with positive integer partial quotients that agree in their terms of index less than~$m$ lie between two consecutive convergents of index $m-2$ and $m-1$, and hence differ by at most $1/(F_{m-1}F_m)$.
Second, the formula for $M_k^{-1}$ in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit} uses only the terms $a_0,\dots,a_k$:
if $k\ge1$ and $p_i/q_i$ are the convergents of $[a_0;a_1,\dots,a_k]$, the map $M(x)=[a_0;a_1,\dots,a_{k-1},a_k+1/x]$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mobius-inverse}
M^{-1}(x)=-\frac{q_{k-1}}{q_k}\cdot\frac{x-p_{k-1}/q_{k-1}}{x-p_k/q_k}\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}),
\qquad
M^{-1}(\infty)=-\frac{q_{k-1}}{q_k},
\end{equation}
where $q_{k-1}/q_k=[0;a_k,a_{k-1},\dots,a_1]$.

\emph{Limits along initial segments.}
Let $\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ be given, and put $\eta=\eta(\omega)=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$.
For $m\ge1$, the word $(\omega\restr m)^\sharp$ consists of the first $m^2$ partial quotients of~$\eta$, so that $\gamma_{\omega\restr m}=M_{m^2}^{-1}$.
The terms $a_{m^2},a_{m^2-1},\dots,a_{m^2-m+1}$ are $\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots,\omega_m$, and $a_{m^2+1}=\dots=a_{m^2+m}=1$;
by the first fact, $q_{m^2-1}/q_{m^2}=[0;a_{m^2},\dots,a_1]$ tends to~$[0;\omega]$, and the complete quotient $\eta_{m^2+1}$ tends to $[1;1,1,\dots]=\varphi$, as $m\to\infty$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit}, the maps $\gamma_{\omega\restr m}$ therefore converge pointwise to~$\tau_\omega$.

\emph{Limits along other words.}
Let $(t_j)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of words that converges to~$\omega$, none of which is an initial segment of~$\omega$, and let $n_j$ be the length of the longest common initial segment of $t_j$ and~$\omega$, so that $n_j\to\infty$.
We show that $\gamma_{t_j}\to c_\omega$ pointwise.
Write $t_j^\sharp=(\mathsf{w}_1,\dots,\mathsf{w}_k)$, where $k=k_j=|t_j|^2$, and let $\ell=\ell_j=n_j(n_j+1)+1$.
The blocks $\Lambda_1(t_j),\dots,\Lambda_{n_j}(t_j)$ coincide with $\Lambda_1(\omega),\dots,\Lambda_{n_j}(\omega)$, and the next term of $t_j^\sharp$ is the term of index $n_j+1$ of~$t_j$;
hence $\mathsf{w}_i=a_i$ for $i<\ell$, while $\mathsf{w}_\ell\neq a_\ell$, both terms lying in $\{1,2\}$.
Moreover, since $t_j^\sharp$ ends with the terms of~$t_j$ in reverse order, the terms $\mathsf{w}_k,\mathsf{w}_{k-1},\dots$ begin with $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{n_j}$, and $k-\ell\ge(n_j+1)^2-\ell=n_j$.
Let $x\in\RP^1$ be distinct from~$\eta$.
The convergents of $[0;\mathsf{w}_1,\dots,\mathsf{w}_k]$ of index at least $\ell-1$ agree with~$\eta$ in their terms of index less than~$\ell$, so, by the first fact, those of index $k-1$ and~$k$ tend to~$\eta$ as $j\to\infty$, while $q_{k-1}/q_k=[0;\mathsf{w}_k,\dots,\mathsf{w}_1]$ tends to~$[0;\omega]$;
by~\eqref{eq:Mobius-inverse}, it follows that $\gamma_{t_j}(x)\to-[0;\omega]$.
At the point~$\eta$ we write $\gamma_{t_j}$ as the composite of the map $x\mapsto1/x$ followed by the maps $x\mapsto1/(x-\mathsf{w}_i)$, for $i=1,\dots,k$ in this order, and we apply these $k+1$ factors one at a time.
The first $\ell$ factors send $\eta$ to its complete quotient $\eta_\ell=[a_\ell;a_{\ell+1},\dots]$, which lies in $(a_\ell,a_\ell+1)$.
The next factor sends $\eta_\ell$ to $z_j=1/(\eta_\ell-\mathsf{w}_\ell)$, which lies in $(\frac12,1)$ if $(a_\ell,\mathsf{w}_\ell)=(2,1)$ and in $(-\infty,-1)$ if $(a_\ell,\mathsf{w}_\ell)=(1,2)$;
in either case $z_j<1$.
The remaining factors form the inverse of the map $M'(x)=[\mathsf{w}_{\ell+1};\mathsf{w}_{\ell+2},\dots,\mathsf{w}_{k-1},\mathsf{w}_k+1/x]$, and therefore $\gamma_{t_j}(\eta)=M'^{-1}(z_j)$.
Let $p'_i/q'_i$ be the convergents of $[\mathsf{w}_{\ell+1};\mathsf{w}_{\ell+2},\dots,\mathsf{w}_k]$, the last of which has index $i=k-\ell-1\ge n_j-1$.
Every convergent of index at least~$1$ has the form $\mathsf{w}_{\ell+1}+1/y$ with $1\le y\le3$, since all its partial quotients lie in $\{1,2\}$;
it is therefore at least $\frac43$, and its distance to $z_j$ exceeds~$\frac13$.
The convergents of index $i-1$ and~$i$ differ by $1/(q'_{i-1}q'_i)\le1/(F_iF_{i+1})$, and $q'_{i-1}/q'_i=[0;\mathsf{w}_k,\dots,\mathsf{w}_{\ell+2}]$ tends to~$[0;\omega]$, since its first $n_j-1$ terms are $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{n_j-1}$.
By~\eqref{eq:Mobius-inverse}, applied to~$M'$, we conclude that $\gamma_{t_j}(\eta)\to-[0;\omega]$, and hence that $\gamma_{t_j}\to c_\omega$.

\emph{The closure.}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:euclid} and Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, the space $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ is a Rosenthal compactum, so every point of the closure of $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ that does not belong to $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the limit of a sequence $(\gamma_{t_j})$ with pairwise distinct words~$t_j$~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}.
By compactness, such a sequence of words has a subsequence that converges to some $\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, and a further subsequence that consists either of initial segments of~$\omega$ or of words that are not initial segments of~$\omega$;
by the two preceding paragraphs, the corresponding maps converge to $\tau_\omega$ or to~$c_\omega$.
Both kinds of limit occur for every~$\omega$, and the closure of $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ is therefore the set described in~(i).

\emph{The homeomorphism.}
Identify $\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ with $2^{\mathbb{N}}$, and $\{1,2\}^{<\mathbb{N}}$ with $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, by replacing the letters $1$ and~$2$ with $0$ and~$1$, and define $\Theta$ on $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ by $\Theta(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty)=\gamma_t$, $\Theta(\delta_\omega,\omega)=\tau_\omega$ and $\Theta(\mathbf{0},\omega)=c_\omega$.
The map $\Theta$ is surjective by the preceding paragraph.
It is injective:
the constant maps $c_\omega$ are pairwise distinct, because distinct sequences~$\omega$ give distinct numbers $[0;\omega]$, and for the same reason so are the maps $\tau_\omega$, none of which is constant;
neither kind of map is injective, while the maps $\gamma_t$ are homeomorphisms;
the maps $\gamma_t$, finally, are pairwise distinct, because the number $M_{\mathsf{w}}(\infty)=[0;\mathsf{w}_1,\dots,\mathsf{w}_k]$ and the determinant $(-1)^{k+1}$ of a matrix that represents $M_{\mathsf{w}}$ determine the word~$\mathsf{w}$, a rational number in $[0,1]$ having at most two expansions of this form, whose lengths differ by one, and because $t^\sharp$ determines~$t$.
Since $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is first countable, the continuity of $\Theta$ may be tested on sequences.
A sequence that converges to a point $(\delta_\omega,\omega)$ consists, apart from that point, of points $(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty)$ with $t$ an initial segment of~$\omega$, whose lengths tend to infinity, and its image converges to $\tau_\omega$ by the first of the paragraphs above.
A sequence that converges to a point $(\mathbf{0},\omega)$ consists, apart from that point, of points $(\mathbf{0},\omega')$ and $(\delta_{\omega'},\omega')$ with $\omega'\to\omega$ and $\omega'\neq\omega$, and of points $(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty)$ with $t$ converging to~$\omega$ and eventually not an initial segment of~$\omega$.
The images of the points of the first kind converge to~$c_\omega$ because $[0;\omega']$ depends continuously on~$\omega'$, those of the third kind by the second of the paragraphs above, and those of the second kind because, for each $x\in\RP^1$, the equality $\tau_{\omega'}(x)=-[0;\omega']$ fails only if $\eta(\omega')=x$, which happens for at most one~$\omega'$.
Hence $\Theta$ is a continuous bijection of a compact space onto a Hausdorff space, and therefore a homeomorphism;
it maps $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ onto $\{\tau_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{c_\omega:\omega\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}$.
The set $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ is countable, and it has the NIP because $\Gamma$ has it (see Corollary~\ref{cor:euclid});
its closure, a copy of $\hat D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, is first countable but not hereditarily separable (see Section~\ref{sec:trichotomy}), and $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ is therefore $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.

(ii) We first describe the deep computations that are not computations.
For $y,z,w\in\RP^1$, let $\zeta_{y,z,w}$ be the map that sends $z$ to~$w$ and every other point to~$y$.
Let $\xi\in E(\RP^1,\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma$.
Since $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ is a Rosenthal compactum, $\xi$ is the limit of a sequence $(g_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of pairwise distinct elements of~$\Gamma$.
Represent each $g_n$ by an integer matrix $\mathbf{G}_n$ with determinant $\pm1$;
since only finitely many integer matrices have norm below a given bound, we have $\|\mathbf{G}_n\|\to\infty$, and, passing to a subsequence, we may assume that $\mathbf{G}_n/\|\mathbf{G}_n\|$ converges to a matrix $\mathbf{G}$ of norm~$1$.
Since $\det(\mathbf{G}_n/\|\mathbf{G}_n\|)=\pm\|\mathbf{G}_n\|^{-2}\to0$, the matrix $\mathbf{G}$ has rank one, and $\mathbf{G}\mathbf{e}=\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}'\rangle\,\mathbf{e}''$ for all $\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^2$, for some nonzero vectors $\mathbf{e}',\mathbf{e}''\in\mathbb{R}^2$.
If $x\in\RP^1$ is the line spanned by a vector~$\mathbf{e}$ with $\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}'\rangle\neq0$, then $\mathbf{G}_n\mathbf{e}/\|\mathbf{G}_n\|\to\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}'\rangle\,\mathbf{e}''\neq0$, and $g_n(x)$ converges to the line $y$ spanned by~$\mathbf{e}''$.
Thus, if $z$ is the line orthogonal to~$\mathbf{e}'$ and $w=\xi(z)$, then $\xi=\zeta_{y,z,w}$.

Now let $K\subseteq E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ be compact and hereditarily separable, and let $Z_K$ be the set of points $z\in\RP^1$ for which some map $\zeta_{y,z,w}$ with $y\neq w$ belongs to~$K$;
such a map determines~$z$.
Choose, for each $z\in Z_K$, one such map $\zeta_z=\zeta_{y_z,z,w_z}\in K$.
The set $\{\zeta_z:z\in Z_K\}$ is discrete.
Indeed, fix a metric $d$ on~$\RP^1$ and a point $z\in Z_K$, let $\varepsilon=d(y_z,w_z)/2$, choose distinct points $x,x'\in\RP^1\setminus\{z\}$, and consider the neighborhood of $\zeta_z$ formed by the maps $g$ with $d(g(z),w_z)<\varepsilon$, $d(g(x),y_z)<\varepsilon$ and $d(g(x'),y_z)<\varepsilon$.
If $z'\in Z_K\setminus\{z\}$, one of the points $x,x'$, say~$x$, differs from~$z'$, and then $\zeta_{z'}(z)=\zeta_{z'}(x)=y_{z'}$;
were $\zeta_{z'}$ in this neighborhood, we would have $d(y_z,w_z)\le d(y_z,y_{z'})+d(y_{z'},w_z)<2\varepsilon$, which is impossible.
Since $K$ is hereditarily separable, the discrete set $\{\zeta_z:z\in Z_K\}$ is countable, and so is~$Z_K$.
Evaluation at the points of the countable set $Z_K\cup\mathbb{Q}$ separates the members of~$K$:
two members of~$\Gamma$ that agree on the dense set~$\mathbb{Q}$ agree everywhere;
a member of~$\Gamma$ is injective, whereas a map $\zeta_{y,z,w}$ takes the value~$y$ at all points of~$\mathbb{Q}$ but at most one;
two maps $\zeta_{y,z,w}$ and $\zeta_{y',z',w'}$ with $y\neq y'$ differ at a point of $\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{z,z'\}$;
and two distinct maps $\zeta_{y,z,w}$ and $\zeta_{y,z',w'}$, of which the first, say, satisfies $w\neq y$, differ at the point $z\in Z_K$, at which the second takes the value~$y$ if $z'\neq z$ and the value $w'\neq w$ if $z'=z$.
Hence restriction to $Z_K\cup\mathbb{Q}$ is a continuous injection of the compact space~$K$ into the metrizable space $(\RP^1)^{Z_K\cup\mathbb{Q}}$, and $K$ is metrizable.

Finally, the split Cantor set $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is hereditarily separable and not metrizable, and so is the closed subspace of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ formed by its points with first coordinate~$\mathbf{0}$;
neither space therefore embeds into $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.
The subspace just described is hereditarily separable, since the projection onto the second coordinate maps it homeomorphically onto a subspace of $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
To see that it is not metrizable, let $\varpi\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$ have infinitely many zeros, let $a$ be the branch of the subtree $\{s_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} that extends the words $s_{\varpi\restr n}$, and let $u_1,u_2,\dots$ be the words $s_{(\varpi\restr n)^\frown1}$ with $\varpi(n)=0$, listed by increasing~$n$.
These words are pairwise incomparable and converge to~$a$, and, since the canonical isomorphism preserves the lexicographic order, the sequences $u_k^\frown0^\infty$ decrease;
by the description of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ in Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}, the point $(\mathbf{0},\chi_{(a})$ belongs to it, and distinct sequences~$\varpi$ give distinct branches.
In $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, each point $\chi_{(a}$ has the neighborhoods $\{\chi_{(a}\}\cup\{\chi_{[x},\chi_{(x}:a<x<b\}$, for $b>a$, and the argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:double-arrow-metrizable} shows that a metrizable subspace of $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ contains only countably many of the points~$\chi_{(a}$.
\end{proof}

Together with Corollary~\ref{cor:euclid}, Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid-duplicate} shows that the space of deep computations of the Euclidean CCS contains copies of $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ and of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$, but no copy of $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ or of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.
At the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$, the set $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ thus realizes the alternative of Todorčević's trichotomy opposite to the one realized by the three-dimensional Bresenham algorithm (see Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham3});
the two alternatives are drawn side by side in Figure~\ref{fig:trichotomy-ii}.

\emph{Free reduction.}
A second example at this level is the free group $F_2$ on two generators $a,b$,
acting on its boundary $\partial F_2$, the space of infinite reduced words in
$a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1}$, which is a Cantor set.
A generator acts on an infinite word by prepending a letter and cancelling it
against the first letter if they are inverse, as a stack does.
With the predicates $w\mapsto\Brack{w_n=x}$, for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and each letter $x$, this is a dynamical CCS, and
Glasner and Megrelishvili proved that the system is tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Corollary~6.6 and Example~6.7]{glasner2022tame}.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, this CCS is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$.

\emph{An open question.}
Both examples at this level are actions of groups with two generators.
Whether a single map can reach this level, that is, whether some cascade CCS
is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, is Question~\ref{q:cascade-NIP1}.
For actions of $\mathbb{Z}$, and more generally of abelian groups, the
existence of a tame metrizable system whose enveloping semigroup is not first
countable is Question~6.8 of Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame};
to our knowledge, it is open.


\subsection{Beyond NIP}
\label{sec:beyond-NIP}
Chaotic iteration leads beyond the hierarchy, to sets of computations with the independence property, and we close the section with examples, among them Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere, the doubling map of the circle and the logistic map beyond the Feigenbaum parameter.
In a set of computations with the independence property, the computations realize, for some feature and on suitable inputs, every finite pattern of high and
low values.

\emph{Newton's method on the Riemann sphere.}
On its Julia set the Newton map is chaotic, and the chaos is visible to the
predicates of the CCS.
We exhibit the independence in the simplest case, the square roots of~$1$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Newton-wild}
Let $p(z)=z^2-1$, and let $N(z)=\frac12\bigl(z+\frac1z\bigr)$ be its Newton map on
$\hatC$.
The functions $P_2\circ N^{3k}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, where $P_2$ is the second coordinate of the inverse stereographic projection $\hatC\to S^2$ of Section~\ref{sec:examples}, form an independent sequence:
for all finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is $z\in\hatC$ with
$P_2(N^{3k}z)\ge\frac12$ for $k\in I$ and $P_2(N^{3k}z)\le-\frac12$ for $k\in J$.
In particular, the Newton CCS of $p$ is not $\NIP$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The Möbius transformation $\psi(z)=(z-1)/(z+1)$ conjugates $N$ to the squaring
map: $\psi(N(z))=\psi(z)^2$.
It maps the imaginary axis, together with $\infty$, onto the unit circle, with
$\psi^{-1}(e^{i\theta})=i\cot(\theta/2)$.
Hence, for $z=i\cot(\theta/2)$, we have $N^n(z)=i\cot(2^{n}\theta/2)$ and
\[
P_2\bigl(N^n(z)\bigr)=\frac{2\cot(2^{n-1}\theta)}{\cot^2(2^{n-1}\theta)+1}=\sin(2^n\theta).
\]
Write $\theta=2\pi\sum_{j\ge1}x_j2^{-j}$ with bits $x_j$.
The fractional part of $2^{3k}\theta/2\pi$ has binary expansion
$0.x_{3k+1}x_{3k+2}x_{3k+3}\cdots$.
If $(x_{3k+1},x_{3k+2},x_{3k+3})=(0,1,0)$, this fractional part lies in
$[\frac14,\frac38]$, so $\sin(2^{3k}\theta)\ge\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}>\frac12$; if the block
is $(1,1,0)$, it lies in $[\frac34,\frac78]$, so $\sin(2^{3k}\theta)<-\frac12$.
Choosing the block $(0,1,0)$ for $k\in I$ and $(1,1,0)$ for $k\in J$ gives the
required~$z$.
\end{proof}

The proof also shows that the doubling map $x\mapsto2x$ of the circle
$\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, with the predicates $\cos2\pi x$ and
$\sin2\pi x$, is not $\NIP$: with $x=\theta/2\pi$, the functions
$\sin(2\pi\,2^{3k}x)$ form an independent sequence.
The doubling map shifts the binary digits of~$x$, and the independence comes
from the freedom to choose those digits.

On the real projective line, by contrast, the same algorithm is tame.
The Julia set of $N$, the imaginary axis together with $\infty$, meets
$\RP^1$ only at~$\infty$, and on $\RP^1$ the iterates converge to the deep
computation of Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute} for $a=1$;
by Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent}, the corresponding CCS is therefore $\NIP_3$ (see Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP3}).
Whether Newton's method is tame is thus decided by the space of states on which it acts, and not by the
formula that defines its step.

\emph{Rational maps of the sphere.}
The chaos exhibited in Proposition~\ref{prop:Newton-wild} is not peculiar to the square roots of~$1$: it is present on the Julia set of every rational map of degree at least~$2$, and with it the independence property.
The argument rests on two classical facts about the Julia set $\mathcal{J}_g$ of such a map~$g$ (see Section~\ref{sec:Newton-map}): it is a nonempty compact set without isolated points that satisfies $g^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_g)=\mathcal{J}_g$, and, as a consequence of Montel's theorem and of the density of repelling cycles in~$\mathcal{J}_g$, for every neighborhood $U$ of a point of $\mathcal{J}_g$ the image $g^m(U)$ contains $\mathcal{J}_g$ for all sufficiently large~$m$~\cite[Sections~4 and~14]{Milnor2006}.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:rational-IP}
Let $g\colon\hatC\to\hatC$ be a rational map of degree at least~$2$, and consider the CCS with states~$\hatC$, the predicates $P_1,P_2,P_3$ of Section~\ref{sec:examples}, and the semigroup of iterates of~$g$.
There are $j\in\{1,2,3\}$, a positive integer~$n$ and reals $a<b$ such that the functions $P_j\circ g^{nk}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, form an independent sequence:
for all finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is $z\in\hatC$ with $P_j(g^{nk}(z))\le a$ for $k\in I$ and $P_j(g^{nk}(z))\ge b$ for $k\in J$.
In particular, the CCS is not~$\NIP$, and neither are the Newton CCSs for $q(z)=z^3-2z+2$ and for $u(z)=z^3-1$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Choose distinct points $z_0,z_1\in\mathcal{J}_g$ and an index $j$ with $P_j(z_0)\neq P_j(z_1)$, say $P_j(z_0)<P_j(z_1)$, and reals $a<b$ strictly between these two values.
By continuity, the points $z_0$ and $z_1$ have closed neighborhoods $U_0$ and~$U_1$ such that $P_j\le a$ on~$U_0$ and $P_j\ge b$ on~$U_1$, and we may choose $n$ with $g^n(U_0)\supseteq\mathcal{J}_g$ and $g^n(U_1)\supseteq\mathcal{J}_g$.
Let $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ be finite and disjoint, and let $m$ exceed all their elements;
put $U^{(k)}=U_0$ for $k\in I$ and $U^{(k)}=U_1$ for $k\notin I$.
Let $C_m=U^{(m)}\cap\mathcal{J}_g$ and, for $k=m-1,\dots,0$, let $C_k=U^{(k)}\cap\mathcal{J}_g\cap g^{-n}(C_{k+1})$.
Each $C_k$ is nonempty: since $g^n(U^{(k)})\supseteq\mathcal{J}_g\supseteq C_{k+1}$, some point of $U^{(k)}$ is mapped by $g^n$ into~$C_{k+1}$, and that point lies in $\mathcal{J}_g$ because $g^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_g)=\mathcal{J}_g$.
Every $z\in C_0$ satisfies $g^{nk}(z)\in U^{(k)}$ for $k\le m$, and hence $P_j(g^{nk}(z))\le a$ for $k\in I$ and $P_j(g^{nk}(z))\ge b$ for $k\in J$.
For the last assertion, note that the Newton maps $N_q(z)=(2z^3-2)/(3z^2-2)$ and $N_u(z)=(2z^3+1)/(3z^2)$ of Section~\ref{sec:examples} have degree~$3$, since in each quotient the numerator and the denominator have no common zero.
\end{proof}

\emph{The logistic map at $r=4$.}
The doubling map also governs the logistic map $f_4(x)=4x(1-x)$ of
Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP3}, as Figure~\ref{fig:doubling} illustrates.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:logistic-four}
The functions $f_4^{3k}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, form an independent sequence on
$[0,1]$: for all finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is
$x\in[0,1]$ with $f_4^{3k}(x)\ge\frac34$ for $k\in I$ and
$f_4^{3k}(x)\le\frac14$ for $k\in J$.
In particular, the logistic CCS for $r=4$ is not $\NIP$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let $h(\theta)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$ for $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$.
Then $f_4(h(\theta))=4\sin^2(\theta/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)=\sin^2\theta=h(2\theta)$, so $f_4^{n}(h(\theta))=h(2^n\theta)=\sin^2(2^{n-1}\theta)$.
Write $\theta=2\pi\sum_{j\ge1}x_j2^{-j}$ with bits $x_j$.
The fractional part of $2^{3k}\theta/2\pi$ has binary expansion $0.x_{3k+1}x_{3k+2}x_{3k+3}\cdots$, and $2^{3k-1}\theta$ differs from $\pi$ times this fractional part by an integer multiple of~$\pi$.
If $(x_{3k+1},x_{3k+2},x_{3k+3})=(0,1,1)$, this fractional part lies in $[\frac38,\frac12]$, so $f_4^{3k}(h(\theta))=\sin^2(2^{3k-1}\theta)\ge\sin^2\frac{3\pi}8>\frac34$; if the block is $(0,0,0)$, it lies in $[0,\frac18]$, so $f_4^{3k}(h(\theta))\le\sin^2\frac{\pi}8<\frac14$.
Choosing the block $(0,1,1)$ for $k\in I$ and $(0,0,0)$ for $k\in J$, and putting $x=h(\theta)$, gives the required point.
\end{proof}

Thus the logistic family, which is stable for $0<r\le1$ and $\NIP_3$ but not stable for $1<r\le3$ (see Proposition~\ref{prop:logistic}), leaves the hierarchy at $r=4$.
The passage takes place at the Feigenbaum parameter $r_\infty\approx3.5699$, the limit of the cascade of period doublings, and the family skips the intermediate levels: in Proposition~\ref{prop:logistic-all} below we show that it remains $\NIP_3$ below~$r_\infty$ and has the independence property beyond it, periodic windows included, and in Remark~\ref{rem:logistic-feigenbaum} we sketch the argument that it is still $\NIP_3$ at~$r_\infty$.
The arguments rest on the kneading theory of Milnor and Thurston~\cite{MilnorThurston1988} and on the theory of interval maps all of whose periodic points have periods that are powers of~$2$~\cite{BlockCoppel1992,deMeloVanStrien1993}.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:logistic-all}
Let $r_\infty$ be the supremum of the parameters $r\in(0,4]$ for which every periodic point of~$f_r$ has a power of~$2$ as its period.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item
  For $3<r<r_\infty$, the logistic CCS is $\NIP_3$ but not stable.
\item
  For $r_\infty<r\le4$, there are a positive integer~$n$ and reals $a<b$ such that the functions $f_r^{nk}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, form an independent sequence:
  for all finite disjoint sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is $x\in[0,1]$ with $f_r^{nk}(x)\le a$ for $k\in I$ and $f_r^{nk}(x)\ge b$ for $k\in J$.
  In particular, the logistic CCS is not~$\NIP$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Write $f=f_r$.
We use three classical facts~\cite{MilnorThurston1988,BlockCoppel1992,deMeloVanStrien1993}.
First, by the monotonicity of the kneading invariant in the logistic family, every periodic point of~$f$ has a power of~$2$ as its period if and only if $r\le r_\infty$, and for $r<r_\infty$ only finitely many periods, and hence only finitely many periodic points, occur.
Second, for $2<r<4$, every limit point of an orbit that starts in $(0,1)$ lies in the interval $[f^2(\frac12),f(\frac12)]$, which does not contain~$0$.
Third, a continuous map of a compact interval with only finitely many periodic points has every orbit asymptotic to a periodic orbit~\cite{BlockCoppel1992}.

(i) Suppose that $3<r<r_\infty$, and let $N$ be a common multiple of the periods of~$f$, which are powers of~$2$.
If the orbit of~$x$ is asymptotic to the periodic orbit~$\mathcal{Q}$, then $f^{Nk}(x)$ converges to a point of~$\mathcal{Q}$, since $f^N$ fixes $\mathcal{Q}$ pointwise and, by uniform continuity, the point of~$\mathcal{Q}$ nearest to $f^i(x)$ is eventually carried by~$f$ to the point of~$\mathcal{Q}$ nearest to $f^{i+1}(x)$;
let $\ell_r(x)$ be this limit.
Then $f^{Nk+i}\to f^i\circ\ell_r$ pointwise for $0\le i<N$, and every limit of the iterates along a nonprincipal ultrafilter on~$\mathbb{N}$ is one of these $N$ maps, each of which is of Baire class~$1$ as the pointwise limit of a sequence of iterates.
Hence $E([0,1],f)$ is countable, and therefore metrizable, and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS is $\NIP_3$.
The map $\ell_r$ fixes~$0$ and maps $(0,1)$ into the interval $[f^2(\frac12),f(\frac12)]$, so it is discontinuous at~$0$, and the CCS is not stable.

(ii) Suppose that $r_\infty<r\le4$.
By the first fact, $f$ has a periodic point whose period is not a power of~$2$, and hence, by a theorem of Block and Coppel~\cite[Chapter~II]{BlockCoppel1992}, some iterate $f^n$ is strictly turbulent, that is, there are disjoint closed intervals $H_0$ and~$H_1$, with $H_0$ to the left of~$H_1$, such that $f^n(H_0)$ and $f^n(H_1)$ both contain $H_0\cup H_1$.
Let $a=\max H_0$ and $b=\min H_1$, let $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ be finite and disjoint, and let $m$ exceed all their elements;
put $H^{(k)}=H_0$ for $k\in I$ and $H^{(k)}=H_1$ for $k\notin I$.
Let $C_m=H^{(m)}$ and, for $k=m-1,\dots,0$, let $C_k=H^{(k)}\cap f^{-n}(C_{k+1})$;
each $C_k$ is nonempty, since $f^n(H^{(k)})\supseteq C_{k+1}$.
Every $x\in C_0$ satisfies $f^{nk}(x)\in H^{(k)}$ for $k\le m$, and hence $f^{nk}(x)\le a$ for $k\in I$ and $f^{nk}(x)\ge b$ for $k\in J$.
\end{proof}

\begin{rem}\label{rem:logistic-feigenbaum}
At the Feigenbaum parameter itself the logistic CCS is again $\NIP_3$ but not stable; we sketch the argument, which rests on the structure of infinitely renormalizable maps~\cite{BlockCoppel1992,deMeloVanStrien1993}.
For $r=r_\infty$, the map $f=f_r$ has, for every $m\in\mathbb{N}$, a cycle of $2^m$ pairwise disjoint closed intervals, permuted cyclically by~$f$ and contained in the intervals of the cycle of level $m-1$;
the lengths of these intervals tend to~$0$ as $m\to\infty$, and every orbit that starts in $(0,1)$ either lands on a periodic orbit or enters the cycle of every level.
Label the intervals of the cycle of level~$m$ by the residues modulo~$2^m$, in such a way that $f$ adds~$1$ to the label, let $\mathcal{U}$ be a nonprincipal ultrafilter on~$\mathbb{N}$, and let $\varsigma$ be the limit of $n$ along~$\mathcal{U}$ in the ring $\mathbb{Z}_2$ of $2$-adic integers.
If the orbit of~$x$ lands on a periodic orbit, of period~$2^m$, then $f^n(x)$ is eventually determined by the residue of $n$ modulo~$2^m$;
otherwise, for each~$m$, the point $f^n(x)$ eventually lies in the interval of level~$m$ whose label differs from $n$, modulo~$2^m$, by a residue that depends on $x$ and~$m$ but not on~$n$, and these intervals shrink to a point.
In either case $f^n(x)$ converges along~$\mathcal{U}$, to a limit $\ell^{\varsigma}(x)$ that depends only on~$\varsigma$;
the map $\ell^{\varsigma}$ is the pointwise limit of the iterates $f^{n_j}$ along any sequence of natural numbers $n_j$ that tends to infinity and converges to~$\varsigma$ in~$\mathbb{Z}_2$, and it is therefore of Baire class~$1$.
Hence $E([0,1],f)$ consists of the iterates and the maps $\ell^{\varsigma}$, $\varsigma\in\mathbb{Z}_2$.
The rational points of $[0,1]$, together with the point~$\frac12$, separate the members of $E([0,1],f)$:
distinct iterates are continuous and differ at a rational point;
an iterate $f^n$ and a map $\ell^{\varsigma}$ differ at every rational point $x>0$ so small that $f^n(x)<f^2(\frac12)$, since $\ell^{\varsigma}$ maps $(0,1)$ into $[f^2(\frac12),f(\frac12)]$;
and two maps $\ell^{\varsigma}$ and $\ell^{\varsigma'}$ with $\varsigma\neq\varsigma'$ differ at~$\frac12$, whose orbit lands on no periodic orbit, because for $\varsigma\not\equiv\varsigma'$ modulo~$2^m$ the points $\ell^{\varsigma}(\frac12)$ and $\ell^{\varsigma'}(\frac12)$ lie in distinct intervals of level~$m$.
As in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}(i), the space $E([0,1],f)$ is therefore metrizable, and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS is $\NIP_3$;
it is not stable, since each map $\ell^{\varsigma}$ fixes~$0$ and is discontinuous there.
\end{rem}

\emph{The Thue--Morse sequence.}
Even a sequence produced by a simple substitution rule can be wild.
The Thue--Morse sequence $t_n$ is the parity of the number of ones in the binary
expansion of~$n$; it is the limit of the words obtained from $0$ by the
substitution $0\mapsto01$, $1\mapsto10$.
The two-sided sequences all of whose finite blocks occur in $(t_n)$ form the
Thue--Morse subshift $X_{TM}\subseteq2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, on which the shift $\sigma$ is a
homeomorphism.
Glasner and Megrelishvili observed that the Morse minimal system, which consists of the subshift $X_{TM}$ and the action of~$\mathbb{Z}$ generated by~$\sigma$, is not tame~\cite{GlasnerMegrelishvili2018}.
In Appendix~\ref{app:thue-morse} we give a self-contained proof, which relies only on the structure theorem for tame minimal systems.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS with states $X_{TM}$, the coordinate predicates and the group
$\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is therefore not $\NIP$.
The cascade CCS of~$\sigma$, whose computations are only the iterates $\sigma^n$ with $n\in\mathbb{N}$, is not $\NIP$ either.
Indeed, the reversal of two-sided sequences maps $X_{TM}$ onto itself and conjugates~$\sigma$ to its inverse, and it therefore turns an independent sequence of functions $f\circ\sigma^{n_k}$ with negative exponents~$n_k$ into one with positive exponents.
Both facts are stated, with complete proofs, in Proposition~\ref{prop:thue-morse}.

\section{Randomized versions of NIP and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}
\label{sec:measure-theoretic-NIP}

Replacing topology by measure, we dispense in this section with the countability of the set $\mathcal{P}$ of predicates, on which the theory of Sections~\ref{sec:Classification}--\ref{sec:iterative} depends.
In those sections we studied deep computations as \emph{topological} limits of computations;
under the NIP, they are of the first Baire class on the type shards, and on each shard each of them is the limit of a sequence of computations.
That theory rests on the type shards being Polish, and hence on the countability of~$\mathcal{P}$, which is used in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}.
Instead of asking that a deep computation be of the first Baire class, we ask that it be \emph{universally measurable}, that is, measurable with respect to every Radon probability measure on the type shards;
by Luzin's theorem, each output feature of such a deep computation is then, for every such measure, continuous on a closed subset of the shard whose complement has arbitrarily small measure, and hence, by the Stone--Weierstrass theorem, a uniform limit of polynomials in the features on that subset.
We call such deep computations \emph{universally Monte Carlo computable} (see Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable}).
The role of the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy is then taken by its measure-theoretic form (see Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F}), and the role of $\NIP$ is shared with \emph{Talagrand stability}, a measure-theoretic condition introduced by Talagrand in his study of Pettis integration (see Definition~\ref{def:Talagrand-stable}).

The main results of the section are Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}, which characterizes the NIP by the universal measurability of the restrictions of ultracomputations to the type shards and derives from it Monte Carlo computability, and Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}, which derives Monte Carlo computability from universal Talagrand stability.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Talagrand-countable}, the two hypotheses coincide for countable sets of computations (see Corollary~\ref{cor:NIP-Talagrand-computations}).

\subsection{NIP and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}

Of the two routes to universal Monte Carlo computability followed in this section, the first starts from the NIP and passes through universal measurability, the measure-theoretic counterpart of membership in the first Baire class, which we introduce here.
It leads to Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}, by which the deep computations of a confined set of computations with the NIP on shards and features are universally Monte Carlo computable.
By Fact~\ref{baire}, a function $f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}$ on a perfectly normal space $X$ is of Baire class~1 if and only if $f^{-1}[U]$ is an $F_\sigma$ subset of $X$ for every open $U\subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
The following definition replaces the $F_\sigma$ condition by measurability with respect to every Radon measure.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:universal-measurability}
  Given a Hausdorff space $X$ and a measurable space $(Y,\Sigma)$, we say that $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is \emph{universally measurable} (with respect to $\Sigma$) if $f^{-1}(E)$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$ and every $E\in\Sigma$.
  When $Y=\mathbb{R}$, we will always take $\Sigma=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{defn}

If $X$ is a compact Hausdorff space, then Radon measures on $X$ are finite, and every nonzero Radon measure on $X$ becomes a probability measure after normalization.
Moreover, the sets $E\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ whose preimages under a given function are $\mu$-measurable form a $\sigma$-algebra, which contains the Borel sets as soon as it contains the open sets.
Together, these two observations yield the following simplification of Definition~\ref{def:universal-measurability} for compact spaces.

\begin{rem}
  \label{rem:probability}
  If $X$ is compact, then a function $f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is universally measurable if and only if $f^{-1}(U)$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on $X$ and every open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$.
\end{rem}

Universal measurability thus asks for measurability with respect to every Radon measure on the domain, that is, with respect to every admissible way of measuring its subsets;
an account of universally measurable sets and functions may be found in the \emph{Handbook of measure theory}~\cite[Chapters 1 and 2]{Pap:2002}.

\begin{notn}
  Following Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}, we denote by $M_r(X)$ the collection of all universally measurable real-valued functions on~$X$;
  for a Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$, we denote by $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$ the collection of all $\mu$-measurable real-valued functions on~$X$.
\end{notn}

The maps that arise in the study of deep computations, transitions and deep computations themselves among them, send a state space $L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ into $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$.
The product space $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ carries two natural $\sigma$-algebras:
the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, generated by the open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$,
and the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra, generated by the subbasic open sets $\pi_P^{-1}(U)$ with $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $U\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ open.
In general, the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra is strictly smaller, but the two coincide when $\mathcal{P}$ is countable.
We use the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra to define universally measurable maps into $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$, because of the following characterization.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:coordinated_univ_meas}
  Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and $Y=\prod_{i\in I}Y_i$ be any product of measurable spaces $(Y_i,\Sigma_i)$ for $i\in I$.
  Let $\Sigma_Y$ be the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra generated by the measurable spaces $(Y_i,\Sigma_i)$, and let $\pi_i\colon Y\to Y_i$ be the projections.
  The following are equivalent for  $f:X\rightarrow Y$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is universally measurable (with respect to $\Sigma_Y$).
  \item
    $\pi_i\circ f:X\rightarrow Y_i$ is universally measurable (with respect to $\Sigma_i$) for all $i\in I$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  The implication (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) holds because each projection $\pi_i$ is measurable and $(\pi_i\circ f)^{-1}(E)=f^{-1}(\pi_i^{-1}(E))$ for every $E\in\Sigma_i$.
  To prove (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i), fix a Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$, and note that the sets $C\subseteq Y$ for which $f^{-1}(C)$ is $\mu$-measurable form a $\sigma$-algebra.
  This $\sigma$-algebra contains every measurable cylinder $C=\prod_{i\in I}C_i$:
  if $J$ is the finite set of indices $i\in I$ with $C_i\neq Y_i$, then $f^{-1}(C)=\bigcap_{i\in J}(\pi_i\circ f)^{-1}(C_i)$ is $\mu$-measurable by~(ii).
  Since the measurable cylinders generate $\Sigma_Y$, the set $f^{-1}(C)$ is $\mu$-measurable for every $C\in\Sigma_Y$.
\end{proof}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinated_univ_meas}, the universal measurability of a map into $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ can be checked feature by feature, just as membership in the first Baire class can (see Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications}).

We saw in Section~\ref{sec:prelim} that the NIP corresponds to relative compactness in the space of functions of Baire class~1 (see Theorems~\ref{thm:BFT}, \ref{thm:BFT2} and~\ref{thm:Generalized-BFT}), and in Section~\ref{sec:Classification} that, when the set $\mathcal{P}$ of predicates is countable, this correspondence extends to deep computations (see Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
The following theorem, valid on every Hausdorff space, characterizes the NIP in terms of universal measurability; applied to the type shards, it requires no countability of~$\mathcal{P}$.

\begin{thm}
  [Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand {\cite[Theorem~2F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}}]
  \label{thm:BFT-2F}
  Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space, and let $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\overline{A}\subseteq M_r(X)$.
  \item
    For every compact $K\subseteq X$, $A|_K$ satisfies the NIP.
  \item
    For every Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$, $A$ is relatively countably compact in~$\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$, i.e., every countable subset of $A$ has a limit point in $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F} leads to a measure-theoretic counterpart of computability, the universal Monte Carlo computability of Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable} below, whose motivation lies in two classical facts.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  Littlewood’s second principle, that every Lebesgue measurable function is “nearly continuous”, is made precise by Luzin’s theorem:
  if $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ is a finite Radon measure space and $Y$ is a second-countable topological space (e.g., $Y=\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ with $\mathcal{P}$ countable) with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra, then a function $f\colon X\to Y$ is measurable if and only if, for every $E\in\Sigma$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a closed set $F\subseteq E$ such that the restriction $f|_F$ is continuous and $\mu(E\setminus F) < \varepsilon$.
\item
  Computability of a transform is characterized by its continuous extendibility to the space of types (see Section~\ref{sec:computability});
  this characterization is at the core of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.
\end{enumerate}
Taken together, the two facts justify the definition, for
if the extension of a transform is universally measurable on a type shard, then, by Luzin’s theorem, for every Radon probability measure on the shard each output feature is continuous on a closed subset of the shard whose measure is as close to~$1$ as desired, and there, by the Stone--Weierstrass theorem, it is a uniform limit of polynomials in the features.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:universally-essentially-computable}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS, and let $\mathscr{R}$ be a collection of sizers.
  A transform $f\colon L\to\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ is \emph{universally Monte Carlo computable on~$\mathscr{R}$} if it has an extension $\tilde f\colon\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\to \mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ with the following property:
  for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$ there is a sizer $s_\bullet$ such that the restriction $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_{\bullet}]}\colon\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\to\mathcal{L}[s_\bullet]$ is universally measurable,
  that is, $\pi_P\circ \tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_{\bullet}]}\colon\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\to [-s_P,s_P]$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ and every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
\end{defn}

The deep computations of a set of computations confined by an exhaustive collection of sizers are transforms $L\to\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ (see Proposition~\ref{prop:confined-compact}).
Since each type shard $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ is compact, Remark~\ref{rem:probability} shows that Radon probability measures suffice in Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable}, in place of general Radon measures.

\begin{thm}
  \label{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers, and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $\mathscr{R}$-confined.
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ satisfies the NIP for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and all $r_{\bullet}\in \mathscr{R}$, where $\pi_P\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the projection onto the coordinate~$P$;
  \item
    for every $r_\bullet\in\mathscr{R}$, every member of $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is universally measurable, as a map into $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ with the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra.
  \end{enumerate}
  If these conditions hold, then every deep computation in $\overline{\Delta}$ is universally Monte Carlo computable on~$\mathscr{R}$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
  Fix $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$, and write $K=\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$, a compact subset of $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]^{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$, and $A_P=\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$.
  By the Extendibility Axiom and confinement, $A_P$ is a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on the compact space $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$, and, since the map $g\mapsto\pi_P\circ g$ is continuous, the closure of~$A_P$ is the compact set $\{\pi_P\circ g:g\in K\}$.
  Assume~(i).
  The restriction of $A_P$ to $L[r_\bullet]$ is $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$, which has the NIP, and $L[r_\bullet]$ is dense in $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$;
  by Lemma~\ref{lem:NIP-and-closure}, the set $A_P$ itself has the NIP, and so does its restriction to every compact subset of $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$;
  Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F} then gives $\overline{A_P}\subseteq M_r(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$.
  Since $P$ is arbitrary, Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinated_univ_meas} shows that every member of~$K$ is universally measurable, and (ii) follows.
  Conversely, assume~(ii).
  By Proposition~\ref{prop:coordinated_univ_meas}, every member of $\overline{A_P}$ is universally measurable, and, by the implication (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F}, applied to the compact set $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ itself, the set $A_P$ has the NIP;
  hence so does its restriction $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$, and (i) holds.
  Finally, let $f\in\overline{\Delta}$, and let $\tilde f$ be the extension of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension}.
  For every $r_\bullet\in\mathscr{R}$, the restriction $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ belongs to $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$, and it is therefore universally measurable when (ii) holds;
  since $\tilde f$ maps $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ into itself, the deep computation $f$ satisfies Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable} with $s_\bullet=r_\bullet$ for every $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{rem}\label{rem:doubling-rationals}
Condition~(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} concerns all the members of the sets $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$, that is, the restrictions of ultracomputations to the type shards, and the equivalence fails if (ii) is weakened to the universal Monte Carlo computability of every deep computation, which requires only that some extension be universally measurable.
Let $L=\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{T}$, with the predicates $\cos2\pi x$ and $\sin2\pi x$, and let $\Gamma$ consist of the iterates of the doubling map $x\mapsto2x$.
The space of types is the circle~$\mathbb{T}$, and each computation extends to the continuous map $x\mapsto2^nx$ of~$\mathbb{T}$;
thus the Extendibility Axiom holds, and the single sizer with both bounds equal to~$1$ forms an exhaustive collection that confines~$\Gamma$.
By the paragraph that follows Proposition~\ref{prop:Newton-wild}, the iterates of the doubling map of~$\mathbb{T}$ do not have the NIP, and, by Lemma~\ref{lem:NIP-and-closure}, neither do their restrictions to the dense set~$L$.
Every deep computation $f\colon L\to\mathbb{T}$, however, extends to the map that agrees with $f$ on~$L$ and takes the value~$0$ on $\mathbb{T}\setminus L$, and this extension is Borel, since $L$ is countable;
hence every deep computation is universally Monte Carlo computable.
\end{rem}

\subsection{Talagrand stability and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}

The second route to universal Monte Carlo computability starts from a notion closely related to the NIP, which Talagrand introduced in his study of Pettis integration~\cite{talagrand1984pettis};
we follow this route here, and at the end of the subsection we compare the two.
Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, let $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ be a set of functions, and let $\mu$ be a Radon probability measure on $X$.
For a $\mu$-measurable set $E\subseteq X$, a positive integer $k$ and real numbers $a<b$, writing the points of $E^{2k}$ as $x=(x_0,\dots,x_{2k-1})$, let
\[
  D_k(A,E,a,b)=\bigcup_{f\in A}\{x\in E^{2k}:f(x_{2i})\leq a, \hspace{1mm} f(x_{2i+1})\geq b \hspace{1mm}{\text{ for all }i<k} \}.
\]

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:Talagrand-stable}
  The set $A$ is \emph{Talagrand $\mu$-stable} if, for every $\mu$-measurable set $E\subseteq X$ of positive measure and all reals $a<b$, there is an integer $k\geq 1$ such that
  \[
    (\mu^{2k})^*(D_k(A,E,a,b))<(\mu(E))^{2k},
  \]
  where $(\mu^{2k})^*$ denotes the outer measure associated with the product measure $\mu^{2k}$ on~$X^{2k}$.
\end{defn}

The outer measure is needed in Definition~\ref{def:Talagrand-stable} because $D_k(A,E,a,b)$ need not be measurable.
The set $D_k(A,E,a,b)$ is measurable when $A$ is a countable set of $\mu$-measurable functions, since it is then a countable union of measurable sets.

\begin{rems}
  \label{rem:Talagrand-stability}
  Let $X$, $\mu$, $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ and $E\subseteq X$ be as above, and let $k\ge1$ be an integer and $a<b$ be reals.
  The following hold.
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    If $A\subseteq B\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$, then
    \[
      D_k(A,E,a,b)\subseteq D_k(B,E,a,b).
    \]

  \item
    If $a<a'<b'<b$, then
    \[
      D_k(\overline{A},E,a,b)\subseteq D_k(A,E,a',b').
    \]
    [\emph{Proof}:
      If $x\in D_k(\overline{A},E,a,b)$, then there is $f\in\overline{A}$ such that $f(x_{2i})\leq a<a'$ and $f(x_{2i+1})\geq b>b'$ for all $i<k$.
      By the definition of the topology of pointwise convergence, there is $g\in A$ such that $g(x_{2i})<a'$ and $g(x_{2i+1})>b'$ for all $i<k$;
      hence $x\in D_k(A,E,a',b')$.]

  \item
    Every subset of a Talagrand $\mu$-stable set is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, by~(1).

  \item
    If $A$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then so is $\overline{A}$, by~(2).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{rems}

In Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo} below we show that the deep computations of a confined set of computations are universally Monte Carlo computable when, for every feature and every type shard, the corresponding set of functions is Talagrand $\mu$-stable for every Radon probability measure~$\mu$, that is, \emph{universally Talagrand stable} in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:universal-Talagrand} below.
The proof of the corollary rests on Lemma~\ref{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}, by which the pointwise closure of a Talagrand $\mu$-stable set consists of $\mu$-measurable functions.
The lemma, in turn, rests on the following characterization of measurable functions (compare Fact~\ref{baire}).

\begin{fact}[{\cite[Lemma~413G]{fremlin2003vol4}}]\label{fact:meas-fns-charn}
  Let $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ be a measure space, and let $\mathcal{K}\subseteq\Sigma$ be a collection of measurable sets such that the following conditions hold:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is complete, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(E)=0$ and $F\subseteq E$ we have $F\in\Sigma$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is semi-finite, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(E)=\infty$ there exists $F\subseteq E$ such that $F\in\Sigma$ and $0<\mu(F)<\infty$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is saturated, i.e., $E\in\Sigma$ if and only if $E\cap F\in\Sigma$ for all $F\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(F)<\infty$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is inner regular with respect to $\mathcal{K}$, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$
    \[
      \mu(E)=\sup\{\mu(K):K\in\mathcal{K} \text{ and } K\subseteq E\}.
    \]
  \end{itemize}
  (In particular, if $X$ is compact Hausdorff, $\mathcal{K}$ is the collection of compact subsets of $X$, $\mu$ is a Radon probability measure on $X$, and $\Sigma$ is the completion of the Borel $\sigma$-algebra by $\mu$, then all these conditions hold.)
  Then a function $f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}$ is measurable if and only if,
  for every $K\in\mathcal{K}$ with $0<\mu(K)<\infty$ and all reals $a<b$, either $\mu^*(K_{\le a})<\mu(K)$ or $\mu^*(K_{\ge b})<\mu(K)$,
  where $K_{\le a}=\{x\in K:f(x)\leq a\}$ and $K_{\ge b}=\{x\in K:f(x)\geq b\}$.
\end{fact}

We first show that Talagrand stability passes to the pointwise closure and forces measurability.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, let $\mu$ be a Radon probability measure on~$X$, and let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$ be a set of functions.
  If $A$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then $\overline{A}$ is also Talagrand $\mu$-stable and $\overline{A}\subseteq\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  By Remarks~\ref{rem:Talagrand-stability}, the closure $\overline{A}$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, and hence so is $\{f\}$ for every $f\in\overline{A}$.
  It therefore suffices to show that if $\{f\}$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then $f$ is $\mu$-measurable.
  Suppose that $f$ is not $\mu$-measurable.
  Then Fact~\ref{fact:meas-fns-charn} gives a compact set $E$ of positive measure and reals $a<b$ with $\mu^*(E_{\le a})=\mu^*(E_{\ge b})=\mu(E)$, where $E_{\le a}=\{x\in E: f(x)\leq a\}$ and $E_{\ge b}=\{x\in E: f(x)\geq b\}$.
  For every $k\ge1$ we have $(E_{\le a}\times E_{\ge b})^k\subseteq D_k(\{f\},E,a,b)$, and this product has outer measure $(\mu(E))^{2k}$ (see~\cite{talagrand1984pettis});
  hence $\{f\}$ is not Talagrand $\mu$-stable.
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}\label{def:universal-Talagrand}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space.
  A set $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$ is \emph{universally Talagrand stable} if it is Talagrand $\mu$-stable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on~$X$.
\end{defn}

For pointwise bounded sets of continuous functions on a compact space, universal Talagrand stability implies the NIP.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, and let $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  If $A$ is universally Talagrand stable, then $A$ satisfies the NIP.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  By Lemma~\ref{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}, the closure $\overline{A}$ is contained in $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$ for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on~$X$, that is, $\overline{A}\subseteq M_r(X)$.
  By the implication (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F}, applied with $K=X$, the set $A$ satisfies the NIP.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}
  \label{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers, and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $\mathscr{R}$-confined.
  If $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is universally Talagrand stable for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and all $r_{\bullet}\in \mathscr{R}$, then every deep computation in $\overline{\Delta}$ is universally Monte Carlo computable on~$\mathscr{R}$.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
  Let $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $r_\bullet\in \mathscr{R}$ be given.
  By the Extendibility Axiom and confinement, $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on the compact space~$\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$, and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP}, it has the NIP;
  hence so does its restriction $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$.
  Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} now completes the proof.
\end{proof}

In the context of deep computations we have thus found two routes to universal Monte Carlo computability, the NIP and universal Talagrand stability, and the second implies the first (see Proposition~\ref{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP}).
For countable sets of measurable functions, the following two results show that whether the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability depends on the axioms of set theory.
On the one hand, it is consistent with the Zermelo--Fraenkel axioms of set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC) that, for such sets, the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability.

\begin{thm}[Talagrand {\cite[Theorem~9-3-1(a)]{talagrand1984pettis}}]
  \label{thm:NIP-implies-Talagrand-stab}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, and let $A\subseteq M_r(X)$ be countable and pointwise bounded.
  Assume that $[0,1]$ is not the union of fewer than $2^{\aleph_0}$ closed sets of Lebesgue measure zero.
  If $A$ satisfies the NIP, then $A$ is universally Talagrand stable.
\end{thm}

(The hypothesis on $[0,1]$ follows, for example, from the Continuum Hypothesis.)

On the other hand, for Lebesgue measure, the failure of this implication is also consistent.

\begin{thm}[Fremlin and Shelah~\cite{fremlin1993pointwise}]
  It is consistent with ZFC that there exists a countable pointwise bounded set of Lebesgue measurable functions with the NIP that is not Talagrand stable with respect to Lebesgue measure.
\end{thm}

Both results concern countable sets of measurable functions.
For countable sets of \emph{continuous} functions on a compact space, the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability in ZFC, without any further set-theoretic hypothesis (see Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Talagrand-countable} below);
since the Extendibility Axiom makes computations continuous on the type shards, the two routes to universal Monte Carlo computability therefore coincide for countable sets of computations.
After a reduction to a compact metrizable space, this implication can be derived from two results on the Glivenko--Cantelli property of a family of functions, the almost sure convergence of empirical means to expectations, uniformly over the family:
a theorem of van Handel~\cite[Theorem~1.3]{vanHandel2013}, by which a separable, uniformly bounded family of measurable functions on a standard Borel space has this property for every probability measure if and only if it contains no Boolean $\sigma$-independent sequence,
and a theorem of Talagrand~\cite{talagrand1987glivenko}, which identifies, for uniformly bounded families, the Glivenko--Cantelli families for a measure~$\mu$ with the Talagrand $\mu$-stable ones (see also~\cite[\S465]{fremlin2003vol4}).
We give a direct proof, which reduces the question to a compact metrizable space and then applies Fact~\ref{baire} to a cluster point of the family.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:NIP-Talagrand-countable}
Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space, and let $A\subseteq\Cp(X)$ be countable and pointwise bounded.
If $A$ satisfies the NIP, then $A$ is universally Talagrand stable.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Suppose that $A$ satisfies the NIP, let $\mu$ be a Radon probability measure on~$X$, and suppose, towards a contradiction, that $A$ is not Talagrand $\mu$-stable.
Then there are a $\mu$-measurable set $E\subseteq X$ of positive measure and reals $a<b$ such that $\mu^{2k}(D_k(A,E,a,b))=(\mu(E))^{2k}$ for every $k\ge1$;
the sets $D_k(A,E,a,b)$ are measurable, since $A$ is countable and its members are continuous.
We first transfer the situation to a compact metrizable space.
The map $\Psi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^A$, $\Psi(x)=(f(x))_{f\in A}$, is continuous, its image $Y=\Psi(X)$ is compact and metrizable because $A$ is countable, and each $f\in A$ factors as $f=\hat f\circ\Psi$, where $\hat f\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}$ is the restriction of the coordinate projection.
The set $\hat A=\{\hat f:f\in A\}\subseteq\Cp(Y)$ is pointwise bounded, and it satisfies the NIP, because $Y^{\hat f_I}_{\le a'}\cap Y^{\hat f_J}_{\ge b'}=\Psi(X^{f_I}_{\le a'}\cap X^{f_J}_{\ge b'})$ for every sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in~$A$, all finite sets $I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ and all reals $a'<b'$.
Let $\nu$ be the Borel probability measure on~$Y$ given by $\nu(B)=\mu(E\cap\Psi^{-1}(B))/\mu(E)$.
Since $D_k(A,E,a,b)=E^{2k}\cap(\Psi^{2k})^{-1}(D_k(\hat A,Y,a,b))$, where $\Psi^{2k}$ applies $\Psi$ to each coordinate, we obtain $\nu^{2k}(D_k(\hat A,Y,a,b))=1$ for every $k\ge1$.

Now consider the product measure $\nu^{\mathbb{N}}$ on $Y^{\mathbb{N}}$, under which the coordinates $y_n$ of a sequence $(y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ are independent, each with distribution~$\nu$, and let $Z$ be the support of~$\nu$, a nonempty closed and hence Polish subspace of~$Y$.
For each $k\ge1$, almost every sequence admits some $\hat f\in\hat A$ with $\hat f(y_{2i})\le a$ and $\hat f(y_{2i+1})\ge b$ for all $i<k$, since this event has probability $\nu^{2k}(D_k(\hat A,Y,a,b))=1$;
moreover, since $Y$ has a countable base and every basic open set that meets~$Z$ has positive measure, for almost every sequence both $\{y_{2i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}$ and $\{y_{2i+1}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}$ are dense subsets of~$Z$.
Fix a sequence $(y_n)$ with all these properties, and, for each $k\ge1$, choose $\hat f_k\in\hat A$ with $\hat f_k(y_{2i})\le a$ and $\hat f_k(y_{2i+1})\ge b$ for all $i<k$.
Since $\hat A$ is pointwise bounded, the sequence $(\hat f_k)$ has a cluster point $g$ in~$\mathbb{R}^Y$, and $g\in\mathrm{B}_1(Y)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2}.
For every~$i$, the inequalities $\hat f_k(y_{2i})\le a$ and $\hat f_k(y_{2i+1})\ge b$ hold for all $k>i$, and therefore $g(y_{2i})\le a$ and $g(y_{2i+1})\ge b$.
By condition~(3) of Fact~\ref{baire}, applied to the Polish space~$Y$ and its closed subset~$Z$, the restriction $g|_Z$ has a point of continuity~$z$;
yet every neighborhood of $z$ in~$Z$ contains points $y_{2i}$, at which $g\le a$, and points $y_{2i+1}$, at which $g\ge b$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}\label{cor:NIP-Talagrand-computations}
Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, let $\mathscr{R}$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers, and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be countable and $\mathscr{R}$-confined.
For all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $r_\bullet\in\mathscr{R}$, the set $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is universally Talagrand stable if and only if $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ satisfies the NIP.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
By the Extendibility Axiom and confinement, $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is a countable, pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on the compact space~$\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$, in which $L[r_\bullet]$ is dense.
If this set is universally Talagrand stable, it has the NIP by Proposition~\ref{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP}, and so does its restriction to~$L[r_\bullet]$.
Conversely, if $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ has the NIP, then so does $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:NIP-and-closure}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Talagrand-countable} applies.
\end{proof}

For countable sets of computations, Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Talagrand-countable} thus supersedes Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-implies-Talagrand-stab} and its set-theoretic hypothesis, and the example of Fremlin and Shelah, a set of measurable functions, has no counterpart among them.

\section{Open questions}
\label{sec:open-questions}

We close with the questions that the paper leaves open, in the order in which they arise from its sections.
The first concerns the hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:trichotomy}, whose levels were defined through the topology of the space of deep computations;
Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests} describes them by the number of tests needed to tell deep computations apart, but these descriptions still involve infinitely many tests.

\begin{question}\label{q:NIP-levels}
  Is there a finitary, combinatorial characterization of $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_3$, in the spirit of the characterization of $\NIP$ by finite patterns in Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}?
\end{question}

In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we place a single iterated map at every level of the hierarchy except one:
at the level $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, both of our examples are actions of groups with two generators (see Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP}).

\begin{question}\label{q:cascade-NIP1}
  Is there a cascade CCS that is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$?
  Equivalently, is there a continuous map $\gamma$ of a compact metrizable space $L$ such that $(L,\gamma)$ is tame and $E(L,\gamma)$ is not first countable?
\end{question}

For actions of $\mathbb{Z}$, and more generally of abelian groups, the analogous question is Question~6.8 of Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame}.

At the level $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$, the deep computations of the three-dimensional Bresenham algorithm contain a copy of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ and none of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ (see Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham3}), whereas the countable family $\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}$ of elements of the group generated by the steps of the Euclidean algorithm realizes the other alternative of Todorčević’s trichotomy (see Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid-duplicate}).

\begin{question}\label{q:cascade-duplicate}
  Is there a cascade CCS that is $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$ and whose space of deep computations contains a copy of $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$?
\end{question}

\section{The Thue--Morse system is not tame}
\label{app:thue-morse}

In this appendix we prove that the shift on the Thue--Morse subshift generates an action of~$\mathbb{Z}$ that is not tame, a fact observed by Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{GlasnerMegrelishvili2018}, and we derive from it that the two dynamical CCSs defined by this shift, which we use in Section~\ref{sec:beyond-NIP} as examples beyond the hierarchy, are not $\NIP$.
Our proof is self-contained, except for the structure theorem for tame minimal systems under actions of abelian groups, due to Huang, to Kerr and Li, and to Glasner, which we recall below;
we combine that theorem with an elementary computation of the correlations of the Thue--Morse sequence.

We first recall the objects involved.
The Thue--Morse sequence $(t_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is defined by letting $t_n$ be the parity of the number of ones in the binary
expansion of~$n$, so that $t_{2k}=t_k$ and $t_{2k+1}=1-t_k$ for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$; it is the limit of the words $W_m$, for $m\in\mathbb{N}$, obtained from $0$ by $m$ applications of the
substitution $0\mapsto01$, $1\mapsto10$.
The two-sided sequences all of whose finite blocks occur in $(t_n)$ form the
Thue--Morse subshift $X_{TM}\subseteq2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, a closed subset of the compact metrizable space $2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, on which the shift~$\sigma$, defined by $\sigma(x)_n=x_{n+1}$, is a homeomorphism.
As in Section~\ref{sec:dynamical-CCS}, a system $(L,\Gamma)$, consisting of a compact metrizable space~$L$ and a countable semigroup~$\Gamma$ of continuous maps $L\to L$, is \emph{tame} if, for every continuous function $f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$, the family $\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ has the $\NIP$ of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns};
we call a sequence $(f\circ\gamma_k)_k$ of members of that family \emph{independent} if, for some reals $a<b$, it witnesses the finitary independence property of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns}, so that $(L,\Gamma)$ is tame exactly when no such sequence exists.
The CCS with states $X_{TM}$, the coordinate predicates $x\mapsto x_n$ for $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, and the group $\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is a dynamical CCS in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:dynamical-CCS}, and the cascade CCS of~$\sigma$ is the dynamical CCS with the same states and predicates and with the semigroup $\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$;
by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, each of them is $\NIP$ if and only if its dynamical system is tame.

That the dynamics of~$\sigma$ on~$X_{TM}$ is wild follows from the structure theory of tame minimal systems under actions of abelian groups.
Huang~\cite{Huang2006} and, independently, Kerr and Li~\cite{KerrLi2007} proved that a metrizable minimal tame system under an action of an abelian group is uniquely ergodic, that is, carries exactly one invariant Borel probability measure, and is an almost one-to-one extension of its maximal equicontinuous factor;
Glasner~\cite{Glasner2007} showed, moreover, that the factor map onto that factor is an isomorphism of measure-preserving systems.
For a minimal homeomorphism, the maximal equicontinuous factor is a rotation $y\mapsto y+\alpha$ of a compact abelian group~$Y$ in which the multiples of~$\alpha$ are dense, equipped with its Haar measure;
the characters of~$Y$ are eigenfunctions of this rotation and span a dense subspace of the space of square-integrable functions on~$Y$.
In a minimal system given by a homeomorphism~$\sigma$ that generates a tame action, therefore, every function that is square-integrable with respect to the invariant measure is a limit in mean square of linear combinations of eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator, which sends each such function to its composite with~$\sigma$.
The Thue--Morse subshift fails this test, because the correlations of the Thue--Morse sequence are, on average, too small.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:thue-morse}
Let $X_{TM}\subseteq2^{\mathbb{Z}}$ be the Thue--Morse subshift, and let $\sigma$ be the shift on~$X_{TM}$.
The action of $\mathbb{Z}$ on $X_{TM}$ generated by~$\sigma$ is not tame.
Consequently, neither the CCS with states $X_{TM}$, the coordinate predicates and the group $\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$, nor the cascade CCS of~$\sigma$, is $\NIP$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
We first show that $X_{TM}$ is minimal.
Every finite block~$w$ of $(t_n)$ occurs in some word~$W_m$.
Let $\overline{W}_m$ be the word obtained from $1$ by $m$ applications of the substitution;
the words $W_{m+1}=W_m\overline{W}_m$ and $\overline{W}_{m+1}=\overline{W}_mW_m$ then both contain~$W_m$, and, since $t_{2k}=t_k$ and $t_{2k+1}=1-t_k$, the block of $(t_n)$ of length $2^{m+1}$ that begins at the index $2^{m+1}j$ is $W_{m+1}$ or $\overline{W}_{m+1}$ according as $t_j$ is $0$ or~$1$.
Hence the block~$w$ occurs in every block of length $2^{m+2}$ of $(t_n)$, and therefore in every point of~$X_{TM}$;
every orbit of~$\sigma$ is thus dense in~$X_{TM}$.

Suppose now that the action of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by~$\sigma$ is tame.
By the structure theorem recalled above, the subshift $X_{TM}$ carries a unique $\sigma$-invariant Borel probability measure~$\mu$, and the eigenfunctions of the unitary operator~$V$ on $L^2(\mu)$ that sends each function to its composite with~$\sigma$ span a dense subspace of $L^2(\mu)$.
Let $\vartheta(x)=(-1)^{x_0}$ for $x\in X_{TM}$, and let $c(n)=\int \vartheta\cdot(\vartheta\circ\sigma^n)\,d\mu$ for $n\in\mathbb{N}$.
By minimality and compactness, the subshift $X_{TM}$ contains a point~$x$ with $x_j=t_j$ for every $j\ge0$;
every weak$^*$ limit of the measures $\frac1K\sum_{k<K}\delta_{\sigma^kx}$ is $\sigma$-invariant, and hence equal to~$\mu$, so that
\[
c(n)=\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac1K\sum_{k<K}(-1)^{t_k+t_{k+n}} .
\]
Splitting the sum according to the parity of~$k$ and using $t_{2k}=t_k$ and $t_{2k+1}=1-t_k$, we obtain $c(2n)=c(n)$ and $c(2n+1)=-\frac12\bigl(c(n)+c(n+1)\bigr)$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$; in particular, $c(1)=-\frac13$.
For $K\ge1$, let $A(K)=\sum_{n<K}c(n)^2$ and $B(K)=\sum_{n<K}c(n)c(n+1)$.
For every $K\ge1$, the recursions yield the identities and inequalities
\begin{align*}
B(2K)&=-\tfrac12\sum_{n<K}\bigl(c(n)+c(n+1)\bigr)^2\le0,\\
A(2K)&=A(K)+\tfrac14\sum_{n<K}\bigl(c(n)+c(n+1)\bigr)^2\le\tfrac32A(K)+\tfrac12B(K),
\end{align*}
where the last inequality follows by expanding the squares, because $c(0)=1\ge c(K)^2$.
Hence we have $A(4K)\le\frac32A(2K)$ for every $K\ge1$, and, since $A(2)=1+c(1)^2\le2$, it follows that $A(2^k)\le2\bigl(\frac32\bigr)^{k-1}$ for every $k\ge1$;
since the function~$A$ is nondecreasing, we conclude that $A(K)/K\to0$ as $K\to\infty$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:thue-morse}).
Now let $\lambda$ be a complex number of modulus~$1$, and let $\Pi_\lambda$ be the orthogonal projection of $L^2(\mu)$ onto the kernel of $V-\lambda I$, which consists of the eigenfunctions of~$V$ with eigenvalue~$\lambda$ and the function~$0$.
By von Neumann's mean ergodic theorem, applied to the unitary operator $\bar\lambda V$, the averages $\frac1K\sum_{n<K}\bar\lambda^n\,\vartheta\circ\sigma^n$ converge to $\Pi_\lambda\vartheta$ in $L^2(\mu)$;
since $c(n)$ is the inner product of $\vartheta\circ\sigma^n$ and~$\vartheta$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality yields
\[
\|\Pi_\lambda\vartheta\|^2=\lim_{K\to\infty}\Bigl|\frac1K\sum_{n<K}\bar\lambda^n c(n)\Bigr|\le\lim_{K\to\infty}\Bigl(\frac{A(K)}{K}\Bigr)^{1/2}=0 .
\]
Thus the function~$\vartheta$ is orthogonal to every eigenfunction of~$V$, and therefore, since these eigenfunctions span a dense subspace of $L^2(\mu)$, to every function in $L^2(\mu)$, which is absurd, since $|\vartheta|=1$.
The action is therefore not tame, and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS with the group $\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is not $\NIP$.

For the cascade CCS of~$\sigma$, we use a symmetry of the Thue--Morse sequence.
The words $W_{2m}$ are palindromes, and every finite block of $(t_n)$ occurs in one of
them, so the reversal $\iota$ of two-sided sequences, $\iota(x)_n=x_{-n}$, maps $X_{TM}$ onto itself
and satisfies $\iota\circ\sigma=\sigma^{-1}\circ\iota$.
Since the action of~$\mathbb{Z}$ is not tame, there are a continuous function $f\colon X_{TM}\to\mathbb{R}$ and pairwise distinct integers~$n_k$ such that the sequence $(f\circ\sigma^{n_k})_k$ is independent, and infinitely many of the indices $n_k$ have the same sign.
If they are positive, the corresponding terms already form an independent sequence in the
cascade; if they are negative, the functions
$f\circ\sigma^{n_k}\circ\iota=(f\circ\iota)\circ\sigma^{-n_k}$ form an independent
sequence with positive exponents, because $\iota$ is a bijection of $X_{TM}$.
In either case the cascade of~$\sigma$ is not tame, and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, its CCS is not $\NIP$.
\end{proof}
