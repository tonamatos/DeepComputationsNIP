\begin{abstract}
  We use the topology of function spaces to study the complexity of deep computations, the limits of computations that arise, for instance, in recurrent neural networks.
  The classification of Rosenthal compacta yields new complexity classes of deep computations, and these classes form a strict hierarchy.
  Each level of the hierarchy is attained by classical computations, among them the power method, Bresenham’s line algorithm and the Euclidean algorithm, while Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere lies beyond it.
  For iterative algorithms, the hierarchy coincides with a hierarchy of tame dynamical systems.
  The translation between topology and computation is made through model theory, specifically through the \emph{independence property} of Shelah’s classification theory.
  The same methods give conditions under which deep computations are limits of sequences of computations and, in a measure-theoretic form, conditions under which they can be evaluated almost surely by random sampling.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

In this paper we study the asymptotic behavior of computations as a parameter of the computation tends to a limit: for example, as the depth of a neural network tends to infinity, or as the time interval between the layers of a time-series network tends to zero.
Particular cases of such limits have recently attracted considerable attention in deep learning research
(e.g., Neural Ordinary Differential Equations~\cite{Chen:2018}, Physics-Informed Neural Networks~\cite{Raissi-Perdikaris-Karniadakis:2019}, and Deep Equilibrium Models~\cite{Bai:2019}).
Our results pertain to classical models of computation, in which real-valued quantities are known and manipulated to a finite degree of precision.
The framework of compositional computation structures, introduced in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability} and developed here, provides a unified setting in which to study these limit phenomena from a foundational viewpoint.

In that paper, Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton introduced limits of computations,
which they called \emph{ultracomputations}, since they arise as ultrafilter limits of standard computations,
or \emph{deep computations}, following usage in machine learning~\cite{Bai:2019}.
The two notions differ in a technical point that plays no role here, and we speak of deep computations throughout.
Using a classical criterion of Grothendieck~\cite{Grothendieck:1952}, they proved a dichotomy between \emph{stable} and unstable classes of deep computations; the deep computations of a stable class are \emph{polynomially approximable}, that is, on every \emph{shard} (a set of states on which each feature obeys a prescribed bound), each feature of their output is a uniform limit of polynomials in the features of the input.
Polynomial approximability of computations corresponds to continuous extendibility in topology, and to \emph{stability} and \emph{type definability} in model theory.

In this paper we refine that dichotomy.
An unstable class of deep computations is either \emph{wild}, that is, it has Shelah’s \emph{independence property} (IP), or \emph{tame}, that is, it is \emph{NIP}: it does \emph{not} have the independence property.
Between stability and NIP we identify three new levels, $\NIP_3$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_1$, which together with NIP form a strict hierarchy of four tame classes beyond the stable ones;
we also read the seven minimal families of Baire class~1 functions found by Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos, described below, as prototypes of tame families of computations.
The tools are more recent than those used in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}:
on the topological side, the theory of \emph{Rosenthal compacta} replaces Grothendieck’s criterion;
on the model-theoretic side, \emph{neostability} replaces stability.

The translation between topology and computation goes through model theory.
To every intermediate state of a computation we associate its \emph{type}:
a “lookup table” of the (real) values of all the features of the computation at that state.
The types, together with their limits, form a space of real-valued functions on the set of features —the \emph{space of types}— on which computations act;
the embedding of a structure into its space of types is a central device of model theory.
This embedding allows us to study topological limits of computations by means of the theory of function spaces (known as \emph{$\Cp$-theory}).
The space of types thus becomes a “Rosetta stone” connecting three classification programs:
in topology, the classification of Rosenthal compacta pioneered by Todorčević~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire};
in logic, the classification of theories developed by Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1971,Shelah:1990};
and in statistical learning, the theory of VC dimension and PAC learnability~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971, Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974, Valiant:1984}.
Rosenthal compacta correspond to NIP;
finiteness of VC dimension implies NIP, and for first-order formulas the two notions coincide (Section~\ref{sec:NIP-poly-exp}).

Deep computations arise as topological limits of standard (continuous) computations.
Pointwise limits of sequences of continuous functions form the \emph{first Baire class} —one topological rung above the class of continuous functions (intuitively, functions of Baire class~1 have “controlled” discontinuities).
A \emph{Rosenthal compactum} is a space of Baire class~1 functions, on a Polish (i.e., separable, completely metrizable) space, that is compact under the topology of \emph{pointwise} convergence.
Since the late 1970s, Rosenthal compacta have played a central role in functional analysis, especially in the theory of Banach spaces;
Todorčević’s trichotomy for such compacta~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire} has been used to organize tame dynamical systems into a hierarchy~\cite{glasner2022tame}.
Todorčević’s proof relies on set-theoretic forcing and infinite Ramsey theory, and his methods continue to be analyzed and exploited~\cite{Todorcevic:2023,horowitz2019compactsetsbaireclass}.
Going beyond Todorčević’s trichotomy, Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos~\cite{argyros2008rosenthal} found a heptachotomy, which we also use:
seven minimal families of Baire class~1 functions, indexed by the binary tree, such that every relatively compact family of Baire class~1 functions on a Polish space, indexed by the binary tree, contains, along a dyadic subtree (a copy of the binary tree inside itself), a family equivalent to one of the seven.
Here two families are equivalent when the bijection between them that respects the indexing extends to a homeomorphism of their closures.

For approximation, the decisive fact is that \emph{compactness and sequential compactness coincide}, and that every point in the closure of a relatively compact set of functions is the limit of a sequence from the set, both in the setting of Grothendieck~\cite{Grothendieck:1952} and in that of Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand~\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}.
Consequently, when the features are countably many and the computations are $\NIP$, every deep computation (in principle, a nonconstructive ultrafilter limit over a possibly uncountable set of computations) is, on each shard, the limit of a \emph{sequence} of standard computations.

We believe that the results presented in this paper show practitioners of computation, topology, descriptive set theory, or model theory how classification invariants used in their field translate into classification invariants of the other fields.
In the interest of accessibility, we do not assume familiarity with advanced topology, model theory, or computing.
The only technical prerequisites are undergraduate-level topology and measure theory;
the necessary background beyond them is covered in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}.

Our first main result, Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, concerns structures with countably many predicates.
It shows that a set of computations has the $\NIP$ on every shard, one observable feature at a time, if and only if its deep computations are of the first Baire class on each shard, that is, if and only if, on each shard, the deep computations form a Rosenthal compactum;
in that case, every deep computation is, on each shard, the limit of a \emph{sequence} of computations.
Guided by Todorčević’s trichotomy, we define three levels of tameness (Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}) and obtain the hierarchy
\[
  \text{stable}\Rightarrow\NIP_3\Rightarrow\NIP_2\Rightarrow\NIP_1\Rightarrow\NIP,
\]
in which every implication is strict.
In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we realize each level by a classical algorithm:
\begin{itemize}
\item
  a rotation of the circle is stable;
\item
  the power method is $\NIP_3$ but not stable;
\item
  Bresenham’s line algorithm is
  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    $\NIP_2$ but not $\NIP_3$ in the plane,
  \item
    $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$ in three-dimensional space;
  \end{itemize}
\item
  the Euclidean algorithm, acting on the projective line by the group that its steps generate, is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, and its deep computations include exact equality tests;
\item
  Newton’s method for the roots of $z^2-1$ on the extended complex plane is not~$\NIP$.
\end{itemize}
In both of our examples at the level $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, the computations form a group with two generators;
whether a single iterated map can reach this level is an open question, closely related to a question of Glasner and Megrelishvili (Question~\ref{q:cascade-NIP1}).
For iterative algorithms, the hierarchy coincides with that of tame dynamical systems due to Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame}.
The heptachotomy of Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos then shows that, on each shard and for each feature, every tame family of computations indexed by the binary tree contains, along a dyadic subtree,
a family equivalent to one of the seven minimal families, which we read as primitives of binary search (Section~\ref{sec:heptachotomy}).
Finally, Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} and Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo} relate $\NIP$ and Talagrand stability to the Monte Carlo computability of deep computations, that is, to their evaluation almost surely by random sampling.

\section{Topological preliminaries:\\
  From continuity to Baire class~1}
\label{sec:prelim}

Deep computations are pointwise limits of continuous maps, and the two questions that drive this paper, whether a deep computation is the limit of a sequence of computations and how complicated the set of all deep computations can be, concern spaces of functions with the topology of pointwise convergence.
In this section we recall the notions of general topology and of function space theory that these questions require: Polish spaces and Baire category; the first Baire class; the dichotomies of Rosenthal and of Bourgain, Fremlin and Talagrand, from which the NIP emerges; and the Rosenthal compacta, five of which serve as yardsticks for the classification.
We close the section with the role of the NIP in model theory, combinatorics and statistical learning.
All spaces are assumed to be Hausdorff unless explicitly stated otherwise.

A subset of a topological space is $F_\sigma$ if it is a countable union of closed sets, and $G_\delta$ if it is a countable intersection of open sets.
In a metric space, every open set is~$F_\sigma$;
equivalently, every closed set is~$G_\delta$.

A \emph{Polish space} is a separable and completely metrizable topological space, i.e., one admitting a complete metric that induces its topology.
The class of Polish spaces is closed under countable topological products.
Classical examples of Polish spaces (besides the real line) include
the Cantor space~$2^\mathbb{N}$ (the set of all infinite binary sequences, endowed with the product topology),
the Baire space $\mathbb{N}^\mathbb{N}$ (the set of all infinite sequences of natural numbers, also with the product topology),
and the space $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ of sequences of real numbers.

\begin{fact}\cite[4.3.24]{Engelking:1989}
  A subset of a Polish space is itself Polish in the subspace topology if and only if it is a $G_{\delta }$ set.
  In particular, closed subsets and open subsets of Polish spaces are also Polish spaces.
\end{fact}

If $X$ is a topological space and $A$ is a subset of $X$,
then $A$ is said to be \emph{nowhere dense} if its closure has an empty interior,
and of the \emph{first category} if $A$ is a countable union of closed nowhere dense sets;
otherwise, $A$ is said to be of the \emph{second category}.
Sets $A\subseteq X$ of the first category are also called \emph{meager}, and their complements $X\setminus A$ are called \emph{comeager}.
Thus, the set of meager subsets of $X$ is the $\sigma$-ideal generated  by the nowhere  dense subsets of $X$.
A topological space $X$ is \emph{Baire} if every comeager subset of $X$ is dense.
Equivalently, $X$ is Baire if any countable intersection of open dense sets remains dense.
The Baire Category Theorem states that compact Hausdorff spaces and all completely metrizable spaces (hence, all Polish spaces) are Baire.%
\footnote{More generally, any Čech-complete space is Baire~\cite[Theorem 3.9.3]{Engelking:1989}.}

Given two topological spaces $X$ and $Y$ we denote by $\Cp(X,Y)$ the set of all continuous functions $f:X\rightarrow Y$ endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence,
i.e., with the relative (subspace) product topology~$\Cp(X,Y)\subseteq Y^X$—where $Y$ carries its topology, but $X$ serves merely as a (discrete) index set stripped of any topology.
The space $\Cp(X, \mathbb{R})$ of continuous real-valued functions on~$X$ is denoted simply~$\Cp(X)$.

A function $f\in Y^X$ between topological spaces $X$, $Y$ is said to be of \emph{Baire class~1}, or a \emph{Baire-1} function,
if there is a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\subseteq \Cp(X, Y)$ such that $f = \lim_{n\to\infty}f_n$
(i.e., $f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)$ for all $x\in X$).
An elementary argument shows that Baire-1 functions are continuous everywhere except on a set of the first category.
If $X$ and $Y$ are topological spaces, the space of Baire-1 functions $f:X\rightarrow Y$ endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence is denoted $\mathrm{B}_1(X,Y)$;
thus, $\Cp(X,Y)\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X,Y)\subseteq Y^X$ are topological inclusions.
We abbreviate $\mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R})$ as $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$.
Relatively compact subsets of $\Cp(X)$ and of $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ (particularly for $X$ Polish) are of interest in analysis and topological dynamics.

A topological space $X$ is \emph{perfectly normal} if it is normal and every closed subset of $X$ is a $G_\delta$ set (equivalently, every open subset of $X$ is an $F_\sigma$ set).
Every metrizable space (hence, every Polish space) is perfectly normal.

The following characterization of Baire class~1 functions combines the equivalence of (1) and (2), which goes back to Lebesgue and Hausdorff~\cite[Theorem~24.10]{kechris1995cdst}, with the characterization by condition~(3), which Baire proved for functions of real variables in his thesis.
For a proof, see~\cite[Section 10]{Todorcevic_1997_TopicsTop}.

\begin{fact}[Baire]
  \label{baire}
  If $X$ is perfectly normal, then the following conditions are equivalent for a function $f:X\to \mathbb{R}$:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[(1)]
    $f$ is a Baire class~1 function, that is, $f$ is a pointwise limit of continuous functions.
  \item[(2)]
    $f^{-1}[U]$ is an $F_\sigma$ subset of $X$ whenever $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is open.
  \end{itemize}
  If $X$ is Polish, then (1) and (2) are equivalent to:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item[(3)]
    For every nonempty closed $F\subseteq X$, the restriction $f|_F$ has a point of continuity.
  \end{itemize}
  Moreover, if $X$ is Polish and $f\notin \mathrm{B}_1(X)$, then there exist countable $D_0,D_1\subseteq X$ and reals $a<b$ such that
  \[
    D_0\subseteq f^{-1}(-\infty,a],
    \quad
    D_1\subseteq f^{-1}[b,\infty),
    \quad
    \overline{D_0}=\overline{D_1}.
  \]
\end{fact}

\subsection{From Rosenthal’s dichotomy to the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy to Shelah’s NIP}

A deep computation is accessible to approximation when it is the limit of a \emph{sequence} of standard computations.
In a metrizable space, every point of the closure of a set is the limit of a sequence of points of the set;
in spaces of functions with the topology of pointwise convergence, this characterization of the closure fails in remarkable ways, and the dichotomies of this subsection describe when it does.

The $n$-th coordinate map $p_n: 2^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}$ on the Cantor space $X = 2^\mathbb{N}$ ($= \{0, 1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$) is continuous for each $n\in \mathbb{N}$;
however,  $(p_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ has \emph{no} convergent subsequences in $\mathbb{R}^X$ (\cite[Chapter~1.1]{Todorcevic_1997_TopicsTop}).
In a sense, this example exhibits the worst failure of sequential convergence possible:
The closure of $\{p_n\}$ in $\{0, 1\}^X$ (or in $\mathbb{R}^X$ for that matter) is homeomorphic to the Stone--Čech compactification $\beta\mathbb{N}$ of the (discrete) space of natural numbers.
The space $\beta \mathbb{N}$ has cardinality $2^{2^{\omega}}$ (the largest possible for a separable Hausdorff space), and its points correspond to the ultrafilters on~$\mathbb{N}$.%
\footnote{\label{fn:id-ufilters}The proof of the existence of deep equilibria in compositional computation structures given by Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton~\cite{alva2024approximability} relies on a classical result on the existence of idempotent elements of~$\beta \mathbb{N}$.}

The following theorem, proved by Haskell Rosenthal in 1974, is fundamental in functional analysis and captures a sharp division in the behavior of sequences in a Banach space.

\begin{thm}[Rosenthal’s Dichotomy, \cite{Rosenthal:1974}]
  \label{thm:Rosenthal}
  If $X$ is Polish and $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq \Cp(X)$ is pointwise bounded, then $(f_n)$ has a convergent subsequence, or a subsequence whose closure in $\mathbb{R}^X$ is homeomorphic to~$\beta\mathbb{N}$.
\end{thm}

The dichotomy grew out of \emph{Rosenthal’s $\ell_1$-Theorem}, which states that every bounded sequence in a Banach space has a subsequence that is either weakly Cauchy or equivalent to the unit vector basis of $\ell_1$ (the space of absolutely summable sequences);
in particular, a Banach space contains no isomorphic copy of $\ell_1$ if and only if every bounded sequence in it has a weakly Cauchy subsequence.
The $\ell_1$-Theorem connects diverse areas:
Banach space geometry, Ramsey theory, set theory, and topology of function spaces.

As we move from $\Cp(X)$ to the larger space $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, a dichotomy paralleling the $\ell_1$-Theorem holds.
This result is usually not phrased as a dichotomy in the above manner, but rather as an equivalence (see Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} below).

First, we introduce some useful notation.
For any $f\in \mathbb{R}^X$ and any real $a$, define
\begin{align*}
X^f_{\le a} & \coloneqq f^{-1}(-\infty,a],  &
X^f_{\ge a} & \coloneqq f^{-1}[a, +\infty).
\end{align*}
For any set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ and any real $a$, define (by a slight abuse of notation)
\begin{align*}
X^A_{\le a} & \coloneqq \bigcap_{f\in A}X^f_{\le a} = \{x\in X: \text{$f(x)\le a$ for all $f\in A$}\}, \\
X^A_{\ge a} & \coloneqq \bigcap_{f\in A}X^f_{\ge a} = \{x\in X: \text{$f(x)\ge a$ for all $f\in A$}\}.
\end{align*}
(In case $A=\emptyset$, we define $X^\emptyset_{\ge a} = X = X^\emptyset_{\le a}$.)
For any sequence $\{f_n\}\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ and $I\subseteq \mathbb{N}$, define $I^{\complement} \coloneqq \mathbb{N}\setminus I$ and $f_I \coloneqq \{f_i: i\in I\}$.

\begin{thm}[“The BFT Dichotomy”.
    Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand {\cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}}]
  \label{thm:BFT}
  Let $X$ be a Polish space and $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$.
  \item
    For every $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A$ and every $a<b$ there is $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
    X_{\le a}^{f_I} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_{I^{\complement}}} = \emptyset.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
(As stated above, the BFT Dichotomy is a particular case of the equivalence (ii)$\Leftrightarrow$(v) in {\cite[Corollary 4G]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}}.)

The sets $X_{\le a}^{f_I}$ and $X_{\ge b}^{f_{I^{\complement}}}$ appearing in condition~\emph{(ii)} of Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} are defined, respectively,
in terms of $|I|$-many inequalities of the form $f_i(x)\le a$, and $|I^{\complement}|$-many of the form $f_j(x)\ge b$.
Thus, at least one of $X_{\le a}^{f_I}$ and $X_{\ge b}^{f_{I^{\complement}}}$ is defined by the satisfaction of infinitely (countably) many inequalities.
For our purposes, it is more natural to consider only finitely many inequalities at a time, which motivates the definitions below.

\begin{defn}\label{def:NIP-fns}
  We say that a collection of functions \emph{$A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ has the finitary No-Independence Property (NIP)}
  if, for all sequences $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A$ and reals $a<b$,
  there exist finite disjoint sets $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that $X_{\le a}^{f_E} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_F} = \emptyset$.
  We say that such $E, F$ \emph{witness finitary NIP for $(f_n)$ and $a, b$}.

  A set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$ \emph{has the finitary Independence Property (IP)} if it does not have finitary NIP,
  i.e., if there exists a sequence $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A$ and reals $a<b$ such that
  for every pair of finite disjoint sets $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, we have $X_{\le a}^{f_E} \cap X_{\ge b}^{f_F} \ne \emptyset$.
\end{defn}

If the word “finite” is omitted in the above definitions, we obtain the definitions of \emph{countable} NIP (weaker than finitary NIP) and \emph{countable} IP (stronger than finitary IP), respectively.

If we insist on witnesses $E,F\subseteq \mathbb{N}$ such that $F = E^{\complement}$, we call the respective properties “BFT-NIP” and “BFT-IP”.
Thus, Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} states (for pointwise bounded function collections $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$) that $A$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ if and only if $A$ has BFT-NIP.

From now on, IP and NIP mean \emph{finitary} IP and NIP unless otherwise specified.

\begin{thm}
  \label{thm:BFT2}
  Let $X$ be a compact metrizable  (hence Polish) space.
  For every pointwise bounded  $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$, the following properties are all equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A$ is relatively compact in~$\mathrm{B}_1(X)$;
  \item
    $A$ has BFT-NIP;
  \item
    $A$ has countable NIP;
  \item
    $A$ has finitary NIP.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
(The equivalence \emph{(ii)}$\Leftrightarrow$\emph{(iv)} holds for arbitrary compact~$X$, without assuming pointwise boundedness.
The equivalence \emph{(ii)}$\Leftrightarrow$\emph{(iii)}, for arbitrary~$X$.)
\begin{proof}
  (i)$\Leftrightarrow$(ii) is Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}.
  The implications (iv)$\Rightarrow$(iii) and (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii) are trivial, and (iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii) follows by taking $I=E$, since then $F\subseteq I^{\complement}$.
  For (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iv), suppose that $(f_n)$ and $a<b$ witness finitary IP and fix $I\subseteq\mathbb{N}$.
  The closed sets $X^{f_n}_{\le a}$ ($n\in I$) and $X^{f_m}_{\ge b}$ ($m\in I^{\complement}$) have the finite intersection property, so by compactness $X^{f_I}_{\le a}\cap X^{f_{I^{\complement}}}_{\ge b}\neq\emptyset$.
\end{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} may be stated as the following dichotomy (under the assumptions) for any compact metrizable~$X$:
either $A$ is relatively compact in~$\mathrm{B}_1(X)$, or $A$ has IP (in either sense).

The independence property was isolated by Saharon Shelah~\cite[Definition 4.1]{Shelah:1971} as one of the dividing lines of his classification of first-order theories~\cite{Shelah:1990}.
We return to it in Section~\ref{sec:NIP-poly-exp}, where it appears together with its counterparts in combinatorics and in statistical learning.

\subsection{Rosenthal compacta}

The dichotomy of Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT} led Godefroy~\cite{Godefroy:1980} to the following definition.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:Rosenthal-compacta}
  A Rosenthal compactum is any topological space realized as a compact subset of the space $\mathrm{B}_1(X) = \mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R})$ (equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence)
  of all real functions of the first Baire class on some Polish space~\(X\).
\end{defn}
A Rosenthal compactum~$K$ is necessarily Hausdorff since it is a topological subspace of the Hausdorff product space $\mathbb{R}^X$.

Rosenthal compacta enjoy strong tameness properties;
for instance, every point in the closure of a subset of a Rosenthal compactum is the limit of a sequence of points of the subset~\cite[Theorem~3F]{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}.
They play an important role in functional analysis, measure theory, dynamical systems, descriptive set theory, and model theory;
in this paper they serve as spaces of deep computations, since, for countably many features, the deep computations of an NIP set of computations form a Rosenthal compactum on each shard (Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).

\subsection{The special case~\texorpdfstring{$\mathrm{B}_1(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$}{B\_1(X, R\textasciicircum P)} with~\texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{P}$}{P} countable}

A state of a computation is described by the values of its features, and a computation is therefore a map with values in a product of copies of the real line indexed by the features;
in this subsection we reduce the study of such maps to the study of real-valued functions.
Fix an arbitrary (at most) countable set~$\mathcal{P}$ whose elements $P\in \mathcal{P}$ will be called \emph{predicate symbols} or \emph{formal predicates}.
Our present goal is to characterize relatively compact subsets of $\mathrm{B}_1(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$, where $X$ is always assumed to be a perfectly normal space (typically a Polish space).

The set $\mathcal{P}$ shall be considered discrete whenever regarded as a topological space.
Since $\Cp(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})\subseteq (\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X$, the “ambient” space $(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X$ is quite relevant.
The product $X\times \mathcal{P}$ will be regarded as either a point set, or as a topological product depending on context.
We have natural homeomorphic identifications
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X \cong \mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \cong (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}},
\end{equation*}
given by
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \to (\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X: \varphi & \mapsto {\hat{}}\varphi    \\
\mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \to (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}}: \varphi & \mapsto \varphi{\,\hat{}},
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
\hat{}\varphi(x)\coloneqq \varphi(x, \cdot)\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}, &  &
\varphi\,\hat{}(P)\coloneqq \varphi(\cdot, P) \in \mathbb{R}^X.
\end{align*}
Such identifications view $X$ and $\mathcal{P}$ as mere point sets (the topology of~$X$ in particular plays no role).

For $x\in X$, define the “left projection” map
\begin{equation*}
\lambda_x: \mathbb{R}^{X\times\mathcal{P}}\to \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}: \varphi\mapsto \lambda_x(\varphi)\coloneqq \hat{}\varphi(x);
\end{equation*}
for $P\in \mathcal{P}$, the “right projection” map
\begin{equation*}
\rho_P: \mathbb{R}^{X\times\mathcal{P}}\to \mathbb{R}^X: \varphi\mapsto \rho_P(\varphi)\coloneqq \varphi\,\hat{}(P).
\end{equation*}
For fixed $x\in X$ and $P\in \mathcal{P}$, we also have canonical projection maps
\begin{align*}
\lambda_x: \mathbb{R}^X\to \mathbb{R}: f\mapsto f(x), &  &
\rho_P: \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to \mathbb{R}: f\mapsto f(P).
\end{align*}
When clear from context, rather than using the specific symbols (“$\lambda$” for left, “$\rho$” for right) to denote projections, we may use the generic symbol~“$\pi$”;
thus, $\pi_x$ may mean $\lambda_x$, and $\pi_P$ may mean $\rho_P$.

The following proposition reduces the study of $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$-valued continuous or Baire-1 functions on~$X$ to the special case of real-valued ones.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Baire-identifications}
  The identification $(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^X \cong \mathbb{R}^{X\times \mathcal{P}} \cong (\mathbb{R}^X)^{\mathcal{P}}$ induces identifications
  \begin{align*}
  \Cp(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}) \cong \Cp(X\times \mathcal{P}) \cong \Cp(X)^{\mathcal{P}}, &  &
  \mathrm{B}_1(X, \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}) \cong \mathrm{B}_1(X\times \mathcal{P}) \cong \mathrm{B}_1(X)^{\mathcal{P}}.
  \end{align*}
\end{prop}
(The cardinality of $\mathcal{P}$ plays no role.)
\begin{proof}
  The identification of $\Cp$-spaces follows trivially from the definition of topological product and the fact that $\mathcal{P}$ is discrete:
  a continuous map $X\to \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ is precisely a $\mathcal{P}$-indexed family of continuous functions $X\to \mathbb{R}$, and these correspond to continuous functions $X\times \mathcal{P}\to \mathbb{R}$.
  The identification of Baire-1 spaces follows immediately, since it is defined in terms of the purely topological notion of limit (in the ambient space) of sequences of continuous functions.
\end{proof}

Given $A\subseteq Y^X$ and $K\subseteq X$ we write $A|_K:=\{f|_K:f\in A\}$, i.e., the set of all restrictions of functions in $A$ to $K$.
The following theorem extends Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$-valued functions.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Generalized-BFT}
  Assume that $\mathcal{P}$ is countable, $X$ is a Polish space, and $A\subseteq \Cp(X,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$ is pointwise bounded in the sense that $\pi_P\circ A$ ($\subseteq \Cp(X)$) is pointwise bounded for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  The following are equivalent for every compact $K\subseteq X$:

  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A|_K$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(K,\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P})$;
  \item
    $\pi_P\circ A|_K$ has NIP for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
\begin{proof}
  By Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications} and Tychonov’s theorem, (i) holds if and only if $\pi_P\circ A|_K$ is relatively compact in $\mathrm{B}_1(K)$ for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  Since $K$ is compact metrizable, Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2} shows that this is equivalent to~(ii).
\end{proof}

We conclude this subsection with an elementary but important observation.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:NIP-and-closure}
  Assume that $X$ is Hausdorff and that $A\subseteq \Cp(X)$.
  The following are equivalent for every $L\subseteq X$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $A|_L$ satisfies the NIP;
  \item
    $A|_{\overline{L}}$ satisfies the NIP.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) is trivial.
  Conversely, if $(f_n)\subseteq A$ and $a<b$ witness the IP on $\overline{L}$, fix $a<a'<b'<b$.
  For finite disjoint $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, the open set $\bigcap_{n\in E}f_n^{-1}(-\infty,a')\cap\bigcap_{n\in F}f_n^{-1}(b',\infty)$ meets $\overline{L}$, hence meets~$L$;
  so $(f_n)$ and $a'<b'$ witness the IP on~$L$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Five canonical Rosenthal compacta}
\label{sec:five-compacta}
Five Rosenthal compacta, all built on the Cantor set, are the canonical members of the classes in Todorčević’s trichotomy (Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}).
By the trichotomy, the space of deep computations of each non-metrizable $\NIP$ example of Section~\ref{sec:iterative} contains a copy of one of them.
We fix their notation here.
Recall that a topological space is \emph{separable} if it has a countable dense subset, \emph{hereditarily separable} if every subspace is separable, and \emph{first countable} if every point has a countable base of neighborhoods.

Let $2^{\mathbb{N}}$ carry the product topology and the lexicographic order~$<$ (whose order topology is the product topology).
The length of a finite word $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is denoted~$|t|$;
for $a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$, we write $a\restr n$ for the word formed by the first $n$ bits of~$a$.
Two words are \emph{comparable} if one extends the other, and an \emph{antichain} is a set of pairwise incomparable words.
For a finite word $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, let $t^\frown0^\infty$ and $t^\frown1^\infty$ be the sequences that begin with $t$ and continue with all $0$’s, respectively all $1$’s;
the set of sequences extending $t$ is the clopen interval $[t^\frown0^\infty,t^\frown1^\infty]$.

For a statement~$S$, we write $\Brack{S}$ for its truth value:
$1$ if $S$ is true and $0$ if it is false.
We introduce the following functions $2^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item
  the \emph{prefix test} $v_t\coloneqq \Brack{\cdot{\restriction}|t| = t}$, the characteristic function of the set of sequences extending a fixed word~$t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$;
\item
  the \emph{equality test} $\delta_a\coloneqq\Brack{\cdot = a}$, the characteristic function of the singleton $\{a\}$ for fixed~$a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$;
\item
  the \emph{threshold tests} $\chi_{[a}\coloneqq\Brack{a\le \cdot}$ and $\chi_{(a}\coloneqq\Brack{a<\cdot}$, the characteristic functions of the final intervals $\{x:a\le x\}$ (closed) and~$\{x:a<x\}$ (open), for fixed~$a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$;
  the special case $\mathbf{0}\coloneqq \chi_{(1^\infty}$ is the identically zero function.
\end{itemize}
Prefix tests are continuous, and so are $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$ and $\chi_{(t^\frown1^\infty}$ —they all depend only on the first $|t|$ bits of their argument in~$2^{\mathbb{N}}$;
equality tests, and threshold tests at other points, are discontinuous Baire class~$1$ functions.
All closures below are taken in the topology of pointwise convergence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  The \emph{Alexandroff compactification}
  $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{\delta_a:a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}$.
  Each $\delta_a$ is isolated, and a neighborhood of $\mathbf{0}$ omits only finitely many $\delta_a$.
  Thus, $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is not separable, nor first countable at~$\mathbf{0}$.
\item
  The \emph{extended Alexandroff compactification}
  $\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\overline{\{v_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}}=\{v_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\cup A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
  As $n\to\infty$, the prefix tests $v_{a\restr n}$ converge to $\delta_a$;
  along an antichain they converge to $\mathbf{0}$.
  Thus, $\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is separable but not first countable.
\item
  The \emph{split Cantor set}
  $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{\chi_{[a},\chi_{(a}:a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  The continuous thresholds $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$, together with the isolated point $\mathbf{0}=\chi_{(1^\infty}$, form a countable dense subset:
  thresholds approaching $a$ from below converge to $\chi_{[a}$, and from above to~$\chi_{(a}$.
  Thus, $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is separable, first countable and hereditarily separable, but not metrizable.
\item
  The \emph{Alexandroff duplicate}
  $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\{(\mathbf{0},a),(\delta_a,a):a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq A(2^{\mathbb{N}})\times2^{\mathbb{N}}$.
  The points $(\delta_a,a)$ are isolated, and a neighborhood of $(\mathbf{0},a)$ contains $(\mathbf{0},b)$ and $(\delta_b,b)$ for all $b$ near~$a$ with finitely many exceptions.
  Thus, $D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is first countable but not separable;
  its separable extension is $\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})=\overline{\{(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty):t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}}=D(2^{\mathbb{N}})\cup\{(v_t,t^\frown0^\infty):t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
\item
  The \emph{extended duplicate of the split Cantor set}
  \[
    \hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))=\overline{\{(v_t,\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty})\}_{t}}\subseteq\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})\times S(2^{\mathbb{N}}),
  \]
  which consists of the points $(v_t,\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty})$
  and, for $a\in2^{\mathbb{N}}$, the points $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$, $(\mathbf{0},\chi_{[a})$ and $(\mathbf{0},\chi_{(a})$ \cite[4.3.7]{argyros2008rosenthal}.
  Along the words $t=a\restr n$, the thresholds $t^\frown0^\infty$ increase to $a$, which gives the points $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$.
  Thus, $\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ is separable and first countable;
  since the points $(\delta_a,\chi_{[a})$ form an uncountable discrete subspace, it is not hereditarily separable.
\end{enumerate}

The constructions of the Alexandroff compactification and duplicate hold with $2^{\mathbb{N}}$ replaced by another compact metric space~$K$;
these are denoted $A(K)$ and $D(K)$, respectively.
The Alexandroff compactification of any compactum of size continuum (e.g., $A([0,1])$) is homeomorphic to~$A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.

\subsection{NIP in model theory, combinatorics and learning: polynomial versus exponential growth}
\label{sec:NIP-poly-exp}

For families of $\{0,1\}$-valued functions, that is, of sets, a combinatorial dividing line closely related to the NIP of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns} appeared in 1971--1972, some years before Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT}, independently and almost simultaneously in three foundational contexts:
in model theory, in the work of Saharon Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1971,Shelah:1990};
in combinatorics, in the work of Norbert Sauer~\cite{Sauer:1972} and, again, of Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1972,Shelah:1990};
and in statistical learning, in the work of Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971,Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974}.
In each of them, the line separates the families of sets whose number of traces on an $n$-element set (the intersections of the members of the family with the set) is bounded by a polynomial in~$n$ (“tame”) from those for which, for every~$n$, some $n$-element set has all $2^n$ possible traces (“wild”).
The precise relation with the NIP is explained at the end of this subsection.

\begin{description}

\item[In model theory]
  Among the dividing lines of Shelah’s classification theory~\cite{Shelah:1990}, the independence property is detected by counting types over finite sets of parameters.
  Let $T$ be a complete first-order theory, let $\varphi(x;y)$ be a formula of its language, in which $x$ and $y$ are finite tuples of variables of lengths $|x|$ and $|y|$, and let $B$ be a finite set of $|y|$-tuples of elements of a model $M$ of~$T$.
  A \emph{$\varphi$-type} over $B$ is a maximal set of formulas $\varphi(x;b)$ and $\neg\varphi(x;b)$, with $b\in B$, that is consistent with the theory of $M$ with constants for the elements of~$M$;
  since $B$ is finite, every $\varphi$-type over $B$ is realized by an $|x|$-tuple $a$ of elements of $M$, and it is determined by the set $\{b\in B: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$.
  Hence the set $S_\varphi(B)$ of $\varphi$-types over $B$ is in bijection with the set of traces on $B$ of the family of sets $\{b: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$, with $a$ ranging over $|x|$-tuples of elements of $M$, and $|S_\varphi(B)|\le2^{|B|}$.
  The formula $\varphi$ has the \emph{independence property} (IP) if, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$, some set $B$ of $n$ tuples satisfies $|S_\varphi(B)|=2^n$;
  otherwise the formula is \emph{NIP}, or \emph{dependent}, and the theory $T$ is NIP if all its formulas are NIP.
  Since $T$ is complete, these notions do not depend on the model~$M$.

  If $\varphi$ is NIP, the Sauer--Shelah lemma below bounds $|S_\varphi(B)|$ by a polynomial in $|B|$.
  Consequently, $\varphi$ is NIP if and only if the number of $\varphi$-types over a finite set $A$ of parameters (that is, with $b$ ranging over the $|y|$-tuples of elements of $A$) is bounded by a polynomial in $|A|$;
  if $\varphi$ has IP, then for every $n$ some set $A$ of at most $n|y|$ parameters satisfies $|S_\varphi(A)|\ge2^n$~\cite[Section~2.3.4]{simon2015nip}.
  Over infinite sets of parameters, counting $\varphi$-types does not separate NIP from IP without further set-theoretic hypotheses: if $\kappa$ is infinite and $2^\kappa=\kappa^+$, every unstable formula, NIP or not, already has $2^\kappa$ $\varphi$-types over some set of $\kappa$ parameters~\cite[Section~2.3.4]{simon2015nip}.

\item[In combinatorics]
  Sauer~\cite{Sauer:1972} and Shelah~\cite{Shelah:1972} proved independently the following dichotomy, now known as the \emph{Sauer--Shelah lemma}:
  Let $\mathscr{F}=\{S_i:i\in I\}$ be a collection of subsets of a set $S$, indexed by a set~$I$.
  Either:
  for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$ there is a set $A\subseteq S$ with $|A|=n$ such that $|\{S_i\cap A: i\in I\}|=2^n$ ($\mathscr{F}$ is a collection having “exponential complexity”);
  or:
  there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that for every $A\subseteq S$ with $|A|\ge N$, one has
  \[
    |\{S_i\cap A: i\in I\}| \le \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \binom{|A|}{i}.
  \]
  ($\mathscr{F}$ is a collection having “polynomial complexity”;
  note that any collection $\mathscr{F}$ of subsets of a \emph{finite} set $S$ has polynomial complexity.)
  The “polynomial” name is justified:
  indeed, for fixed $N>0$, as a function of the size $|A| = m > 0$, we have
  \begin{equation*}
  \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \binom{m}{i}
  \le \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{m^{i}}{i!}
  \le \left( \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{i!} \right)\cdot m^{N-1}
  < e\cdot m^{N-1},
  \end{equation*}
  which is polynomial in $m$ for fixed~$N$.

\item[In machine learning]
  Readers familiar with statistical learning may recognize the Sauer--Shelah lemma as the dichotomy
  discovered and proved slightly earlier (1971) by Vapnik and Chervonenkis~\cite{Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1971, Vapnik-Chervonenkis:1974}
  to address uniform convergence in statistics.
  The largest size of a set $A\subseteq S$ with $2^{|A|}$ traces $S_i\cap A$, that is, the integer $N-1$ for the least integer $N$ given by the preceding paragraph, is called the \emph{VC dimension} of $\mathscr{F}$;
  it is a core concept in machine learning.
  If such an integer $N$ does not exist, we say that the VC dimension of $\mathscr{F}$ is infinite.
  Through the uniform convergence theorem of Vapnik and Chervonenkis, the lemma bounds the number of data points (the sample size) needed to “learn” a concept class~$\mathscr{F}$ of finite VC dimension in a statistical sense, that is, up to a preset error and with a preset confidence.
  The so-called Fundamental Theorem of Statistical Learning states that a class of $\{0,1\}$-valued functions is PAC-learnable (PAC stands for “Probably Approximately Correct”~\cite{Valiant:1984}) if and only if its VC dimension is finite, provided that the class satisfies a mild measurability condition~\cite{Blumer-Ehrenfeucht-Haussler-Warmuth:1989};
  see also~\cite[Theorem~6.7]{Shalev-Shwartz-Ben-David:2014}.

\end{description}

For first-order formulas the three dichotomies coincide:
by the description of $\varphi$-types given above, a formula $\varphi(x;y)$ has IP exactly when the family of sets $\{b: M\models\varphi(a;b)\}$, with $a$ ranging over the $|x|$-tuples of elements of a model $M$ of~$T$, has infinite VC dimension, and for an NIP formula the Sauer--Shelah lemma gives the polynomial bound on the number of $\varphi$-types.
For an arbitrary family of sets, finiteness of the VC dimension implies that the characteristic functions of its members have the NIP of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns};
the converse fails, since the finite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ form a family of infinite VC dimension whose characteristic functions have the NIP.

\subsection*{Historical remarks}
The Baire hierarchy of functions was introduced by René-Louis Baire in his 1899 doctoral thesis, \emph{Sur les fonctions de variables réelles}.
The field of $\Cp$-theory was pioneered by A.~V.~Arhangel’skiĭ and his students in the 1970s and 1980s~\cite{Arkhangelskii:1992},
and it has found many applications in model theory and functional analysis.
For a recent survey, see~\cite{tkachuk2011cp}.

\section{Compositional Computation Structures: \\
  A structural approach to arbitrary-precision computations}
\label{sec:CCS}

In this section we connect function spaces with arbitrary-precision computations.
A computation on real-valued data is observed through its features, each known to finite precision, and a deep computation is a limit of computations in the topology of this observation, the topology of pointwise convergence.
We first recall the framework of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}: states are described by their types, the tuples of values of all their features;
computations act on states and are composed;
and deep computations are the pointwise limits of computations, taken in the space of types.
We then recall the stable sets of computations, studied in that paper, and close the section with two examples that motivate the classification of Section~\ref{sec:Classification}.

\subsection{Computation States Structures}
\label{sec:CSS}

A \emph{computation states structure} is a pair $(L,\mathcal{P})$, where $L$ is a set whose elements we call \emph{states} and $\mathcal{P}$ is a collection of real-valued functions on $L$ that we call \emph{predicates} or \emph{features}.
For each $P\in \mathcal{P}$, we call the value $P(v)$ the $P$-th \emph{(observable) feature} of~$v$.

A \emph{transition} of a computation states structure $(L,\mathcal{P})$ is a map $f:L\to L$.

The \emph{type} of a state $v\in L$ is the indexed family%
\footnote{As in Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications} above, we regard each predicate on $L$ as named by a specific formal label~$P$ (a \emph{predicate symbol})
  which is given the semantic meaning of a \emph{bona fide} real function $P(\cdot): v\mapsto P(v)$ (the predicate proper) in the computation states structure.
  Such a formal symbol~$P$ becomes \emph{de facto} the name of an observable feature having the real value $P(v)$ at any state $v\in L$.
  Whenever $\mathcal{P}$ is used as an index set (e.g., as in the topological product $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ below), it is regarded as a set of formal symbols.
  This syntactic vs.\ semantic abuse of nomenclature is commonplace in mathematics, but the model-theoretic framework requires contextual awareness of the distinction.
  To disambiguate when necessary, we shall write $P(\cdot)$, implying the semantic meaning of a real function on~$L$, in contrast to~$P$, meant as a purely syntactic symbol.
  Nevertheless, by a standard abuse of nomenclature in appropriate contexts, we may also treat $P$ as a function on states (i.e., take $P$ to mean $P(\cdot)$ proper).}
\[
  \operatorname{tp}(v)= (P(v))_{P\in \mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}
\]
of its features.

Intuitively, $L$ is the set of states of a computation, and the predicates $P \in\mathcal{P}$ are primitives that are given and accepted as computable.
(I.e., each symbol $P\in \mathcal{P}$ denotes a function $P(\cdot)$ taken as a computational primitive on~$L$.)

We assume that each state $v\in L$ is uniquely characterized by its type $\operatorname{tp}(v)$,
so we may identify $L$ with a subset of~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ and topologize it as a subspace
(i.e., $L$ is identified with its forward image~$\tp[L]\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ under the type map).
As a topological space, $L$ is thus endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence induced by the product topology of~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$.
In particular, for each $P\in \mathcal{P}$, the projection map $v\mapsto P(v)$ is continuous.

Important state spaces include $L=\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ (resp., $L=\mathbb{R}^n$ for some positive integer~$n$), endowed with one predicate $P_i(v) = v_i$ (giving the $i$-th coordinate of~$v$) for each~$i\in \mathbb{N}$ (resp., for each $0\le i<n$).
State subspaces $L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ and $L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ are of primary interest in this paper.
(Through the identification of each state with its type, any computation states structure with a finite or countable family of predicates is such a subspace.)

\begin{defn}
  Given a computation states structure $(L,\mathcal{P})$, any type $\tp(v)\in \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of a state $v\in L$ will be called a \emph{realized} (state) type.
  The topological closure of the set of realized types in~$\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ (endowed with the pointwise convergence topology) will be called the \emph{space of (state) types} of~$(L,\mathcal{P})$, denoted~$\mathcal{L}$;
  elements $\mathbf{v}\in \mathcal{L}$ are called \emph{state types}.
  Elements $\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus L$ are \emph{unrealized} state types.
\end{defn}

Intuitively, state types capture a notion of “limit state”.
For each $P\in \mathcal{P}$, the $P$-th coordinate map (natural projection) $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}\to \mathbb{R}: \mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{v}_Q)_{Q\in \mathcal{P}}\mapsto \mathbf{v}_P$,
gives a continuous extension to~$\mathcal{L}$ of the predicate $P(\cdot)$ on~$L$;
abusing notation, the extension will still be denoted~$P(\cdot)$ (or just~$P$).

\begin{rem}\label{rem:poor-mans-ultraprod}
  One may regard unrealized state types as “hyper-states”;
  when such a type is obtained as a sequential limit (or ultralimit) of realized state types, it may be called a “deep state”.
  A comment for readers versed in model theory:
  at least for the purpose of realizing state types (with parameters in the original states space~$L$),
  the type space $(\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{P})$ serves as a “poor man’s” ultraproduct of~$(L, \mathcal{P})$ wherein, by the very definition of~$\mathcal{L}$, each type $\mathbf{v}$ is realized.
\end{rem}

\subsubsection{Bounds on precision and magnitude. Shards}
\label{sec:shards}

In order for computation states structures to serve their purpose as models of real-world computing,
we must keep in mind that computations involving real-valued quantities can only ever be carried out approximately,
to a preset degree of precision (i.e., to within acceptable error bounds).
More subtly, and no less importantly in practice, one must also bound the magnitude of the real quantities involved in all computations:
no machine can store, let alone compute with, arbitrarily large numbers.
Thus, although the collection~$\mathcal{P}$ of predicates may be infinite (even uncountable), actual computations involve choices, made at the outset, of bounds for errors and for the absolute magnitudes of the quantities involved.
Heuristically, different parts of a computation may require suitably matched (different) error bounds, and may deal with quantities of very different sizes.
Focusing for now on the second of these issues, we consider sets of computations for which arbitrary but fixed bounds for each predicate are set in advance.
This leads to the concept of \emph{shard}, introduced in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.

\begin{defn}
  Fix a computation states structure $\underline{L} = (L, \mathcal{P})$, with $L$ implicitly identified with the set $\tp[L]\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of its realized types.
  A \emph{sizer} is a family $r_{\bullet}=(r_P)_{P\in\mathcal{P}}$ of positive real numbers, indexed by~$\mathcal{P}$.
  Given a sizer $r_\bullet$, let $\mathbb{R}[\rb] \coloneqq \prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}[-r_P,r_P]$ (a compact space), and let the $r_\bullet$-\emph{shard} of the states space~$L$ be
  \[
    L[r_\bullet] = L\cap \mathbb{R}[\rb].
  \]

  For a sizer  $r_{\bullet}$, the \emph{$r_{\bullet}$-type shard} is defined as $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]=\overline{L[r_\bullet]}$
  (a closed, hence compact subset of $\mathbb{R}[\rb]$).

  Let also $\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ (the space of \emph{shard-supported types}) be the union of all type-shards as the sizer $r_{\bullet}$ varies.
\end{defn}

Evidently, $\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\subseteq \mathcal{L}$, although the inclusion may be proper.
However, the equality $\mathcal{L}_{\sh} = \mathcal{L}$ holds in the important special case when $\mathcal{P}$ is (at most) countable (see~\cite{alva2024approximability}).

\subsection{Compositional Computation Structures}

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:CCS}
  A \emph{compositional computation structure} (CCS) is a triple $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$, where
  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    $(L,\mathcal{P})$ is a computation states structure, and
  \item
    $\Gamma\subseteq L^L$ is a semigroup under composition.
  \end{itemize}
  Elements of the semigroup $\Gamma$ are called the \emph{computations} of the structure $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$.
  The state space $L$ is implicitly identified with a subset of the type space~$\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ of~$(L, \mathcal{P})$.
  We also assume that the identity map $\text{id}$ on~$L$ is an element of~$\Gamma$ (which is thus not merely a semigroup but a monoid of transformations of~$L$).

  We topologize $\Gamma$ as a subset of the topological product $L^L$, where (as usual) the “exponent”~$L$ serves merely as an index set, but the “base”~$L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$ has the relative product topology (of pointwise convergence).
  In this manner, the semigroup $\Gamma$ is identified with a subset of the topological product~$\mathcal{L}^L \subseteq (\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}})^L$, which leads to an inclusion $\overline{\Gamma}\subseteq \mathcal{L}^L$.
  Elements $\xi\in\overline{\Gamma}$ are called (real-valued) \emph{deep computations} or \emph{ultracomputations}.

  A collection $R$ of sizers is \emph{exhaustive} if $L = \bigcup_{\rb\in R}L[\rb]$ (shards $L[\rb]$ exhaust~$L$).
  A transformation $\gamma\in\Gamma$ is \emph{$R$-confined} if $\gamma$ restricts to a map $\gamma|_{L[r_\bullet]}:L[r_\bullet]\to L[r_\bullet]$ (into $L[r_{\bullet}]$ itself) for every $r_\bullet\in R$.
  A subset $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is \emph{$R$-confined} if each $\gamma\in \Delta$ is.
\end{defn}

Unlike its subspace $L^L$, which is a semigroup under composition, the space $\mathcal{L}^L$ has no natural semigroup structure, so deep computations are not, in general, composable.
Intuitively, deep computations are “final” or “ultimate”.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:confined-compact}
  If $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is confined by an exhaustive sizer collection, then $\overline{\Delta}$ is a compact subset of~$\Lshard^L$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
  If $R$ confines~$\Delta$, choose for each $v\in L$ some $\rb^{(v)}\in R$ with $v\in L[\rb^{(v)}]$.
  Then $\Delta$ lies in the compact space $\prod_{v\in L}\mathcal{L}[\rb^{(v)}]\subseteq\Lshard^L$, which therefore contains $\overline{\Delta}$.
\end{proof}

For a CCS $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$, we regard the elements of $\Gamma$ as “standard” finitary computations, and the elements of $\overline{\Gamma}$, i.e., deep computations, as possibly infinitary limits of standard computations.

\begin{rems}(For readers versed in model theory.)%
  \footnote{Refer to~\cite{Keisler:2023} where a general framework for real-valued structures is introduced; see also~\cite[section 6]{Duenez-Iovino:2017} for a basic tutorial.}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    Compositional computation structures as defined above are not real-valued structures in a literal sense, because there is no intrinsic structural manner to ensure that $\Gamma$ is realized as a literal subset of the product space $L^L$.
    However, in a semantically transparent manner, any CCS as above may alternatively be seen (per its original definition in~\cite{alva2024approximability}) as a real-valued structure $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma,\circ,\operatorname{ev})$, where
    \begin{itemize}
    \item
      $(L,\mathcal{P})$ is a computation states structure,
    \item
      $(\Gamma,\circ)$ is a semigroup,
    \item
      $\operatorname{ev}: \Gamma\times L\to L: (\gamma,v)\mapsto \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,v)$ is a semigroup action of $\Gamma$ on~$L$
      (i.e., $\operatorname{ev}(\gamma\circ\delta,v) = \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,\operatorname{ev}(\delta,v))$ for $\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma$ and $v\in L$).
    \end{itemize}
    Both definitions are reconciled upon identifying $\gamma\in \Gamma$ with the map $(\gamma,v)\mapsto \operatorname{ev}(\gamma,v)$.

  \item
    In the preceding sense of real-valued structures, the class of compositional computation structures (for a fixed predicate symbol collection~$\mathcal{P}$) is first-order elementary.
    The limited notion of state type we have introduced (essentially, quantifier-free state types with parameters of sort $L$ only)
    may be enriched to state types involving more formulas (e.g., with parameters in~$\Gamma$, or including quantified formulas),
    and extended to “transform-types” (i.e., types of sort~$\Gamma$).
    In particular, given a structure $\underline{C}=(L,\mathcal{P}, \Gamma, \circ, \operatorname{ev})$,
    all types are realized in some ultrapower~$\underline{C}^{(\mathcal{U})}$ of~$\underline{C}$ (constructed in the usual manner detailed in the references above).%
    \footnote{By contrast (cf., Remark~\ref{rem:poor-mans-ultraprod}), the ultrapower construction is not necessary to realize state types in a computation states structure.}
    This approach to spaces of types was introduced in Banach space theory by Krivine~\cite{Krivine:1974, Krivine-Maurey:1981,Krivine:1972}.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{rems}

\subsection{Computability of deep computations and the Extendibility Axiom}
A machine evaluates only sums and products of finitely many observed features, and only to within a preset error on a preset range of magnitudes;
for this reason, computability is defined by uniform approximation, on shards, by polynomials in the features.
Let $(L, \mathcal{P})$ be a computation states structure with \emph{countable} predicate collection~$\mathcal{P}$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  A \emph{computable predicate} is any real function $\varphi: L\to \mathbb{R}$ whose restriction to an arbitrary shard is uniformly approximable by polynomials (with real coefficients) in the features~$P(\cdot)$.
\item
  A transform $f: L\to \mathcal{L}$ is \emph{computable} if, for each $Q\in\mathcal{P}$, the output feature $Q{\circ}f: L\to\mathbb{R}$ is a computable predicate.
\end{enumerate}

Alva, Dueñez, Iovino and Walton~\cite{alva2024approximability} showed that a transform $f: L\to\mathcal{L}$ is computable, that is, has uniformly polynomially approximable features on shards, if and only if it extends to a continuous map $\tilde{f}: \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{L}$.
This motivates the following definition.

A computation $\gamma\in \Gamma$ is \emph{extendable} if it has some extension $\tilde\gamma:\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\to\mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ such that,
for every sizer $r_{\bullet}$, there is a sizer $s_\bullet$ such that $\tilde\gamma|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}:\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\to\mathcal{L}[s_\bullet]$ is continuous.

Such an extension is unique if it exists, since it is continuous on each type shard and $L[\rb]$ is dense in~$\mathcal{L}[\rb]$.

Abusing notation, for any $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ consisting of extendable computations, the set $\{\tilde\gamma\colon\gamma\in\Delta\}$ will be denoted $\tilde{\Delta}$.

\begin{AxExt}
  \label{ax:extendibility-axiom}
  Every computation $\gamma\in\Gamma$ is extendable.
\end{AxExt}

The Extendibility Axiom is discussed at greater length in the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}.

\emph{For the rest of the paper, the Extendibility Axiom is imposed on all CCSs.}

The following lemma extends deep computations to the type shards;
it is used in Theorems~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} and~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:extension}
  Let $R$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $R$-confined.
  Every deep computation $f\in\overline{\Delta}$ has an extension $\tilde f:\Lshard\to\Lshard$ such that, for every $\rb\in R$, $\tilde f$ maps $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ into itself and $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}\in\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}}$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
  Write $f=\mathcal{U}\lim_i\gamma_i$ with $\gamma_i\in\Delta$ and $\mathcal{U}$ an ultrafilter.
  If $\rb\in R$, each $\tilde\gamma_i$ maps $L[\rb]$ into itself, hence, by continuity, its closure $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ into itself;
  since $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ is compact, the limit $\tilde f(v)\coloneqq\mathcal{U}\lim_i\tilde\gamma_i(v)$ exists for every $v\in\mathcal{L}[\rb]$ and lies in $\mathcal{L}[\rb]$, and $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}=\mathcal{U}\lim_i\tilde\gamma_i|_{\mathcal{L}[\rb]}$.
  Since $R$ is exhaustive, this defines $\tilde f$ on a set that contains~$L$, and there $\tilde f$ agrees with~$f$;
  at the remaining points of $\Lshard$ we let $\tilde f$ be the identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stability}
\label{sec:stable}
By the result recalled in the preceding subsection, the computable transforms are exactly those that extend to continuous maps on the space of types.
The sets of computations all of whose deep computations are computable, in this sense, sit at the top of the hierarchy of this paper.

\begin{defn}\label{def:stable}
  A set $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ of computations is \emph{stable} if, for every sizer $r_\bullet$, every deep computation in $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is continuous, that is, computable.
\end{defn}

Stable sets of computations are the subject of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability}, where they are characterized by Grothendieck’s double limit criterion and identified with stability in the sense of model theory.

\subsection{Two motivating examples}
\label{sec:preview}
Before developing the classification, we describe two computations whose deep computations behave very differently.
Both are treated in detail in Section~\ref{sec:examples}.

\emph{Square roots.}
Newton’s method for $p(x)=x^2-a$, with $a>0$, acts on the real projective line $\RP^1=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ by $N_p(x)=(x^2+a)/(2x)$.
Its iterates are continuous, and they converge pointwise to the map $\mathfrak f$ that sends every positive number to $\sqrt a$, every negative number to $-\sqrt a$, and $0$ and $\infty$ to~$\infty$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sqrt}).
The deep computations are the iterates together with~$\mathfrak f$.
The map $\mathfrak f$ is discontinuous, so it is not computable and the set of iterates is not stable;
but it is the limit of a \emph{sequence} of computations, and the space of deep computations is countable.

\emph{Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere.}
For $\rho(z)=z^3-2z+2$, Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere $\hatC=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ behaves in three ways at once (Figure~\ref{fig:newton} in Section~\ref{sec:Newton-cubic}).
On the basins of the three roots the iterates converge.
On the basin of the cycle $0\mapsto1\mapsto0$ they do not:
the even and the odd iterates converge there to two different deep computations.
On the common boundary of these basins, a fractal set, the iterates are chaotic;
for the simpler polynomial $z^2-1$, such chaos produces deep computations that are not even limits of sequences of computations (Proposition~\ref{prop:Newton-wild} and Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).

The classification of Section~\ref{sec:Classification} is designed to separate such behaviors.
Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} characterizes the sets of computations whose deep computations are limits of sequences, as for square roots, and Todorčević’s trichotomy measures how complicated the space of such deep computations can be.

    The continuous maps $N_p^n$ converge pointwise to the deep computation $\mathfrak f$, which sends the positive half line to $\sqrt a$, the negative half line to $-\sqrt a$, and $0$ and $\infty$ to~$\infty$.}

\section{Classifying deep computations}
\label{sec:Classification}

This is the main section of the paper.
The examples of Section~\ref{sec:preview} show that natural algorithms have discontinuous deep computations that are nevertheless limits of sequences of computations, while for others some deep computations are not even limits of sequences.
We use topology of function spaces to classify sets of computations by the complexity of their deep computations, and thereby obtain a new hierarchy of complexity classes.
The main topological tool is Todorčević’s trichotomy for Rosenthal compacta~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}.
Our inspiration is the work of Glasner and Megrelishvili on tame semigroups in topological dynamics~\cite{glasner2022tame}.

\subsection{NIP, Rosenthal compacta, and deep computations}

Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, the cornerstone of the section, extends results of the first paper of this series~\cite{alva2024approximability} from continuous deep computations to deep computations of the first Baire class.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:nip-baire1def}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a compositional computation structure (Definition~\ref{def:CCS}) satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, with $\mathcal{P}$ countable.
  Let $R$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers,
  and suppose that $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ is $R$-confined.
  Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_\bullet\in R$;
  \item
    $\pi_P\circ \Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ satisfies the NIP for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $r_\bullet\in R$;
    that is, for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$, $r_\bullet\in R$, $a<b$, $\{\gamma_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Delta$ there are finite disjoint $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that
    \[
      L[r_\bullet]\cap\bigcap_{n\in E}(\pi_P\circ\gamma_n)^{-1}(-\infty,a]\cap\bigcap_{n\in F}(\pi_P\circ\gamma_n)^{-1}[b,\infty)=\emptyset.
    \]
  \end{enumerate}

  Furthermore, if any (hence all) of the preceding conditions hold, then every deep computation $f\in\overline{\Delta}$ can be extended to a Baire-1 function on shards:
  there is $\tilde f:\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\rightarrow \mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ such that $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}\in \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_{\bullet}\in R$.
  In particular, on each shard, every deep computation is the pointwise limit of a sequence of computations.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\mathcal{P}$ is countable, $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ is Polish.
  The Extendibility Axiom implies that $\pi_P\circ \tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is a pointwise bounded set of continuous functions for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
  Hence, Theorem \ref{thm:Generalized-BFT} and Lemma \ref{lem:NIP-and-closure} prove the equivalence of (i) and (ii).
  If (i) holds, the extension $\tilde f$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension} satisfies
  $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}\in\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet],\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$ for all $r_\bullet\in R$.
  The assertion that every deep computation is a pointwise limit of a countable sequence of computations follows from the fact that for a Polish space $X$, every compact subset of $\mathrm{B}_1(X)$ is Fréchet--Urysohn
  (that is, a space where any point in the closure of  a set $A$ is the limit of a sequence of points in  $A$;
  see Theorem 3F in \cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire} or Theorem 4.1 in \cite{debs2013rosenthal}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Todorčević’s trichotomy and levels of NIP}
\label{sec:trichotomy}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, under its hypotheses, the deep computations of a countable $\NIP$ set of computations form, on each type shard, a separable Rosenthal compactum.
We measure the complexity of such a compactum by three topological properties, recalled in Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}, which form a chain:
every compact metrizable space is hereditarily separable, and R.~Pol proved that every hereditarily separable Rosenthal compactum is first countable (see~\cite[Section~10]{debs2013rosenthal}).
These properties define the following three levels of NIP.

\begin{defn}\label{def:NIP-levels}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, and let $R$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers.
  Let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be an $R$-confined countable set of computations satisfying the NIP on shards and features (condition (ii) in Theorem \ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
  We say that $\Delta$ is:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\NIP_1$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is first countable for every $r_\bullet\in R$;
  \item
    $\NIP_2$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is hereditarily separable for every $r_\bullet\in R$;
  \item
    $\NIP_3$ if $\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$ is metrizable for every $r_\bullet\in R$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Observe that $\NIP_3 \Rightarrow \NIP_2\Rightarrow \NIP_1\Rightarrow \NIP$.
Moreover, a countable stable set of computations (Definition~\ref{def:stable}) is $\NIP_3$:
on each shard its deep computations form a separable compact subset of a countable product of spaces $\Cp(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$, that is, a separable Eberlein compactum, and separable Eberlein compacta are metrizable~\cite{Arkhangelskii:1992}.
The five compacta of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta} show that the converse implications fail for Rosenthal compacta:
$\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is separable but not first countable;
$\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$ and $\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ are separable and first countable but not hereditarily separable;
and $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ is hereditarily separable but not metrizable.
Todorčević proved that these examples are canonical \cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}.

\begin{thm}[Todorčević’s Trichotomy, \cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire}, Theorem 3 in \cite{argyros2008rosenthal}]\label{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}
  Let $K$ be a separable Rosenthal compactum.
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    If $K$ is hereditarily separable but non-metrizable, then $S(2^\mathbb{N})$ embeds into $K$.
  \item
    If $K$ is first countable but not hereditarily separable, then either $\mathrm{D}(2^\mathbb{N})$ or $\hat{D}(S(2^\mathbb{N}))$ embeds into $K$.
  \item
    If $K$ is not first countable, then $A(2^\mathbb{N})$ embeds into $K$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

We thus have the following classification for separable Rosenthal compacta:

        child {node {\hspace{2cm} $K$ is not hereditarily separable}

  \end{tikzpicture}

The three levels can also be read without topology, as statements about how many inputs are needed to tell deep computations apart.
Fix $r_\bullet\in R$ and write $K_{r_\bullet}=\overline{\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}$.
Identify each $f\in K_{r_\bullet}$ with the real-valued function $(P,x)\mapsto P(f(x))$ on $X_{r_\bullet}=\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$, whose points we call \emph{tests}:
a test applies the feature $P$ to the output on the input $x$.
The topology of pointwise convergence on $K_{r_\bullet}$ is then the topology of pointwise convergence on tests.
A set $D$ of tests \emph{separates} a set of deep computations if any two of them differ on some test in $D$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:levels-tests}
  Let $\Delta$ be as in Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels} and $r_\bullet\in R$.
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $K_{r_\bullet}$ is metrizable if and only if one countable set of tests separates $K_{r_\bullet}$.
  \item
    $K_{r_\bullet}$ is first countable if and only if every $f\in K_{r_\bullet}$ is \emph{countably determined}:
    some countable set $D_f$ of tests separates $f$ from every other member of $K_{r_\bullet}$.
  \item
    $K_{r_\bullet}$ is hereditarily separable if and only if there is no set $E\subseteq K_{r_\bullet}$ of cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$ whose members are \emph{finitely separated}:
    for each $f\in E$ there are finitely many tests and $\varepsilon_f>0$ such that every $g\in E\setminus\{f\}$ differs from $f$ by at least $\varepsilon_f$ on one of them.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  (i) If a countable set $D$ of tests separates $K_{r_\bullet}$, restriction to $D$ is a continuous injection of the compact space $K_{r_\bullet}$ into the metrizable space $\mathbb{R}^D$, hence a homeomorphism onto its image.
  Conversely, if $K_{r_\bullet}$ is metrizable, then $K_{r_\bullet}\times K_{r_\bullet}$ is second countable, so the open set $\{(f,g):f\ne g\}$ is Lindelöf;
  it is covered by the open sets $\{(f,g):f(t)\ne g(t)\}$, for tests $t$, and a countable subcover gives the required set $D$.
  (ii) If $f$ is countably determined, $\{f\}$ is a countable intersection of open sets, and in a compact Hausdorff space such a point has a countable base of neighborhoods.
  Conversely, if $(U_k)$ is a countable base at $f$ and each $U_k$ contains a basic neighborhood defined by a finite set $F_k$ of tests, then $D_f=\bigcup_kF_k$ works.
  (iii) A set is finitely separated exactly when it is a discrete subspace, and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, $K_{r_\bullet}$ is a separable Rosenthal compactum;
  for such compacta, hereditary separability is equivalent to the absence of discrete subspaces of cardinality $2^{\aleph_0}$, by a theorem of Todorčević~\cite{Todorcevic_1999_CompactSubsetsBaire} (see also~\cite[Theorem~0.4]{glasner2022tame}).
\end{proof}

Thus $\Delta$ is $\NIP_3$ when one countable set of tests decides every deep computation, $\NIP_1$ when each deep computation is decided by countably many tests of its own, and $\NIP_2$ when no continuum of deep computations can be told apart one by one with finitely many tests.

In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we exhibit computations at each level of the hierarchy and show that every implication between the classes stable, $\NIP_3$, $\NIP_2$, $\NIP_1$ and $\NIP$ is strict.

The definitions of $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_3$ given here are topological, and the descriptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests} still involve infinitely many tests;
whether the three levels admit a finitary, combinatorial characterization, like that of $\NIP$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}, is Question~\ref{q:NIP-levels}.

\subsection{The Argyros--Dodos--Kanellopoulos heptachotomy and binary search}
\label{sec:heptachotomy}

Todorčević’s trichotomy describes the closure of a countable set of computations;
when the computations are indexed by the binary tree, a finer description is available.
In the separable compacta of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}, the countable dense subsets are indexed by the binary tree $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$.
This indexing suits our computational emphasis:
real numbers are represented by infinite binary sequences, i.e., branches of the binary tree, and approximated by finite bitstrings, i.e., its nodes.
A \emph{dyadic subtree} of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is a subset that is isomorphic to $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ under the order of extension;
we write it as $\{s_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$, indexed through the canonical isomorphism, which preserves extension and the lexicographic order.

\begin{defn}
  Let $X$ be a Polish space.

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    If $I$ is countable and $\{f_i:i\in I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$, $\{g_i:i\in I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^X$ are two families indexed by $I$, we say that they are \emph{equivalent} if the map $f_i\mapsto g_i$ extends to a homeomorphism from $\overline{\{f_i:i\in I\}}$ to $\overline{\{g_i:i\in I\}}$.
  \item
    If $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is a pointwise bounded family, we say that it is \emph{minimal} if for every dyadic subtree $\{s_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$, $\{f_{s_t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos proved that, up to equivalence, there are exactly seven minimal families, and that every relatively compact family $\{f_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X)$ contains, along a dyadic subtree, a family equivalent to one of them (see the theorem below).
We describe the seven minimal families next, in the notation of Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos.
Fix a regular dyadic subtree $T=\{s_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ (i.e., a dyadic subtree such that every level of $T$ is contained in a level of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$)
with the property that for all distinct $s,s'\in T$, we have $s^\frown 0^{\infty}\neq s'^\frown 0^\infty$ and $s^\frown 1^{\infty}\neq s'^\frown 1^\infty$.
We use the prefix tests $v_t$ and the threshold tests $\chi_{[a}$, $\chi_{(a}$ of Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}.
A pair $(f,g)$ of functions on $2^{\mathbb{N}}$ is regarded as a single function on the disjoint union $2^{\mathbb{N}}\sqcup2^{\mathbb{N}}$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  $D_1=\{\frac{1}{|t|+1}v_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  Its closure is $D_1\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}$, the one-point compactification of a countable discrete set.
\item
  $D_2=\{s_t^\frown 0^{\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_2}=2^{\leq\mathbb{N}}$.
\item
  $D_3=\{\chi_{[s_t^\frown 0^\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_3}=S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_4=\{\chi_{(s_t^\frown 1^\infty}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_4}=S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_5=\{v_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_5}=\hat{A}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_6=\{(v_{s_t},s_t^\frown 0^\infty):t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_6}=\hat{D}(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\item
  $D_7=\{(v_{s_t},\chi_{[s_t^\frown 0^\infty}):t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$.
  This is discrete in $\overline{D_7}=\hat{D}(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{thm}[Heptachotomy of minimal families, Theorem 2 in \cite{argyros2008rosenthal}]
  Let $X$ be Polish.
  For every relatively compact $\{f_{t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq \mathrm{B}_1(X)$, there exists $i=1,2,\dots, 7$ and a regular dyadic subtree $\{s_t:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ of $2^{<\mathbb{N}}$
  such that $\{f_{s_t}:t\in 2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ is equivalent to $D_i$.
  Moreover, the families $D_i$ are minimal and mutually non-equivalent.
\end{thm}

For deep computations, the seven families have a direct reading:
their building blocks of the families $D_1,\dots,D_7$ are primitives of binary search on $2^{\mathbb{N}}$, the space of binary expansions.
A node $t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}$ is a dyadic interval $[t^\frown0^\infty,t^\frown1^\infty]$, the set of inputs that round to $t$ at precision $|t|$.
The prefix test $v_t$ asks whether the input rounds to $t$;
the point $t^\frown0^\infty$ is the rounded value itself;
the comparisons $\chi_{[t^\frown0^\infty}$ and $\chi_{(t^\frown1^\infty}$ ask whether the input lies to the right of the left endpoint, or strictly to the right of the right endpoint, of the interval.
Table~\ref{tab:hepta-search} reads the seven families accordingly.

Let $\{\gamma_t:t\in2^{<\mathbb{N}}\}$ be a family of computations of a CCS with countably many features, confined by an exhaustive collection $R$ of sizers and satisfying the NIP on shards and features (condition~(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
For every feature $P$ and every $r_\bullet\in R$, Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} shows that the functions $P\circ\tilde\gamma_t|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ form a relatively compact subset of $\mathrm{B}_1(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$;
by the theorem of Argyros, Dodos and Kanellopoulos, this family contains, along a dyadic subtree, a family equivalent to one of the seven.

\section{Examples of  Compositional Computation Structures}
\label{sec:examples}
We now turn from the general theory to examples, and compute the deep computations of three compositional computation structures explicitly:
Newton’s method on the Riemann sphere for two cubic polynomials (Sections~\ref{sec:Newton-cubic} and~\ref{sec:Newton-cyclotomic}),
and Newton’s method for square roots on the real projective line (Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}).
In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we then place these and other computations at the levels of the hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:Classification}.

We begin by formalizing the Newton--Raphson root-finding method as a CCS~\cite{Galantai200025}, \cite[Section~2.3]{Burden2016numerical}.
Let $\hatC = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$ be the extended complex plane (the Alexandroff —one-point— compactification of~$\mathbb{C}$).
We identify $\hatC$ with the unit sphere $S^2\subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ (the Riemann sphere) through the inverse stereographic projection $z \mapsto (P_1(z), P_2(z), P_3(z))$, a homeomorphism $\hatC\to S^2$, where $(P_1(\infty), P_2(\infty), P_3(\infty)) = (0,0,1)$ and, for $z\ne\infty$,
\begin{align*}
P_1(z) & = \frac{2\,\Re{z}}{|z|^2+1}, &
P_2(z) & = \frac{2\,\Im{z}}{|z|^2+1}, &
P_3(z) & = \frac{|z|^2-1}{|z|^2+1}.
\end{align*}
The extended complex plane $\hatC$ is thus endowed with the real predicates $P_1, P_2, P_3$ with values in~$[-1,1]$;
its topology is metrized by the pullback of the usual Euclidean metric on~$S^2$.

\subsubsection*{Newton  CCSs:  steps and fractals}
\label{sec:Newton-map}
Let $p = p(z)$ be a non-constant rational function with complex coefficients —i.e., an analytic (meromorphic) map $\hatC\to\hatC$.
The \emph{Newton step} (or \emph{Newton map}) for $p$ is the rational map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Np}
N_p: z \mapsto z-\frac{p(z)}{p'(z)}.
\end{equation}
Successive iterations of the Newton--Raphson method (i.e., compositional powers~$N_p^{(n)}$) may be regarded as computations of a CCS whose states space is $L = \hatC$.
The natural choice of predicates is $\mathcal{P} = (P_1, P_2, P_3)$, the inverse stereographic projection coordinates.
(The real- and imaginary part maps $z\mapsto\Re{z}$, $z\mapsto\Im{z}$ are not defined at~$z = \infty$.)

Let $\Gamma_p:=\{N_p^{(n)}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ be the semigroup of iterates of the Newton map~\eqref{eq:Np}.
The CCS $(\hatC, (P_1,P_2,P_3), \Gamma_p)$ is \emph{Newton’s method for~$p(z)$} (or the \emph{Newton CCS for~$p(z)$}).
Note that the states space $\hatC$ is compact and the Newton map $N_p$ is continuous thereon, so the Extendibility Axiom is satisfied by Newton CCSs.

Given an initial estimate $z_0$, one hopes for the sequence of Newton steps applied to~$z_0$
(namely, $N_p^{(\bullet)}(z_0) \coloneqq (z_n)_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$, where $z_n \coloneqq N_p^{(n)}(z_0)$) to converge to a root~$r$ of~$p$.

\paragraph{Fatou and Julia sets}
Recall that the \emph{Fatou set $\mathcal{F}_p$} of a rational map~$p$ consists of all points that have a neighborhood on which every sequence of iterates of~$p$ has a subsequence that converges uniformly with respect to the spherical metric;
the \emph{Julia set} $\mathcal{J}_p\coloneqq\hatC\setminus \mathcal{F}_p$ is its complement:
it is (necessarily closed and) typically a fractal.
The Julia set of the Newton map~$N_p$ is called the \emph{Newton fractal} of~$p$.

\subsection{Example: The Newton CCS for the cubic ${\rho}(z) = z^3-2z+2$}
\label{sec:Newton-cubic}
The polynomial ${\rho}(z) = z^3-2z+2$ has Newton map
\begin{equation*}
N_{\rho}: z \mapsto z - \frac{z^3-2z+2}{3z^2-2} = \frac{2z^3-2}{3z^2-2}.
\end{equation*}
Denote the real root of $\rho$ by $r_0$, and the roots in the upper and the lower half plane by $r_{+}$ and $r_{-}$.
Starting from $z_0=0$, the Newton steps alternate $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$, and neither $0$ nor $1$ is a root of~${\rho}$.

Let $B_0, B_+, B_-$ be the basins of the roots $r_0, r_+, r_-$, and let $B_{01}$ be the basin of the attracting cycle $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$.
The Fatou set of $N_\rho$ is $B_{01} \cup B_0 \cup B_{+} \cup B_{-}$, and each of these four sets is nonempty and open.
Since $B_{01}$ is nonempty, the iterates $N_\rho^{(n)}$ do not converge pointwise;
however, the even and the odd iterates converge pointwise on~$B_{01}$, to two distinct deep computations $f_0$ (constant $0$ on~$B_{01}$) and $f_1$ (constant $1$ on~$B_{01}$).%
\footnote{The point $\infty$ is a repelling fixed point of~$N_\rho$: if $|z|$ is large, then $N_{\rho}(z) = \frac{2}{3}z + O(1)$.
  Hence $\infty$ lies on the Newton fractal of~$\rho$, and $f_0(\infty) = \infty = f_1(\infty)$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:newton} shows $f_0$ and $f_1$.
On the Newton fractal of~$\rho$, the behavior of the iterates is chaotic.

\subsection{Example: The Newton CCS for the $3$-cyclotomic polynomial $\kappa(z) = z^{3}-1$}
\label{sec:Newton-cyclotomic}
Figure~\ref{fig:newton2} shows Newton’s method for ${\kappa}(z) = z^3-1$, whose roots are the cube roots of unity $r_0=1$ and $r_{\pm} = \exp(\pm2\pi i/3)$, and whose Newton map is
\begin{equation*}
N_{\kappa}: z\mapsto z-\frac{z^3-1}{3z^2}=\frac{2z^3+1}{3z^2}.
\end{equation*}
To visualize the iterates themselves, rather than their limits, we color each $z_0$ by blending the colors of the three roots with relative weights $|z_n-r_i|^{-3}$, where $z_n\coloneqq N_\kappa^{(n)}(z_0)$.
This coloring is a continuous function of~$z_0$, and at first it is blurred;
as $n$ increases it sharpens into large regions of nearly constant color, separated by narrow regions where the color varies rapidly, that is, where the coloring is nearly discontinuous.
Unlike $N_\rho$, the map $N_\kappa$ has no attracting cycle other than its three fixed points, the roots:
the Fatou set is the union of the three basins, and on each of them the iterates converge to the corresponding root.

    the limit is constant on each basin and discontinuous across the Newton fractal;

The chaos of Newton’s method on its Julia set is visible to the predicates $P_1,P_2,P_3$,
and it can make a Newton CCS on the Riemann sphere fail to be $\NIP$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:Newton-wild}).

\subsection{Example: Square roots}\label{exa:square-roots-compute}
When $p: \hatC\to\hatC$ has real coefficients, it restricts to a map $\RP^1\to\RP^1$ on the real-projective line $\RP^1 = \mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$.
In this case, the Newton CCS for $p$ may be regarded as having states space $\RP^1$.
We shall explain the details in the simplest case of a polynomial $p(x) = x^2-a$ (real $a>0$) to illustrate iterative square-root approximations.

Let $\mathbb{R}\to S^1: x\mapsto (P_1(x),P_2(x))$ be the (inverse) stereographic projection of the real line onto $S^1\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, i.e.,
\begin{align*}
P_1(x) & = \frac{2x}{x^2+1},    &
P_2(x) & = \frac{x^2-1}{x^2+1},
\end{align*}
extended to a homeomorphism of the real projective line $\RP^1 = \mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ onto~$S^1$ by $\infty \mapsto (P_1(\infty), P_2(\infty)) = (0, 1)$;
this gives a compact computation states structure $(\RP^1, (P_1, P_2))$.
The Newton map $N_p$ is
\begin{equation*}
x \mapsto x - \frac{x^2-a}{2x}
=
\begin{cases}
\frac{x^2+a}{2x}, & x\ne0,\infty; \\
\infty,           & x=0, \infty.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
These Newton steps converge to one of the square roots $\pm\sqrt{a}$ starting from any initial point $x_0\ne0,\infty$.
More explicitly, the iterates of $N_p$ converge pointwise to the map $\mathfrak{f}\colon \RP^1\to\RP^1$ given by
\[
  \mathfrak{f}(x)
  =
  \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}N_p^n(x)
  =
  \begin{cases}
  \sqrt{a},  & x\in(0,\infty); \\
  -\sqrt{a}, & x\in(-\infty,0); \\
  \infty,    & x = 0, \infty.
  \end{cases}
\]
Thus, $\mathfrak{f}$ is discontinuous at $x = 0, \infty$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sqrt}).

\section{Computations across the hierarchy}
\label{sec:iterative}

The hierarchy
\[
\text{stable}\Rightarrow\NIP_3\Rightarrow\NIP_2\Rightarrow\NIP_1\Rightarrow\NIP
\]
of Section~\ref{sec:Classification} was defined through the topology of the
space of deep computations.
In this section we show that each of its levels is attained by classical
algorithms, whence every implication is strict, and that some familiar
algorithms lie beyond it.
In each example the computations are compositions of the steps of an
algorithm.
At all levels but one they are the powers of a single step map; at the level
$\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$ they form a group generated by two steps, in the
Euclidean algorithm and in free reduction alike.
The level of each algorithm is then a theorem about the closure of its
computations, and for such computations the hierarchy coincides with a
hierarchy studied in topological dynamics by Glasner and
Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame}.
We begin with this observation.
Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy} at the end of the section summarizes the examples.

\subsection{Iterative algorithms and tame dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamical-CCS}
The algorithms of this section act on a compact space of states, and each
of their steps is continuous.
We isolate this situation.
A \emph{dynamical CCS} is a compositional computation structure
$(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ in which $L$ is a compact metrizable space,
$\mathcal{P}$ is a countable family of continuous predicates that
separates the points of~$L$, and $\Gamma$ is a countable semigroup of
continuous maps $L\to L$ that contains the identity.
It is a \emph{cascade CCS} if $\Gamma=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ is the
semigroup of iterates of a single continuous map $\gamma\colon L\to L$.
Since $\mathcal{P}$ separates points and $L$ is compact, the map
$v\mapsto\tp(v)$ is a homeomorphism of $L$ onto the space of types, so $L$ is
covered by a single shard, every type is realized, and the Extendibility Axiom
holds with $\tilde\gamma=\gamma$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$.
The deep computations of a dynamical CCS form the pointwise closure
\[
E(L,\Gamma)=\overline{\Gamma}\subseteq L^L ,
\]
which is the \emph{enveloping semigroup} of the dynamical system $(L,\Gamma)$
introduced by Ellis.
For a cascade CCS we write $E(L,\gamma)$.
The system $(L,\Gamma)$ is called \emph{tame} if, for every continuous
$f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$, the family $\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ has the
$\NIP$ of Definition~\ref{def:NIP-fns}~\cite{Kohler1995,Glasner2006}.

A map $p\colon L\to L$ is \emph{of Baire class~$1$} if $f\circ p$ is of
Baire class~$1$ for every continuous $f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$.
It suffices that $P\circ p$ be of Baire class~$1$ for every $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
Indeed, the type map embeds $L$ into $\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}}$, and
$\mathcal{P}$ is countable, so every open subset of $L$ is a countable union
of finite intersections of sets $P^{-1}(U)$, with $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and
$U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ open; by Fact~\ref{baire}, the preimage of every open
set under $p$ is then an $F_\sigma$ set, and so is the preimage of every open
set under $f\circ p$, which is therefore of Baire class~$1$, again by Fact~\ref{baire}.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:cascade-tame}
A dynamical CCS is $\NIP$ if and only if the dynamical system $(L,\Gamma)$ is
tame, and if and only if every deep computation is of Baire class~$1$.
In that case it is $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ or $\NIP_3$ if and only if $E(L,\Gamma)$ is,
respectively, first countable, hereditarily separable or metrizable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The single sizer $r_\bullet$ with $L=L[r_\bullet]$ forms an exhaustive
collection that confines~$\Gamma$.
If $(L,\Gamma)$ is tame, the CCS is $\NIP$: take $f=P$ for $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
If the CCS is $\NIP$, then, by Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications}, $P\circ p$ is of Baire
class~$1$ for every $p\in E(L,\Gamma)$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$, so every deep
computation is of Baire class~$1$.
Finally, suppose that every deep computation is of Baire class~$1$, and let
$f\colon L\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous.
The map $p\mapsto f\circ p$ is continuous on $L^L$, so the closure of
$\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ is the compact set
$\{f\circ p:p\in E(L,\Gamma)\}$, which lies in $\mathrm{B}_1(L)$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT2}, the family $\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ has the
$\NIP$, so $(L,\Gamma)$ is tame.
The last statement is Definition~\ref{def:NIP-levels}, since
$\overline{\tilde\Gamma|_{L}}=E(L,\Gamma)$.
\end{proof}

For metrizable systems, Glasner and Megrelishvili call these three classes
\emph{tame$_1$}, \emph{tame$_2$} and \emph{hereditarily non-sensitive}.
They prove the inclusions between these
classes~\cite[Proposition~0.6]{glasner2022tame}, and they show by examples
that the inclusions are proper.

The simplest iterative methods are those whose iterates converge.
Their level is decided by the continuity of the limit.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:convergent}
Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a cascade CCS whose iterates $\gamma^n$
converge pointwise to a map $\phi\colon L\to L$.
Then $E(L,\gamma)=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\phi\}$, and the CCS is
$\NIP_3$.
It is stable if and only if $\phi$ is continuous.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
A convergent sequence, together with its limit, is a compact subset of the
Hausdorff space $L^L$, hence closed; so $E(L,\gamma)$ consists of the iterates
and~$\phi$.
For each $P\in\mathcal{P}$, the map $P\circ\phi$ is the pointwise limit of the
continuous functions $P\circ\gamma^n$, so it is of Baire class~$1$, and, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the CCS is $\NIP$.
The space $E(L,\gamma)$ is a countable compact Hausdorff space, hence
metrizable, so the CCS is $\NIP_3$.
Its members other than $\phi$ are continuous, which gives the statement about
stability.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Stable and metrizable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP3}
At the top of the hierarchy, the deep computations form a metrizable space.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent}, every convergent method belongs to this level, and
what separates stable from merely $\NIP_3$ is whether the limit is continuous.
A stable computation need not converge, however, as the first example shows.

\emph{A stable example: rotation.}
Let $L=\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, with the predicates $\cos2\pi x$ and
$\sin2\pi x$, and let $\gamma(x)=x+\alpha$ with $\alpha$ irrational.
A pointwise limit of rotations is a rotation, and the rotations $n\alpha$ are
dense, so the deep computations are all the rotations
$x\mapsto x+c$, $c\in\mathbb{T}$.
They are continuous, so the rotation CCS is stable, and its space of deep
computations is the circle group, a metrizable compactum.

\emph{$\NIP_3$ but not stable: square roots.}
The iterates of the Newton map of Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}
converge pointwise to $\mathfrak f$, which is discontinuous at $0$ and $\infty$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent}, the square-root CCS is $\NIP_3$ but not
stable.

\emph{$\NIP_3$ but not stable: the power method.}
The power method computes a dominant eigenvector of a matrix by repeated
multiplication and normalization.
Let $A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d)$ with
$\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\cdots>\lambda_d>0$, let $L=\RP^{d-1}$, and let
$\mathcal{P}$ consist of the predicates
$p_{ij}([v])=v_iv_j/\|v\|^2$, the entries of the orthogonal projection onto the
line $[v]$.
The step of the algorithm is $\gamma([v])=[Av]$.
For $[v]\in\RP^{d-1}$, let $i(v)$ be the least index with $v_i\ne0$, and put
$\pi([v])=[e_{i(v)}]$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:power}
The deep computations of the power method are
$E(L,\gamma)=\{\gamma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\pi\}$.
Hence the power method is $\NIP_3$.
Its only nontrivial deep computation $\pi$ is of Baire class~$1$ and
discontinuous, so the power method is not stable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For $i=i(v)$, we have $\lambda_i^{-n}A^nv=v_ie_i+\sum_{j>i}(\lambda_j/\lambda_i)^nv_je_j\to v_ie_i$,
so $\gamma^n\to\pi$ pointwise, and Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent} applies.
The map $\pi$ is discontinuous at every $[v]$ with $i(v)>1$, since $[v+te_1]\to[v]$ as $t\to0$
while $\pi([v+te_1])=[e_1]\ne\pi([v])$.
\end{proof}

The deep computation $\pi$, which sends each input to the eigenline of the
largest eigenvalue whose eigenvector has a nonzero component in the input, is
not computable in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:CCS}, since it is discontinuous
on the space of types~$L$; yet it is the limit of a sequence of
computations, and the closure in which it lives is countable and metrizable.

\emph{Both levels: the logistic map.}
The logistic map $f_r(x)=rx(1-x)$, with $0<r\le4$, maps $[0,1]$ into itself.
Its CCS has states $L=[0,1]$, the single predicate $x\mapsto x$, and the
semigroup of iterates of~$f_r$.
For $0<r\le1$, let $\ell_r$ be the constant map~$0$; for $1<r\le3$, let
$\ell_r(x)=1-1/r$ for $0<x<1$, and $\ell_r(0)=\ell_r(1)=0$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:logistic}
For $0<r\le3$, the iterates of $f_r$ converge pointwise to~$\ell_r$.
Consequently the logistic CCS is stable for $0<r\le1$, and $\NIP_3$ but not
stable for $1<r\le3$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Write $f=f_r$ and $p=1-1/r$, and note that $f(x)/x=r(1-x)$ for $x>0$.
If $r\le1$, then $f(x)<x$ for $0<x\le1$, so every orbit decreases to a fixed
point of~$f$, and $0$ is the only fixed point in $[0,1]$.

Suppose that $1<r\le2$.
Then $0<p\le\frac12$, and $f(x)>x$ for $0<x<p$, while $f(x)<x$ for $p<x\le1$.
For $0<x<1$ we have $f(x)\in(0,\frac r4]\subseteq(0,\frac12]$, and $f$ is
increasing on $[0,\frac12]$ and fixes~$p$.
Hence the orbit of $f(x)$ is increasing and bounded by $p$ if $f(x)<p$, and
decreasing and bounded below by $p$ if $f(x)>p$.
In both cases, and trivially if $f(x)=p$, it converges to a fixed point of $f$
in $(0,\frac12]$, that is, to~$p$.

Suppose that $2<r\le3$, and put $c=\frac r4$, the maximum of $f$, and $b=f(c)$.
We have $p\le c$, since $c-p=(r-2)^2/(4r)$, and therefore $b\le f(p)=p$, since
$f$ is decreasing on $[\frac12,1]$.
We also have $b\ge1/r$, because $b-1/r=(r^3(4-r)-16)/(16r)$ and $r^3(4-r)$
increases from $16$ on $[2,3]$.
The concave function $f$ takes the value $p$ at $1/r$ and at $p$, so $f\ge p$
on $[1/r,p]$.
Hence $f$ maps $J_-=[b,p]$ into $J_+=[p,c]$, and, being decreasing on $J_+$,
it maps $J_+$ onto~$J_-$.
Let $0<x<1$.
The orbit of $f(x)$ lies in $(0,c]$, and it meets $J_-\cup J_+=[b,c]$.
Otherwise all its points lie in $(0,b)$, where $f(y)>y$ because $b<p$; they
would then increase to a fixed point of $f$ in $(0,b]$, but the only positive
fixed point is $p$, and $p>b$ because $f$ is strictly decreasing on
$[\frac12,1]$ and $c>p$.
Since $f$ maps $J_-\cup J_+$ into itself, it remains to show that the orbit of every $y\in J_-\cup J_+$ converges to~$p$.
The points of period~$2$ of $f$ that are not fixed are the roots of
$r^2y^2-r(r+1)y+(r+1)$, whose discriminant $r^2(r+1)(r-3)$ is negative for
$r<3$; for $r=3$ the only root is~$p$.
Hence the function $f(f(y))-y$ vanishes on $[0,1]$ only at $0$ and $p$, and it
is negative on $(p,1]$, since it is negative at~$1$.
For $y\in J_+$ with $y>p$, the point $f(f(y))$ lies in $J_+$, so
$p\le f(f(y))<y$.
The sequence $(f^{2n}(y))$ therefore decreases to a fixed point of $f\circ f$ in
$[p,y]$, that is, to $p$, and $f^{2n+1}(y)\to f(p)=p$.
Every $y\in J_-$ satisfies $f(y)\in J_+$, so its orbit converges to $p$ as well.

Finally, $f(0)=f(1)=0$.
In all cases the iterates converge pointwise to~$\ell_r$.
The map $\ell_r$ is continuous for $r\le1$, and discontinuous at $0$ for
$r>1$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:convergent} gives the remaining statements.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Hereditarily separable but not metrizable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP2}
Below the metrizable level, no countable set of tests separates the deep
computations (Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}).
Rounding produces such spaces of deep computations, because an algorithm that
rounds at every step makes a fresh decision at every step.

\emph{Drawing a line.}
Let $0<\alpha<1$ be irrational.
To draw the line $y=\alpha x+\rho$ on a screen, Bresenham's
algorithm~\cite{Bresenham1965} moves one pixel to the right at each step and
decides whether to move one pixel up.
The $n$-th decision is
$b_n(\rho)=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha+\rho\rfloor-\lfloor n\alpha+\rho\rfloor
=\Brack{\{n\alpha+\rho\}\in[1-\alpha,1)}$, where $\{\cdot\}$ denotes the
fractional part, and the state of the algorithm is the error term $\rho$
modulo~$1$.
The step $\rho\mapsto\rho+\alpha$ is a rotation of the circle
$\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, but the decision bit is not a continuous
function of~$\rho$.
The standard remedy~\cite{MorseHedlund1940,Lothaire2002} is to take as states
the decision sequences themselves.

Let $D=\{-k\alpha:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}$, a countable dense set
containing both endpoints $0$ and $1-\alpha$ of the decision interval.
The \emph{split circle} $\mathbb{T}_D$ is obtained from $\mathbb{T}$ by replacing
each $t\in D$ by two points $t^-<t^+$, with the circular order topology; it is
a compact metrizable space, homeomorphic to the Cantor set.
The rotation by $\alpha$ preserves $D$ and lifts to a homeomorphism $R$ of
$\mathbb{T}_D$ that preserves the labels $\pm$.
The decision interval becomes clopen in $\mathbb{T}_D$, and the coding map
$\mathbb{T}_D\to2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, $y\mapsto(\Brack{R^ny\in[1-\alpha,1)})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$,
is a homeomorphism onto the \emph{Sturmian shift} $X_\alpha$ that conjugates $R$
to the shift~$\sigma$.
The \emph{Bresenham CCS} has states $L=X_\alpha$, the coordinate predicates, and
the semigroup $\Gamma=\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$.

For $c\in\mathbb{T}$, let $R_c^+$ (respectively $R_c^-$) be the map of
$\mathbb{T}_D$ that sends a point over $y_0$ to $(y_0+c)^+$ (respectively
$(y_0+c)^-$) when $y_0+c\in D$, and to $y_0+c$ otherwise.
The \emph{double arrow circle} $\mathbb{D}$ is $\mathbb{T}\times\{-,+\}$ with the
lexicographic circular order and its order topology; it is compact, first
countable, hereditarily separable and not metrizable~\cite[3.10.C]{Engelking:1989}.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Bresenham}
The Bresenham CCS is $\NIP$.
Its deep computations are
\[
E(X_\alpha,\sigma)=\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{R_c^+,R_c^-:c\in\mathbb{T}\},
\]
where each $\sigma^n$ is isolated and $c^\pm\mapsto R_c^\pm$ is a homeomorphism
of $\mathbb{D}$ onto the remaining part.
In particular, the Bresenham CCS is $\NIP_2$ but not $\NIP_3$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
For $\NIP$, note that the predicates $P_j\circ\sigma^n$ are $\{0,1\}$-valued, and
that the $j$-th coordinate of $\sigma^n y$ is $1$ exactly when $R^{n+j}y$ lies in
the clopen arc $[1-\alpha,1)$ of $\mathbb{T}_D$, that is, when $y$ lies in an arc
of $\mathbb{T}_D$.
A family of arcs of a circularly ordered set has Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension
at most~$3$, so it has no infinite independent subfamily.

For the deep computations, let $\gamma$ be the limit of $\sigma^{n_i}$ along an
ultrafilter, and let $c=\lim n_i\alpha$ in $\mathbb{T}$.
If $n_i$ is eventually constant, then $\gamma$ is a power of $\sigma$.
Otherwise, since the points $n\alpha$ are distinct, the ultrafilter decides
whether $n_i\alpha$ approaches $c$ from the right or from the left.
In the first case, for every point $y$ over $y_0$ the points $R^{n_i}y$ over
$y_0+n_i\alpha$ approach $y_0+c$ strictly from the right, and they converge
in $\mathbb{T}_D$ to $(y_0+c)^+$ if $y_0+c\in D$ and to $y_0+c$ otherwise; so
$\gamma=R_c^+$.
The second case gives $R_c^-$.
Conversely, every $c$ is approached from both sides by the points $n\alpha$
with $n\ge N$, for every $N$, so every $R_c^\pm$ is a deep computation, and
$R_c^+\ne R_c^-$, since they differ at the points over $d-c$ with $d\in D$.
The same argument shows that $c_i^{\varepsilon_i}\to c^+$ in $\mathbb{D}$ implies
$R_{c_i}^{\varepsilon_i}\to R_c^+$ pointwise, and symmetrically for $c^-$; so
$c^\pm\mapsto R_c^\pm$ is a continuous bijection from the compact space
$\mathbb{D}$ onto a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism.
Finally, fix $d\in D$ and consider the two points
$(d-n\alpha)^\pm$ of $\mathbb{T}_D$, which $\sigma^n$ sends to $d^-$ and $d^+$.
Every map $R_c^\pm$ sends them to the same point, and so does every pointwise
limit of $\sigma^{m_i}$ with $m_i\to\infty$, by the argument above.
Hence no net of such maps, and no net of maps $\sigma^m$ with $m\ne n$ and $m$ bounded, converges to $\sigma^n$, so $\sigma^n$ is isolated.
Thus $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)$ is the union of a countable set and a copy of
$\mathbb{D}$; it is hereditarily separable and not metrizable.
\end{proof}

The two deep computations $R_c^+$ and $R_c^-$ are the two ways of drawing the
line infinitely far away, where the accumulated error has reached $c$: they
agree except at the pixels where the line passes exactly through a corner,
and there they apply the two opposite rounding conventions.
Figure~\ref{fig:bresenham} shows the two conventions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{bresenham.pdf}
  \caption{Left: the pixels drawn by Bresenham’s algorithm for $\alpha=(\sqrt5-1)/2$, with the decision bits $b_n$.
    Right: where the line passes exactly through a pixel corner, the deep computations $R_c^+$ and $R_c^-$ break the tie in opposite directions; they agree on every other pixel.}
  \label{fig:bresenham}
\end{figure}

The space $E(X_\alpha,\sigma)$ is the closure of a countable set, so it is a
separable Rosenthal compactum; it is hereditarily separable and not
metrizable, so, by Todorčević's trichotomy
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Todorcevic-trichotomy}), it contains a copy of the split
Cantor set $S(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
For the action of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by the shift, Glasner and
Megrelishvili proved that Sturmian systems are tame$_2$ but not hereditarily
non-sensitive~\cite[Corollary~8.3]{glasner2022tame}.


\subsection{First countable but not hereditarily separable}
\label{sec:ex-NIP1}
Running two rounding algorithms side by side moves the computation one level
down.

\emph{Drawing a line in space.}
Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be irrational numbers in $(0,1)$ such that $1,\alpha,\beta$
are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.
To draw a line in $\mathbb{Z}^3$ with direction $(1,\alpha,\beta)$, the
three-dimensional Bresenham algorithm runs the planar algorithm in two
coordinates at once.
Its CCS has states $L=X_\alpha\times X_\beta$, the coordinate predicates of
both factors, and the semigroup generated by $\sigma\times\sigma$.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Bresenham3}
The three-dimensional Bresenham CCS is $\NIP_1$ but not $\NIP_2$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Each predicate is a coordinate of one factor, so the CCS is $\NIP$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}.
The deep computations form a closed subspace of
$E(X_\alpha,\sigma)\times E(X_\beta,\sigma)$, which is first countable as a
product of two first countable spaces; so the CCS is $\NIP_1$.
By Kronecker's theorem, the points $(n\alpha,n\beta)$ with $n\ge N$ are dense in
$\mathbb{T}^2$ for every $N$, so every point $(c,c')$ is approached by them from
inside each of the four open quadrants at $(c,c')$; arguing as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:Bresenham}, every pair $(R_c^\varepsilon,R_{c'}^{\varepsilon'})$
is a deep computation.
Consider the pairs $(R_c^+,R_{-c}^+)$, for $c\in\mathbb{T}$.
In $\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}$, a neighborhood of $(c^+,(-c)^+)$ is
$[c^+,(c+\delta)^-)\times[(-c)^+,(-c+\delta)^-)$, and it contains
$(s^+,(-s)^+)$ only if $c\le s<c+\delta$ and $-c\le -s<-c+\delta$, that is,
only if $s=c$.
These pairs therefore form an uncountable discrete subspace, and the space of
deep computations is not hereditarily separable.
\end{proof}

The uncountable discrete set consists of the deep computations that break
ties upward in both coordinates at points where the two accumulated errors are
opposite.
Each of the two planar algorithms is $\NIP_2$, and drawing in two directions at
once leaves the class: like the Sorgenfrey plane, a product of two
hereditarily separable spaces need not be hereditarily separable.
Compare \cite[Proposition~4.1]{glasner2022tame}, where a product of two
tame$_2$ systems is shown not to be tame$_2$.
By Todorčević's trichotomy, the space of deep computations contains a copy of
$D(2^{\mathbb{N}})$ or of $\hat D(S(2^{\mathbb{N}}))$.


\subsection{NIP but not first countable: the Euclidean algorithm}
\label{sec:ex-NIP}
At the last tame level, some deep computation is not determined by countably
many tests of its own (Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests}).
An exact equality test has this property: a countable set of inputs cannot
tell it apart from the test that always fails.
The Euclidean algorithm produces such tests.

Let $L=\RP^1=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$, with the predicates $P_1,P_2$ of
Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}, and let $\Gamma=\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ be the
group of Möbius maps $x\mapsto(ax+b)/(cx+d)$ with integer coefficients and
$ad-bc=\pm1$.
It is generated by the steps $x\mapsto x+1$ and $x\mapsto1/x$ of the
Euclidean algorithm, and the maps that the continued fraction algorithm
composes are elements of~$\Gamma$.
We call $(\RP^1,(P_1,P_2),\Gamma)$ the \emph{Euclidean CCS}.
It is a dynamical CCS whose semigroup is a group, and its deep computations
form $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.

For an irrational number $\eta=[a_0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$, let $p_k/q_k$ be its
convergents, put $p_{-1}=1$ and $q_{-1}=0$, and let
$\eta_{k+1}=[a_{k+1};a_{k+2},\dots]$ be its complete quotients.
Let $M_k$ be the Möbius map $t\mapsto(p_kt+p_{k-1})/(q_kt+q_{k-1})$, which belongs
to $\Gamma$ because $p_kq_{k-1}-p_{k-1}q_k=(-1)^{k+1}$.
Writing $A_j(t)=a_j+1/t$, we have $M_k=A_0\circ A_1\circ\dots\circ A_k$, so
$M_k^{-1}=A_k^{-1}\circ\dots\circ A_0^{-1}$ with $A_j^{-1}(t)=1/(t-a_j)$.
Thus $M_k^{-1}$ is the map that the first $k+1$ steps of the continued fraction
algorithm for $\eta$ apply, and the classical identity $\eta=M_k(\eta_{k+1})$ says
that it sends $\eta$ to its complete quotient~$\eta_{k+1}$.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:euclid-limit}
Let $\eta$ be irrational, and let $x\in\RP^1$ with $x\ne\eta$.
Then $M_k^{-1}(x)+q_{k-1}/q_k\to0$ as $k\to\infty$, while $M_k^{-1}(\eta)=\eta_{k+1}>1$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Solving $M_k(t)=x$ gives $M_k^{-1}(\infty)=-q_{k-1}/q_k$ and, for real $x$,
\[
M_k^{-1}(x)=\frac{q_{k-1}x-p_{k-1}}{p_k-q_kx}=-\frac{q_{k-1}}{q_k}\cdot\frac{x-p_{k-1}/q_{k-1}}{x-p_k/q_k}
\]
for $k\ge1$ (so that $q_{k-1}\ge1$), where the value is $\infty$ if $x=p_k/q_k$.
The convergents of an irrational number are pairwise distinct, so $x$ equals
$p_k/q_k$ for at most one $k$; for all larger $k$ the formula gives a real number.
Both convergents tend to $\eta\ne x$, so the last fraction tends to $1$, and
$0<q_{k-1}/q_k\le1$ for $k\ge1$; hence $M_k^{-1}(x)+q_{k-1}/q_k\to0$.
The identity $M_k^{-1}(\eta)=\eta_{k+1}$ is the classical one, and
$\eta_{k+1}>a_{k+1}\ge1$ because $\eta_{k+1}$ is irrational.
\end{proof}

Let $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$.
Let $S$ be the set of irrational numbers $\eta=[0;b_1,b_2,\dots]$ whose sequence
of partial quotients is a concatenation $1^{1}d_11^{2}d_21^{3}d_3\cdots$, where
$1^n$ denotes $n$ consecutive ones and each $d_n\in\{1,2\}$.
The block $1^n$ occupies the indices $s_n,\dots,s_n+n-1$ with $s_n=n(n+1)/2$.
Distinct choices of $(d_n)$ give distinct numbers, by the uniqueness of
continued fraction expansions, so $S$ has the cardinality of the continuum.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:euclid}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item For every $\eta\in S$, the map $\delta_\eta$ that sends $\eta$ to $\varphi$
and every other point of $\RP^1$ to $-1/\varphi$ is a deep computation of the
Euclidean CCS.
\item The constant map $c$ with value $-1/\varphi$ is a deep computation, and it
has no countable base of neighborhoods in $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently $E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ is not first countable.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $\eta\in S$, and for each $n$ let $k_n=s_n+\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ be the
middle index of the block $1^n$, and $m_n=\lceil n/2\rceil-1$.
Then $a_{k_n}=a_{k_n-1}=\dots=a_{k_n-m_n}=1$ and $a_{k_n+1}=\dots=a_{k_n+m_n}=1$,
and $m_n\to\infty$.
We have $q_{k-1}/q_k=[0;a_k,a_{k-1},\dots,a_1]$ and
$\eta_{k+1}=[a_{k+1};a_{k+2},\dots]$.
Suppose that two continued fractions, finite or infinite, $[c_0;c_1,\dots]$ and
$[c_0';c_1',\dots]$ satisfy $c_j=c_j'$ for $j<m$, with $c_1=\dots=c_{m-1}=1$.
Then they share the convergents of index at most $m-1$, and both lie between
the last two of them (or equal one of them).
It follows that they differ by at most $1/(q_{m-2}q_{m-1})\le1/(F_{m-1}F_m)$, where $F_j$ are
the Fibonacci numbers, whatever the remaining partial quotients are.
Both $[0;a_{k_n},\dots,a_1]$ versus $[0;1,1,\dots]$ and
$[a_{k_n+1};a_{k_n+2},\dots]$ versus $[1;1,1,\dots]$ satisfy this with $m=m_n$.
Hence $q_{k_n-1}/q_{k_n}\to[0;1,1,\dots]=1/\varphi$ and
$\eta_{k_n+1}\to[1;1,1,\dots]=\varphi$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit}, the maps $M_{k_n}^{-1}\in\Gamma$ converge
pointwise to $\delta_\eta$, at $x=\infty$ as well.

(ii) Let $(\eta_j)$ be a sequence of distinct elements of $S$.
For each $x\in\RP^1$, at most one $j$ has $\eta_j=x$, so
$\delta_{\eta_j}(x)=-1/\varphi$ for all but at most one $j$.
Hence $\delta_{\eta_j}\to c$ pointwise, and $c\in E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$ because this set
is closed.
Now let $(U_i)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ be open neighborhoods of $c$ in
$E(\RP^1,\Gamma)$.
Each $U_i$ contains a basic neighborhood
$\{f:d(f(x),c(x))<\varepsilon_i\text{ for }x\in F_i\}$ with $F_i\subseteq\RP^1$
finite, $\varepsilon_i>0$ and $d$ a metric on~$\RP^1$.
The set $F=\bigcup_iF_i$ is countable, so some $\eta\in S$ lies outside $F$, and
then $\delta_\eta$ agrees with $c$ on~$F$.
Hence $\delta_\eta\in\bigcap_iU_i$, while $\delta_\eta\ne c$.
Thus no countable family of neighborhoods of $c$ has intersection $\{c\}$.
In a Hausdorff space, the intersection of a base of neighborhoods of $c$ is
$\{c\}$, so $c$ has no countable base of neighborhoods.
\end{proof}

  \caption{The Euclidean algorithm, drawn on the projective line (each axis runs from $\infty$ through $0$ back to $\infty$). Left: the maps $M_k^{-1}$ that the continued fraction algorithm applies to a number $\eta$ whose partial quotients contain longer and longer runs of ones; as $k$ grows along the middles of the runs, every point other than $\eta$ is sent near $-1/\varphi$, while $\eta$ is sent near $\varphi$. \begin{cor}\label{cor:euclid}
The Euclidean CCS is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$.
Its space of deep computations contains the copy
$\{\delta_\eta:\eta\in S\}\cup\{c\}$ of the Alexandroff compactification
$A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
Ellis proved that the action of $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ on the projective line is
tame~\cite{Ellis1993}; see~\cite[Example~5.7]{glasner2022tame}.
For every continuous $f\colon\RP^1\to\mathbb{R}$, the family
$\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}$ is contained in the corresponding family for
$\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, so it has the $\NIP$.
Hence the action of $\Gamma$ is tame, and, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the Euclidean CCS is $\NIP$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid}, it is not $\NIP_1$.
Each $\delta_\eta$ is isolated in $\{\delta_\eta:\eta\in S\}\cup\{c\}$, since it
is the only member that sends $\eta$ near~$\varphi$, and every neighborhood of
$c$ contains all but finitely many of the $\delta_\eta$, as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:euclid}.
This is the Alexandroff compactification of a discrete set of cardinality
$2^{\aleph_0}$, which is homeomorphic to $A(2^{\mathbb{N}})$
(Section~\ref{sec:five-compacta}).
\end{proof}

The deep computation $\delta_\eta$ is an exact equality test: it answers one value
if the input is exactly $\eta$ and another value otherwise.
It is the limit of the maps that the continued fraction algorithm applies to
$\eta$, evaluated at all other inputs.
The mechanism is that of convergence groups.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:euclid-limit}, the maps $M_k^{-1}$ draw the images of all
points other than $\eta$ together, near $-q_{k-1}/q_k\in[-1,0)$, while they
send $\eta$ into $(1,\infty)$; this exhibits every irrational $\eta$ as a
conical limit point of~$\Gamma$.
The choice of $S$ makes the two limits the same for uncountably many~$\eta$.
Glasner and Megrelishvili use the same device to prove that an infinite
countable uniform convergence group acting on a compact metric space with
infinite limit set is tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Proposition~6.1 and Theorem~6.5]{glasner2022tame}.
Their theorem does not cover $\Gamma$, which contains parabolic elements and is
not a uniform convergence group on~$\RP^1$.
Ellis's example, the projective action of the whole group
$\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, is also tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Example~5.7]{glasner2022tame}; the Euclidean CCS shows that
the countable group generated by the two steps of the algorithm already
reaches this level.

A single step cannot produce $\delta_\eta$.
For $g\in\Gamma$, the closure of $\{g^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ in $(\RP^1)^{\RP^1}$ is
countable, hence metrizable, and it does not contain $\delta_\eta$.
Indeed, if $g$ has finite order, the set of its powers is finite.
If $g$ is parabolic, with fixed point $r$, then $g^n$ converges pointwise to the
constant map $r$ as $n\to\pm\infty$.
If $g$ is hyperbolic, with attracting and repelling fixed points $a$ and $r$,
then $g^n$ converges to the map that fixes $r$ and sends every other point to
$a$ as $n\to+\infty$, and to the map that fixes $a$ and sends every other point
to $r$ as $n\to-\infty$.
If $g$ reverses orientation and has infinite order, then its trace $t$ is
nonzero, so $g^2$ has trace $t^2+2>2$ and is hyperbolic, with the same fixed
points as $g$; the odd powers $g^{2m+1}=g\circ g^{2m}$ have the same two limits
as the even ones.
In every case the closure consists of the powers of $g$, which are
homeomorphisms, and at most two limit maps, each of which is constant or
fixes the unique point at which it differs from a constant.
The map $\delta_\eta$ is none of these, since it is not injective and
$\delta_\eta(\eta)=\varphi\ne\eta$.

\emph{Free reduction.}
A second example at this level is the free group $F_2$ on two generators $a,b$,
acting on its boundary $\partial F_2$, the space of infinite reduced words in
$a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1}$, which is a Cantor set.
A generator acts on an infinite word by prepending a letter and cancelling it
against the first letter if they are inverse, as a stack does.
With the predicates $w\mapsto\Brack{w_n=x}$, for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and each letter $x$, this is a dynamical CCS, and
Glasner and Megrelishvili proved that the system is tame but not
tame$_1$~\cite[Corollary~6.6 and Example~6.7]{glasner2022tame}.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, this CCS is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$.

\emph{An open question.}
Both examples at this level are actions of groups with two generators.
Whether a single map can reach this level, that is, whether some cascade CCS
is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, is Question~\ref{q:cascade-NIP1}.
For actions of $\mathbb{Z}$, and more generally of abelian groups, the
existence of a tame metrizable system whose enveloping semigroup is not first
countable is Question~6.8 of Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame};
to our knowledge, it is open.


\subsection{Beyond NIP}
\label{sec:beyond-NIP}
Beyond the hierarchy lie the computations with the independence property:
for some feature, the computations realize every finite pattern of high and
low values on suitable inputs.
Chaotic iteration produces such patterns.

\emph{Newton's method on the Riemann sphere.}
On its Julia set the Newton map is chaotic, and the chaos is visible to the
predicates of the CCS.
We exhibit the independence in the simplest case, the square roots of~$1$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Newton-wild}
Let $p(z)=z^2-1$, and let $N(z)=\frac12\bigl(z+\frac1z\bigr)$ be its Newton map on
$\hatC$.
The functions $P_2\circ N^{3k}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, form an independent sequence:
for all finite disjoint sets $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is $z\in\hatC$ with
$P_2(N^{3k}z)\ge\frac12$ for $k\in E$ and $P_2(N^{3k}z)\le-\frac12$ for $k\in F$.
In particular, the Newton CCS of $p$ is not $\NIP$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The Möbius transformation $\phi(z)=(z-1)/(z+1)$ conjugates $N$ to the squaring
map: $\phi(N(z))=\phi(z)^2$.
It maps the imaginary axis, together with $\infty$, onto the unit circle, with
$\phi^{-1}(e^{i\theta})=i\cot(\theta/2)$.
Hence, for $z=i\cot(\theta/2)$, we have $N^n(z)=i\cot(2^{n}\theta/2)$ and
\[
P_2\bigl(N^n(z)\bigr)=\frac{2\cot(2^{n-1}\theta)}{\cot^2(2^{n-1}\theta)+1}=\sin(2^n\theta).
\]
Write $\theta=2\pi\sum_{j\ge1}x_j2^{-j}$ with bits $x_j$.
The fractional part of $2^{3k}\theta/2\pi$ has binary expansion
$0.x_{3k+1}x_{3k+2}x_{3k+3}\cdots$.
If $(x_{3k+1},x_{3k+2},x_{3k+3})=(0,1,0)$, this fractional part lies in
$[\frac14,\frac38]$, so $\sin(2^{3k}\theta)\ge\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}>\frac12$; if the block
is $(1,1,0)$, it lies in $[\frac34,\frac78]$, so $\sin(2^{3k}\theta)<-\frac12$.
Choosing the block $(0,1,0)$ for $k\in E$ and $(1,1,0)$ for $k\in F$ gives the
required~$z$.
\end{proof}

The proof also shows that the doubling map $x\mapsto2x$ of the circle
$\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, with the predicates $\cos2\pi x$ and
$\sin2\pi x$, is not $\NIP$: with $x=\theta/2\pi$, the functions
$\sin(2\pi\,2^{3k}x)$ form an independent sequence.
The doubling map shifts the binary digits of~$x$, and the independence comes
from the freedom to choose those digits.

The same algorithm is tame on the real line.
The Julia set of $N$ is the imaginary axis together with $\infty$, which meets
$\RP^1$ only at~$\infty$, and on $\RP^1$ the iterates converge to the deep
computation of Section~\ref{exa:square-roots-compute}.
Whether Newton's method is tame depends on the space of states, not on the
formula for the step.

\emph{The logistic map at $r=4$.}
The doubling map also governs the logistic map $f_4(x)=4x(1-x)$ of
Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP3}.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:logistic-four}
The functions $f_4^{3k}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, form an independent sequence on
$[0,1]$: for all finite disjoint sets $E,F\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ there is
$x\in[0,1]$ with $f_4^{3k}(x)\ge\frac34$ for $k\in E$ and
$f_4^{3k}(x)\le\frac14$ for $k\in F$.
In particular, the logistic CCS for $r=4$ is not $\NIP$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let $h(\theta)=\sin^2(\pi\theta)$ for $\theta\in\mathbb{T}$.
Then $f_4(h(\theta))=4\sin^2(\pi\theta)\cos^2(\pi\theta)=h(2\theta)$, so
$f_4^{n}(h(\theta))=\sin^2(\pi\,2^n\theta)$.
Write $\theta=\sum_{j\ge1}x_j2^{-j}$ in binary.
The fractional part $u$ of $2^{3k}\theta$ is then $0.x_{3k+1}x_{3k+2}\cdots$
in binary.
If $(x_{3k+1},x_{3k+2},x_{3k+3})=(0,1,1)$, then $u\in[\frac38,\frac12]$ and
$\sin^2(\pi u)\ge\sin^2\frac{3\pi}8>\frac34$; if the block is $(0,0,0)$, then
$u\in[0,\frac18]$ and $\sin^2(\pi u)\le\sin^2\frac{\pi}8<\frac14$.
Choosing the block $(0,1,1)$ for $k\in E$ and $(0,0,0)$ for $k\in F$, and
putting $x=h(\theta)$, gives the required point.
\end{proof}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{doubling_independence.pdf}
  \caption{Independence for the doubling map. The values of the predicate $\sin^2(\pi x)$ at the times $0,3,6,9$ follow any prescribed pattern of high (H, at least $3/4$) and low (L, at most $1/4$): the initial state is chosen with the block $011$ of binary digits for H and $000$ for L.}
  \label{fig:doubling}
\end{figure}

Thus the logistic family, which is stable for $0<r\le1$ and $\NIP_3$ for
$1<r\le3$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:logistic}), leaves the hierarchy at $r=4$.
On the interval, the tame levels between $\NIP_3$ and $\NIP$ are empty.
A continuous map of a compact interval is tame if and only if it is not
chaotic in the sense of Li and Yorke~\cite[Theorems~4.23 and~4.24]{Li2011},
and Glasner and Megrelishvili record, from an unpublished paper, that it is
tame if and only if it is hereditarily
non-sensitive~\cite[Remark~5.4]{GlasnerMegrelishvili2023}.
Thus, for cascade CCSs on $[0,1]$ with the predicate $x\mapsto x$, the levels
$\NIP$, $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_3$ coincide.

\emph{The Thue--Morse sequence.}
Even a sequence produced by a simple substitution rule can be wild.
The Thue--Morse sequence $t_n$ is the parity of the number of ones in the binary
expansion of~$n$; it is the limit of the words obtained from $0$ by the
substitution $0\mapsto01$, $1\mapsto10$.
The two-sided sequences all of whose finite blocks occur in $(t_n)$ form the
Thue--Morse subshift $X_{TM}\subseteq2^{\mathbb{Z}}$, on which the shift $\sigma$ is a
homeomorphism.
Glasner and Megrelishvili note that the action of $\mathbb{Z}$ that it
generates is not tame~\cite[Example~5.3]{GlasnerMegrelishvili2023}.
Hence the CCS with states $X_{TM}$, the coordinate predicates, and the group
$\{\sigma^n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is not $\NIP$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}.
The cascade CCS of $\sigma$ is not $\NIP$ either.
Indeed, the words obtained from $0$ by an even number of applications of the
substitution are palindromes, and every finite block of $(t_n)$ occurs in one of
them, so the reversal $\rho$ of two-sided sequences maps $X_{TM}$ onto itself
and satisfies $\rho\circ\sigma=\sigma^{-1}\circ\rho$.
An independent sequence $f\circ\sigma^{n_k}$, with $f$ continuous and the $n_k$
distinct, has infinitely many indices $n_k$ of the same sign.
If they are positive, these terms already form an independent sequence in the
cascade; if they are negative, the functions
$f\circ\sigma^{n_k}\circ\rho=(f\circ\rho)\circ\sigma^{-n_k}$ form an independent
sequence with positive exponents, because $\rho$ is a bijection of $X_{TM}$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-tame}, the cascade CCS is not $\NIP$.

\section{Randomized versions of NIP and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}
\label{sec:measure-theoretic-NIP}

In Sections~\ref{sec:Classification}--\ref{sec:iterative} we studied deep computations as \emph{topological} limits:
pointwise limits of computations, of the first Baire class on the type shards, and approximated by sequences of computations.
That theory rests on the type shards being Polish, and hence on the assumption that the set $\mathcal{P}$ of predicates is countable (an assumption used in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
In this section we drop that assumption and replace topology by measure:
instead of asking that a deep computation be of the first Baire class, we ask that it be \emph{universally measurable}, that is, measurable with respect to every Radon probability measure on the type shards, so that it can be evaluated almost surely by random sampling.
We call such deep computations \emph{universally Monte Carlo computable} (Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable}).
The role of the Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand dichotomy is taken by its measure-theoretic form (Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F}), and that of $\NIP$ is shared with Talagrand stability.

The main results of the section are Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}, which connects NIP and Monte Carlo computability, and Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}, which connects Talagrand stability and Monte Carlo computability.

\subsection{NIP and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}

By Fact~\ref{baire}, for perfectly normal $X$, a function $f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}$ is of Baire class~1 if and only if $f^{-1}[U]$ is an $F_\sigma$ subset of $X$ for every open $U\subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
This motivates the following definition.

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:universal-measurability}
  Given a Hausdorff space $X$ and a measurable space $(Y,\Sigma)$, we say that $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is \emph{universally measurable} (with respect to $\Sigma$) if $f^{-1}(E)$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$ and every $E\in\Sigma$.
  When $Y=\mathbb{R}$, we will always take $\Sigma=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{defn}

If $X$ is a compact (Hausdorff) space, then Radon measures on $X$ are finite, and hence, through renormalization, we can regard them as probability measures.
We thus have the following remark:

\begin{rem}
  \label{rem:probability}
  If $X$ is compact, then a function $f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is universally measurable if and only if $f^{-1}(U)$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on $X$ and every open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$.
\end{rem}

Intuitively, a function is universally measurable when it is measurable for every reasonable way of measuring sets in its domain.
For universally measurable sets and functions, see~\cite[Chapters 1 and 2]{Pap:2002}.

\begin{notn}
  Following \cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}, the collection of all universally measurable real-valued functions on $X$ will be denoted by $M_r(X)$.
  Given a fixed Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$, the collection of all $\mu$-measurable real-valued functions on $X$ will be denoted by $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$.
\end{notn}

In the context of deep computations, we are interested in transition maps of a state space $L\subseteq \mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ into itself.
In the product space $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$, we have two natural $\sigma$-algebras:
the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, i.e., the $\sigma$-algebra generated by open sets in $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$,
and the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra (i.e., the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the sub-basic open sets in $\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$, that is, the sets $\pi_P^{-1}(U)$ with $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $U\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ open).
In general, the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra is strictly smaller;
however,  when $\mathcal{P}$ is countable, both $\sigma$-algebras coincide.
We will use the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra to define universally measurable maps $f:\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$.
The reason for this choice is the following characterization:

\begin{prop}\label{prop:coordinated_univ_meas}
  Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and $Y=\prod_{i\in I}Y_i$ be any product of measurable spaces $(Y_i,\Sigma_i)$ for $i\in I$.
  Let $\Sigma_Y$ be the cylinder $\sigma$-algebra generated by the measurable spaces $(Y_i,\Sigma_i)$.
  The following are equivalent for  $f:X\rightarrow Y$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is universally measurable (with respect to $\Sigma_Y$).
  \item
    $\pi_i\circ f:X\rightarrow Y_i$ is universally measurable (with respect to $\Sigma_i$) for all $i\in I$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) is clear since the projection maps $\pi_i$ are measurable and the composition of measurable functions is measurable.
  To prove (ii)$\Rightarrow$(i), note that if $C=\prod_{i\in I}C_i$ is a measurable cylinder and $J$ is the finite subset of $I$ such that $C_i\neq Y_i$ for $i\in J$,
  then, $f^{-1}(C)=\bigcap_{i\in J}(\pi_i\circ f)^{-1}(C_i)$ is universally measurable by assumption.
\end{proof}

The preceding proposition says that  we can check measurability of a transition just by checking measurability feature by feature.
This is analogous to the Baire class 1 setting (see Proposition~\ref{prop:Baire-identifications}).

We saw in Section~\ref{sec:prelim} that the NIP corresponds to relative compactness in the space of Baire class~1 functions (see Theorems~\ref{thm:BFT}, \ref{thm:BFT2} and~\ref{thm:Generalized-BFT}), and in Section~\ref{sec:Classification} that, when the set $\mathcal{P}$ of predicates is countable, this correspondence extends to deep computations (Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}).
The following result, which requires no countability of $\mathcal{P}$, gives an analogous characterization of the NIP in terms of universal measurability:

\begin{thm}
  [Bourgain--Fremlin--Talagrand, Theorem 2F in \cite{BFT_1978_PCompactBaire}]
  \label{thm:BFT-2F}
  Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and $A\subseteq \mathrm{C}(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  The following are equivalent:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item
    $\overline{A}\subseteq M_r(X)$.
  \item
    For every compact $K\subseteq X$, $A|_K$ satisfies the NIP.
  \item
    For every Radon measure $\mu$ on $X$, $A$ is relatively countably compact in $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$, i.e., every countable subset of $A$ has a limit point in $\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

This result allows us to formalize the concept of a deep computation being \emph{Monte Carlo computable}, which we define below (Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable}).
To motivate the definition, let us first recall two facts:

\begin{enumerate}
\item
  Littlewood’s second principle states that every Lebesgue measurable function is “nearly continuous”, as captured by Luzin’s theorem:
  if $(X,\Sigma ,\mu )$ is a Radon measure space and $Y$ is a second-countable topological space (e.g., $Y=\mathbb{R}^\mathcal{P}$ with $\mathcal{P}$ countable) equipped with the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, then any given $f:X\to Y$ is measurable if and only if for every $E\in\Sigma$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a closed $F\subseteq E$ such that the restriction $f|_F$ is continuous and $\mu(E\backslash F) < \varepsilon$.
\item
  Computability of deep computations is characterized in terms of continuous extendibility of computations.
  This is at the core of~\cite{alva2024approximability}.
\end{enumerate}

These two facts motivate the following definition:

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:universally-essentially-computable}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS.
  We say that a transition $f:L\rightarrow L$ is \emph{universally Monte Carlo computable} if and only if there exists an extension $\tilde f:\mathcal{L}_{\sh}\rightarrow \mathcal{L}_{\sh}$ of $f$  satisfying the following property:
  for every sizer $r_\bullet$ there is a sizer $s_\bullet$ such that the restriction $\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_{\bullet}]}:\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\rightarrow\mathcal{L}[s_\bullet]$ is universally measurable,
  i.e., $\pi_P\circ \tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_{\bullet}]}:\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]\rightarrow [-s_P,s_P]$ is $\mu$-measurable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$.
\end{defn}

Since each type shard $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$ is compact, Remark~\ref{rem:probability} allows us to restrict attention, in Definition~\ref{def:universally-essentially-computable}, to Radon probability measures instead of general Radon measures.

\begin{thm}
  \label{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom.
  Let $R$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers.
  Let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $R$-confined.
  If $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ satisfies the NIP for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and all $r_{\bullet}\in R$, then every deep computation in $\overline{\Delta}$ is universally Monte Carlo computable.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
  Fix $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and $r_\bullet\in R$.
  By the Extendibility Axiom, $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ is a set of pointwise bounded continuous functions on the compact set $\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$.
  By Lemma~\ref{lem:NIP-and-closure}, $\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ has the NIP, hence so does its restriction to every compact $K\subseteq\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F} gives $\overline{\pi_P\circ\tilde\Delta|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}}\subseteq M_r(\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet])$.
  If $f\in\overline{\Delta}$ and $\tilde f$ is the extension of Lemma~\ref{lem:extension}, then $\pi_P\circ\tilde f|_{\mathcal{L}[r_\bullet]}$ belongs to this closure.
\end{proof}

Whether the converse of Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} holds is Question~\ref{q:Monte-Carlo-converse}.

\subsection{Talagrand stability and Monte Carlo computability of deep computations}

There is another notion closely related to NIP, introduced by Talagrand in \cite{talagrand1984pettis} while studying Pettis integration.
Suppose that $X$ is a compact Hausdorff space and $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$.
Let $\mu$ be a Radon probability measure on $X$.
Given a $\mu$-measurable set $E\subseteq X$, a positive integer $k$ and real numbers $a<b$, let
\[
  D_k(A,E,a,b)=\bigcup_{f\in A}\{x\in E^{2k}:f(x_{2i})\leq a, \hspace{1mm} f(x_{2i+1})\geq b \hspace{1mm}{\text{ for all }i<k} \}.
\]

\begin{defn}
  \label{def:Talagrand-stable}
  We say that $A$ is \emph{Talagrand $\mu$-stable} if and only if for every $\mu$-measurable set $E\subseteq X$ of positive measure and for every $a<b$ there is $k\geq 1$ such that
  \[
    (\mu^{2k})^*(D_k(A,E,a,b))<(\mu(E))^{2k},
  \]
  where $\mu^*$ denotes the outer measure.
\end{defn}

The outer measure is needed in Definition~\ref{def:Talagrand-stable} because $D_k(A,E,a,b)$ need not be measurable.
The set $D_k(A,E,a,b)$ is measurable when $A$ is a countable set of $\mu$-measurable functions, since it is then a countable union of measurable sets.

\begin{rems}
  \label{rem:Talagrand-stability}
  Let $X$,  $\mu$, $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$, and $E\subseteq X$ be as above.
  Then,
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    If $A\subseteq B\subseteq \mathbb{R}^X$, then
    \[
      D_k(A,E,a,b)\subseteq D_k(B,E,a,b).
    \]

  \item
    If  $a<a'<b'<b$,  then
    \[
      D_k(\overline{A},E,a,b)\subseteq D_k(A,E,a',b').
    \]
    [\emph{Proof}:
      If $x\in D_k(\overline{A},E,a,b)$, then there is $f\in\overline{A}$ such that $f(x_{2i})\leq a<a'$ and $f(x_{2i+1})\geq b>b'$ for all $i<k$.
      By definition of pointwise convergence topology, there exists $g\in A$ such that $g(x_{2i})<a'$ and $g(x_{2i+1})>b'$ for all $i<k$.
      Hence, $x\in D_k(A,E,a',b')$.]

  \item
    Any subset of a Talagrand $\mu$-stable set is Talagrand $\mu$-stable.
    (This is immediate from (1).)

  \item
    If $A$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then so is $\overline{A}$.
    (This is immediate from (2).)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{rems}

The main result of this subsection, Corollary~\ref{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}, states that the deep computations of a set of computations that is universally Talagrand stable on every shard and for every feature are universally Monte Carlo computable.
Its proof rests on Lemma~\ref{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}, by which the pointwise closure of a Talagrand $\mu$-stable set consists of $\mu$-measurable functions, and the lemma in turn rests on the following characterization of measurable functions (compare Fact~\ref{baire}):

\begin{fact}[Lemma 413G in \cite{fremlin2003vol4}]\label{fact:meas-fns-charn}
  Suppose that $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is a measure space and let $\mathcal{K}\subseteq\Sigma$ be a collection of measurable sets satisfying the following conditions:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is complete, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(E)=0$ and $F\subseteq E$ we have $F\in\Sigma$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is semi-finite, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(E)=\infty$ there exists $F\subseteq E$ such that $F\in\Sigma$ and $0<\mu(F)<\infty$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is saturated, i.e., $E\in\Sigma$ if and only if $E\cap F\in\Sigma$ for all $F\in\Sigma$ with $\mu(F)<\infty$.
  \item
    $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ is inner regular with respect to $\mathcal{K}$, i.e., for all $E\in\Sigma$
    \[
      \mu(E)=\sup\{\mu(K):K\in\mathcal{K} \text{ and } K\subseteq E\}.
    \]
  \end{itemize}
  (In particular, if $X$ is compact Hausdorff, $\mathcal{K}$ is the collection of compact subsets of $X$, $\mu$ is a Radon probability measure on $X$, and $\Sigma$ is the completion of the Borel $\sigma$-algebra by $\mu$, then all these conditions hold.)
  Then, $f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is measurable if and only if
  for every $K\in\mathcal{K}$ with $0<\mu(K)<\infty$ and $a<b$, either $\mu^*(K_{\le a})<\mu(K)$ or $\mu^*(K_{\ge b})<\mu(K)$,
  where $K_{\le a}=\{x\in K:f(x)\leq a\}$ and $K_{\ge b}=\{x\in K:f(x)\geq b\}$.
\end{fact}

We first show that Talagrand stability passes to the pointwise closure and forces measurability.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}
  If $A$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then $\overline{A}$ is also Talagrand $\mu$-stable and $\overline{A}\subseteq\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  By Remarks~\ref{rem:Talagrand-stability}, $\overline{A}$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, and hence so is $\{f\}$ for every $f\in\overline{A}$.
  It therefore suffices to show that if $\{f\}$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable, then $f$ is $\mu$-measurable.
  If not, Fact~\ref{fact:meas-fns-charn} gives a set $E$ of positive measure and $a<b$ with $\mu^*(E_{\le a})=\mu^*(E_{\ge b})=\mu(E)$, where $E_{\le a}=\{x\in E: f(x)\leq a\}$ and $E_{\ge b}=\{x\in E: f(x)\geq b\}$.
  Then $(E_{\le a}\times E_{\ge b})^k\subseteq D_k(\{f\},E,a,b)$ for every $k\ge1$, and this product has outer measure $(\mu(E))^{2k}$ (see~\cite{talagrand1984pettis}), so $\{f\}$ is not Talagrand $\mu$-stable.
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
  We say that $A$ is \emph{universally Talagrand stable} if $A$ is Talagrand $\mu$-stable for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on $X$.
\end{defn}

We now observe that universal Talagrand stability implies the NIP:

\begin{prop}\label{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space and $A\subseteq \mathrm{C}(X)$ be pointwise bounded.
  If $A$ is universally Talagrand stable, then $A$ satisfies the NIP.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  By Lemma~\ref{lem:talagrand-stab-relcpct}, $\overline{A}\subseteq\mathscr{M}^0(X,\mu)$ for every Radon probability measure $\mu$ on~$X$, that is, $\overline{A}\subseteq M_r(X)$.
  By Theorem~\ref{thm:BFT-2F} (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) with $K=X$, $A$ satisfies the NIP.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}
  \label{cor:Talagrand-stab-Monte-Carlo}
  Let $(L,\mathcal{P},\Gamma)$ be a CCS satisfying the Extendibility Axiom, let $R$ be an exhaustive collection of sizers, and let $\Delta\subseteq\Gamma$ be $R$-confined.
  If $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ is universally Talagrand stable for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and all $r_{\bullet}\in R$, then every deep computation in $\overline{\Delta}$ is universally Monte Carlo computable.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
  This follows directly from Proposition \ref{prop:Talagrand-implies-NIP} and Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}.
\end{proof}

In the context of deep computations, we have identified two routes to Monte Carlo computability, namely the NIP and universal Talagrand stability.
Whether the two notions are equivalent is a natural question.
For countable sets of measurable functions, the following two results show that whether the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability depends on the axioms of set theory.
On the one hand, it is consistent with the standard ZFC axioms of set theory that, for such sets, the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability:

\begin{thm}[Talagrand, Theorem 9-3-1(a) in \cite{talagrand1984pettis}]
  \label{thm:NIP-implies-Talagrand-stab}
  Let $X$ be a compact Hausdorff space and $A\subseteq M_r(X)$ be countable and pointwise bounded.
  Assume that $[0,1]$ is not the union of $<\mathfrak{c}$ closed measure zero sets.
  If $A$ satisfies the NIP, then $A$ is universally Talagrand stable.
\end{thm}

(The assumption that $[0,1]$ is not the union of $<\mathfrak{c}$ closed measure zero sets is a consequence of, for example, the Continuum Hypothesis.)

On the other hand, the opposite is also consistent for the particular case of the  Lebesgue measure:

\begin{thm}[Fremlin, Shelah, \cite{fremlin1993pointwise}]
  It is consistent with the usual axioms of set theory that there exists a countable pointwise bounded set of Lebesgue measurable functions with the NIP which is not Talagrand stable with respect to Lebesgue measure.
\end{thm}

Both results concern countable sets of measurable functions.
By the Extendibility Axiom, computations are continuous on the type shards, and continuous functions are measurable;
hence, by Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-implies-Talagrand-stab}, it is consistent that the NIP implies universal Talagrand stability for countable sets of computations.
The example of Fremlin and Shelah, however, is a set of measurable functions, and it does not settle whether some countable set of computations can consistently have the NIP without being universally Talagrand stable;
we leave this open (Question~\ref{q:NIP-vs-Talagrand}).

\section{Open questions}
\label{sec:open-questions}

We collect the questions left open by this paper.
The first concerns the hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:trichotomy}, whose levels were defined through the topology of the space of deep computations.

Proposition~\ref{prop:levels-tests} describes the three levels by the number of tests needed to tell deep computations apart, but these descriptions still involve infinitely many tests.

\begin{question}\label{q:NIP-levels}
  Is there a finitary, combinatorial characterization of $\NIP_1$, $\NIP_2$ and $\NIP_3$, in the spirit of the characterization of $\NIP$ by finite patterns in Theorem~\ref{thm:nip-baire1def}?
\end{question}

In Section~\ref{sec:iterative} we place a single iterated map at every level of the hierarchy except one:
at the level $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$, both of our examples are actions of groups with two generators (Section~\ref{sec:ex-NIP}).

\begin{question}\label{q:cascade-NIP1}
  Is there a cascade CCS that is $\NIP$ but not $\NIP_1$?
  Equivalently, is there a continuous map $\gamma$ of a compact metrizable space $L$ such that $(L,\gamma)$ is tame and $E(L,\gamma)$ is not first countable?
\end{question}

For actions of $\mathbb{Z}$, and more generally of abelian groups, the analogous question is Question~6.8 of Glasner and Megrelishvili~\cite{glasner2022tame}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo} shows that $\NIP$ suffices for universal Monte Carlo computability;
whether it is also necessary is the next question.

\begin{question}\label{q:Monte-Carlo-converse}
  Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:NIP-Monte-Carlo}, suppose that every deep computation of $\Delta$ is universally Monte Carlo computable.
  Must $\pi_P\circ\Delta|_{L[r_\bullet]}$ have the NIP for all $P\in\mathcal{P}$ and all $r_{\bullet}\in R$?
\end{question}

Finally, in Section~\ref{sec:measure-theoretic-NIP} we obtained two routes to Monte Carlo computability, $\NIP$ and universal Talagrand stability, and the results of Talagrand and of Fremlin and Shelah leave open whether they can differ for computations.

\begin{question}\label{q:NIP-vs-Talagrand}
  Is it consistent with the usual axioms of set theory that some countable, pointwise bounded set of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space satisfies the $\NIP$ but is not universally Talagrand stable?
  Equivalently, can the two routes to Monte Carlo computability of deep computations differ for countable sets of computations?
\end{question}
